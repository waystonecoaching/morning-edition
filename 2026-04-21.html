State of the Science: MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

The field's flagship trauma journal weighs in on where MDMA-assisted therapy actually stands after the 2024 FDA rejection and the rebuild that followed. This is the kind of read that should anchor any practitioner conversation about standard of care for first responder PTSD, including on the BTTY side. Prefer this over advocacy press when clients ask you what the research says.

A state-of-the-science review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress synthesizes the evidence for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in PTSD through early 2026. It covers the published Phase 3 data, the FDA's 2024 CRL and the methodological concerns that drove it, the blinding problem, therapist training variability, and the field's response. The authors outline what credible next-generation trials will need to clear.

Try this: Skim the methods section before your next integration-focused client conversation. If a client cites a headline number, you want to already know what the control problem was.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Texas panel to study firefighter mental health, suicide prevention under new law

Texas stands up a state-level panel to study firefighter suicide and mental health under new legislation. The policy shape matters, because what Texas codifies other large fire states tend to look at next. Arizona is not Texas, but every state-level body dealing with this population is working from a small shared evidence base.

Texas passed legislation creating a panel tasked with studying firefighter mental health and suicide prevention. The body is expected to produce findings and policy recommendations the legislature can act on. The move follows a decade of rising documented suicide rates in the American fire service and continued gaps in behavioral health access for firefighter-paramedics.

Try this: Worth watching as a reference point if Arizona ever moves on a similar panel. Save the final report when it lands.

No. 05 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2025-11-11

Facing trauma under the influence of psychedelics: A phenomenological study with Nova rave survivors

A rare and sober piece of research. Most participants at the Nova rave attack on October 7th, 2023 were under the influence of classical psychedelics when a mass casualty event happened to them. The researchers treated this not as a grim curiosity but as a trauma substrate question. For anyone holding integration work, the findings cut against the easy narrative that psychedelics inoculate against trauma.

A mixed-methods phenomenological study in the Journal of Psychopharmacology examined Nova rave survivors who experienced the October 7th, 2023 attack while under the influence of psychedelics. The authors describe how set, setting, and substance interacted with acute traumatic exposure, and what survivors reported about the shape of the experience and its aftermath. The piece is careful, non-sensational, and useful for practitioners who hold integration work.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology (SAGE)

Trump orders $50M research into psychedelic drugs, including ibogaine, for PTSD and TBI

A federal executive order earmarks $50M for psychedelic research including ibogaine, with PTSD and TBI named as priority indications. For a coach working with first responder PTSD, the standard of care is about to be rewritten upstream, even if the clinical evidence catches up slowly. The politics and the science are not moving in lockstep, and the gap is where practitioners need to stay careful.

The administration signed an EO directing an estimated $50 million toward research on psychedelic compounds including ibogaine, MDMA, and psilocybin, with stated focus on PTSD and TBI in veteran and first responder populations. The order also signals faster FDA review pathways. Coverage notes that critics are asking whether this funds rigorous trials or accelerates regulatory decisions the data does not yet support.

Try this: Expect client questions this week about whether ibogaine is now accessible. It is not, clinically, in the US. Have a clean answer ready.

Nuance in the polyvagal theory controversy

A sober reading of the polyvagal critique. The piece argues that some of Porges' original evolutionary claims do not survive current mammalian physiology, while the clinical framework Dana built on top may still do real work in the room. For anyone teaching polyvagal language to clients whose nervous systems were shaped by chronic threat exposure, the question is which claims you hang your framework on. Worth tightening the language on your site and in your intake conversations.

Psychology Today revisits the ongoing debate over polyvagal theory, distinguishing between Porges' original evolutionary claims about the vagus nerve and the clinical applications developed by practitioners like Deb Dana. The author argues that even where the phylogeny is disputed, the state-based language can still function as a useful clinical map. The case is made for tightening claims rather than discarding the framework.

Try this: Re-read your own Waystone language on nervous system states this week. Decide which sentences rest on the phylogenetic claim and rewrite them to rest on the clinical observation.

The impact of silence pedagogy on vulnerable students' engagement in physical education: a mini-review

Silence as a pedagogical tool, offered by researchers who almost certainly have never read a Desert Father, arriving at a version of the same insight from the far side of the room. The paper argues that deliberately held silence in teaching settings increases engagement among students whose nervous systems have been shaped by scarcity and noise. The contemplative tradition has known this for sixteen centuries. A quiet reminder that what you carry into the work already has good company in the literature.

A mini-review in Frontiers in Psychology examines silence pedagogy as an intervention for vulnerable student populations. The authors argue that intentional silence, held by the teacher rather than imposed on the student, opens space for engagement in ways that noise-dense instruction does not. They review existing studies and outline where more evidence is needed.
