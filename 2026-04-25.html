First responders with PTSD are turning to Arizona's psilocybin trial

This is the closest psilocybin research to home, both in geography and population. Phoenix and the broader Arizona responder community are the recruiting pool. Anything that lifts or stalls this trial will reshape how local fire and EMS leadership talk about treatment for traumatic stress, and how Waystone clients hear about it.

InvestigateTV's national-syndicated piece follows first responders with treatment-resistant PTSD enrolling in an Arizona psilocybin study. Reporters track the protocol, the screening rigor, and the hopes participants bring with them. The framing leans toward access narratives, so read it as a movement-of-the-Overton-window story rather than as outcomes data.

Try this: Hold this for the next responder client conversation about psychedelic-assisted options. The piece itself is not evidence, but it is now a cultural reference point clients will name.

Nuance in the polyvagal theory controversy

The polyvagal critique cycle has been running for two years now, and clients are starting to bring it up. This piece tries to hold the middle: which claims are robust, which are oversold, and where Porges and his critics actually disagree. Read with a pencil. If your framing of the autonomic substrate borrows the strong version of the theory, it is worth tightening the language used with clients.

The author walks through the main scientific objections to polyvagal theory's neuroanatomical claims, separates them from the clinical practices Deb Dana and others have built on top, and argues the practical work survives even when the strong-form theory is bracketed. Good model of how to hold a contested framework without abandoning the felt-sense gains.

Source: Psychology Today

Feasibility RCT of the mobile anger reduction intervention for veterans with PTSD

A randomized feasibility trial tests a smartphone-delivered anger reduction protocol against control with PTSD-diagnosed veterans. Outcomes are feasibility-stage, so read for engagement and dropout rates rather than for effect sizes that would change practice. The signal is that the intervention can be administered at scale outside a clinic.

Anger reads as character before it reads as injury.

Anger is the symptom cluster most likely to bring a first responder to a coaching conversation rather than a clinical one, because anger feels less like illness and more like character. A feasibility trial of a mobile anger-reduction intervention for veterans with PTSD is the kind of study that builds the evidence floor under that work.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Reset between the tones: Micro-practices medics can use on shift

A piece about returning to ground between calls is the right way to close the day's reading. The shift world rewards a contemplative attention without naming it that way. The Fathers said it more plainly: keep your mind in your heart, and bring it back when it wanders. The work between tones is the same work, in a louder room.

EMS1 collects practical between-call resets used by working medics, ranging from physiological sigh and box breathing to short sensory grounding routines. Field-tested rather than theoretical. Reads as a permission slip for the brief stillness most medics already know they need.

Try this: Pick one micro-practice and use it after every patient contact for the next CAP shift. Note which hold up under the actual cadence of the shift and which collapse on contact.
