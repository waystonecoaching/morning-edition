First responders with PTSD are turning to Arizona's psilocybin trial

This is your backyard. The Arizona-funded psilocybin trial sits squarely on the seam where your work lives, first responder PTSD, integration, the question of who holds the container after the medicine. Watch how the reporting handles preparation and integration vs. the dosing event itself. That gap is where the field tends to stumble, and where your training has the most to say.

InvestigateTV's national feed picked up the Arizona psilocybin trial enrolling firefighters, police, and EMS with PTSD. The piece centers on participant accounts of relief from intrusive symptoms, with researchers framing this as one of the larger state-supported responder studies to date. Coverage is sympathetic but light on the integration architecture around the dosing days. Worth reading for the cohort framing, with the usual caveats about media that leads with breakthroughs.

Try this: Read the actual trial protocol on ClinicalTrials.gov before the next BTTY L2P session so you can speak to it from primary sources, not the news package.

State of the Science: MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

Peer-reviewed review article in JTS, published in the wake of the FDA's 2024 rejection. Read this before you read any more advocacy press. It will give you a defensible position on what the trials actually showed, what the methodological criticisms are, and what the path forward looks like with the field under credibility stress.

A formal State of the Science review covering the full MDMA-assisted psychotherapy literature for PTSD, including the Phase 3 trials and the methodological concerns raised during the FDA review. The authors weigh efficacy signals against blinding, expectancy, and adverse event reporting issues. The piece is a good baseline for any practitioner working near this space.

Try this: Pull the PDF and write yourself a one-page summary of the methodological critiques so you can answer client questions directly without leaning on press cycles.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

Adjacent population, same wound. Journalists covering atrocity, like first responders, work inside repeated exposure to suffering they cannot fix. The scoping review charts how moral injury shows up there, and the language travels back to the fire service surprisingly cleanly.

Early-view scoping review in JTS mapping the moral injury literature among journalists. The authors identify themes around witnessing without intervention, organizational betrayal, and the cumulative weight of atrocity reporting. They flag the same definitional and measurement issues that haunt the broader moral injury field. Useful as a sideways read on first responder moral injury since the populations rhyme.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

What London's firefighter mental health numbers are teaching the fire service

Cross-border data point. The London Fire Brigade has been publishing internal mental health numbers more transparently than most US departments, and the trend lines are uncomfortable reading. Pay attention to how they translate the data into program design, and what categories they are tracking that US departments mostly aren't.

FireRescue1 unpacks recent London Fire Brigade mental health data showing rising rates of stress-related leave and PTSD claims, with attention to which subgroups carry the highest load. The piece argues that the value of this kind of reporting is mostly in how it forces program design conversations to be data-driven rather than anecdotal. US departments mostly do not collect, or do not release, comparable numbers.

Source: FireRescue1

Combat veteran turned paramedic launches peer support app

A combat veteran who became a paramedic has launched a peer support app aimed at first responders, framed as a low-friction entry point for peers in distress. Coverage emphasizes the founder's lived experience and the app's connection-on-demand model. The piece does not deeply examine clinical safeguards, escalation pathways, or what happens when a peer-to-peer conversation surfaces acute risk.

An app is not an intervention. It is a doorway, at best.

Peer support is moving into the app layer. The question is whether this is signal or noise. Some of these tools genuinely lower the friction to an initial conversation. Some are vendor PR with a logo on a contact form. Read with both eyes open.

Nuance in the polyvagal theory controversy

The Porges critique is no longer a fringe paper, and dismissing it makes practitioners look defensive. This piece tries to hold the middle ground: the clinical framework can stay even if some of the evolutionary claims are wobblier than first taught. Read it for the language to describe your own framework to clients without overclaiming the neuroscience.

Psychology Today essay walking through the recent academic critiques of polyvagal theory's evolutionary and anatomical claims, and arguing that the clinical utility of the autonomic-states framework can survive the science being more contested than the original presentation allowed. The author distinguishes the heuristic from the mechanism, and suggests practitioners speak in terms of nervous system states without leaning on contested anatomical specifics.

Try this: Audit your Waystone copy and intake language for any place you state polyvagal claims as settled biology rather than clinical heuristic.
Source: Psychology Today

How an Ohio fire department and University Hospitals created a sleep science success story

Sleep is the substrate beneath everything else, and the fire service mostly pretends otherwise. This is a small, concrete program from a small department, dorm-style sleep spaces designed around shift work disorder. Not glamorous, not trauma-coded, just the slow good work of treating rest as part of the job. A fitting close.

Firefighter Nation profiles a partnership between Seven Hills Fire Department and University Hospitals that built five dorm-style sleep spaces designed around firefighter shift work sleep disorder. The article walks through the design choices, the metrics they are tracking, and the cultural shift required to use the spaces without stigma. Modest in scope and unsentimental in tone.

Source: Firefighter Nation
