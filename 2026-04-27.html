Combat veteran turned paramedic launches peer support app

Read this with one eye open. Peer support apps multiply faster than they validate, and the failure mode is well known: a responder reaches out at 0300, the app routes the message to a sleeping volunteer, the moment passes. Worth tracking which builders are designing for that gap, especially given the suicidality risk in the population.

A combat veteran turned paramedic is launching a peer-to-peer mental health app aimed at fellow responders. The pitch is shift-aware, anonymous-by-default contact with vetted peers. The piece does not detail clinical oversight, escalation pathways for acute crisis, or response time guarantees. That gap is the headline.

Try this: Before recommending any peer support app to a client, ask three questions: who answers at 0300, what is the escalation path on a suicide disclosure, what is the deletion policy on user content.

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

Moral injury research outside the military and first responder population is still thin. A scoping review on journalists is useful because the population shares the structural pattern: chronic exposure to suffering, organizational pressure to keep producing, and a culture that treats the wound as the cost of the job. The constructs travel.

A scoping review mapping the moral injury literature in journalism, including witnessing, perpetration-by-omission, and betrayal-based subtypes. The authors note inconsistent measurement across studies and a tendency to collapse moral injury into PTSD without distinguishing the moral and meaning-making dimensions. They argue for population-specific instruments and longitudinal designs.

Try this: If you are working on the Waystone moral injury page, this review is a clean citation for the argument that moral injury is structurally distinct from PTSD.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Nuance in the polyvagal theory controversy

If you describe your work to clients in polyvagal language, this is the piece that should change how you say it. The neuroanatomical claims at the heart of Porges' original model have been challenged for years, and the argument is no longer fringe. Hold the clinical utility, drop the overstated mechanism. The nervous system framework can survive the correction.

Psychology Today walks through the substantive critiques of polyvagal theory: the contested ventral-dorsal vagal distinction in mammals, weak evidence for a hierarchical autonomic ladder as Porges describes it, and the gap between the popular Deb Dana clinical adaptation and the underlying neuroscience. The piece does not dismiss the framework outright. It argues for keeping the practice vocabulary while letting go of the strongest mechanistic claims.

Try this: Audit the Waystone site and any client handouts for hard claims about ventral and dorsal vagal states. Soften to functional language: settled, mobilized, shut down. The work does not need the disputed anatomy.
Source: Psychology Today

Reset between the tones: Micro-practices medics can use on shift

Closing image of the morning. Not a study, not a policy. The thirty seconds between the tone drop and the truck rolling, and what a medic does inside them. The cell of the modern Desert Father is the rig bay. The breath between tones is the practice.

EMS1 walks through small reset practices medics can run inside a single shift: post-call breathwork, a one-minute physiological sigh, brief sensory grounding before a debrief, water and protein after a code rather than coffee. Nothing here is novel. The work is making the practices small enough that a tired medic will actually do them.

Try this: Pick one micro-practice and write it on a sticky note in your CAP locker. Run it after the next code. Do not turn it into a system.
Source: EMS1
