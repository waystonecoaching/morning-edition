First responders with PTSD are turning to Arizona's psilocybin trial

This one is local in every sense that matters. Arizona is running one of the few active state-level psilocybin trials with a first responder cohort, and Phoenix Fire sits inside that orbit. The story broke across local broadcast affiliates nationwide this week, which means it has landed in the awareness of responders and their families who are not actively following the research space. Know what you are being asked about before they ask.

An investigative broadcast report on Arizona's psilocybin clinical trial, which has enrolled first responders with PTSD. The piece covers the regulatory framework enabling the trial under state law, responder participants describing their treatment experience, and the ongoing question of access and continuity of care after the trial window closes. Credibility is mid — this is local broadcast, not primary literature — but it reflects real enrollment and real participants.

Try this: If clients are asking about this trial specifically, know that it operates under Arizona's state framework. The Being True to You integration track is the appropriate referral context post-experience, not during active trial enrollment.

What's the Worst Thing You've Seen?

Civilians ask this question constantly, and most responders have never been given a framework for what it does to them or to the civilian asking. Chris Marella's piece handles this at field level — not clinically, not abstractly. This is the kind of content that names a thing responders experience every day and gives it shape without turning it into a therapy session.

Marella examines the civilian habit of asking responders for graphic details about the worst calls they have run — the morbid curiosity that presents as care — and the harm that follows when responders comply without protective context. He argues the response is not about withholding but about recognizing that trauma deposited on an unprepared listener injures both parties. The piece offers a practical reframe for responders navigating these conversations.

Try this: Useful early-session content for clients who struggle with separating job identity from trauma disclosure. Pair with the distinction between telling a story and ventral-vagal contact.
Source: Firefighter Nation

State of the Science: MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

JTS published a formal state-of-the-science review of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in their April 2026 issue. This matters precisely because the field is under credibility stress after the FDA rejection and the MAPS trial integrity questions. A peer-reviewed synthesis in JTS is the kind of document that shapes clinical consensus — read it not as confirmation but as a map of what the evidence actually supports, where the gaps are, and what remains contested.

A comprehensive review in Journal of Traumatic Stress Vol. 39, Issue 2 (pp. 173-187) synthesizing the current evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in PTSD treatment. The review covers trial methodology, effect sizes, adverse event profiles, and the regulatory landscape following the 2024 FDA non-approval. Scope-of-practice note: this is clinical literature. The value for a coach is in knowing what the current honest consensus is, not in applying the clinical findings directly.

Try this: This is the document to cite when clients or colleagues ask where the science actually stands on MDMA therapy. Read the limitations section before the findings.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

America's Moral Injury Pandemic

Moral injury has broken out of the veteran and first responder space and into mass-market coverage. Time running this as a feature signals that the concept is shifting from specialist vocabulary to public health frame. That has practical consequences: expect clients to arrive with some version of the term already in their vocabulary, applied to their experience with varying accuracy.

Time's feature covers the expansion of moral injury discourse beyond military populations into healthcare, education, and institutional settings — anywhere structural betrayal or forced complicity in harm has accumulated without repair. The piece draws on researchers in the Litz and Shay lineage and frames moral injury as distinct from PTSD: a wound to moral identity and conscience rather than a fear-extinction problem. Treatment implications differ accordingly.

Try this: A useful primer to share with clients who recognize something in the moral injury frame but think it only applies to combat veterans. The distinction between moral injury and PTSD is content worth naming early in an intake.

No. 05 · First Responder MH · 2026-04-23

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

Peer-reviewed scoping review. Journalists are not first responders, but the architecture of their moral injury closely parallels what firefighters and paramedics carry: repeated exposure to suffering, institutional pressure to continue without recovery time, and the wound of witnessing harm they were not permitted to interrupt. Research mapping moral injury in adjacent high-exposure populations strengthens the taxonomy.

A JTS scoping review examining existing literature on moral injury in journalism — a population that experiences mass casualty events, violence, and institutional betrayal at rates comparable to emergency responders, with far less peer support infrastructure. The review maps prevalence, contributing factors, and current gaps in the literature. The absence of peer support structures in most newsrooms is identified as a key risk amplifier.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Ketamine Therapy Offers New Hope for Israeli Combat Veterans With PTSD

Ketamine sits in a different regulatory and pharmacological category than psilocybin or MDMA — it is already legal and in active clinical use, which makes coverage of its application in combat PTSD populations more immediately relevant than trial-stage compounds. Israeli military medicine moves quickly on trauma treatment, and what gets published there often foreshadows stateside clinical adoption.

A report on ketamine therapy programs being offered to Israeli combat veterans with PTSD, covering both the clinical rationale and the institutional context that enabled rapid adoption. Ketamine's mechanism differs from classical psychedelics — it acts on glutamate pathways rather than serotonin — and the dissociative experience requires a different integration frame than psilocybin or MDMA work. The piece represents current practice in a high-volume combat trauma population.

No. 07 · Coaching Practice · 2026-04-27

Coaching is Human-Centered. Can AI Help?

ICF makes the case that empathy and relational presence — the core of the coaching container — cannot be delegated to AI, but that AI tools can support practice management, note synthesis, and session preparation without displacing the coach. The piece offers practical guidance on ethical integration: transparency with clients, data handling, and using AI for administrative load rather than the relational work itself.

AI cannot replace the empathy at the heart of coaching.

The ICF is starting to name the question openly rather than dodge it. A solo practice navigating tool integration needs a principled framework, not a vendor recommendation — and ICF's framing of ethical AI use is closer to the former. Worth reading against your own experience of what you have used, what helped, and what reduced rather than extended human contact.

Try this: Consider whether there are specific administrative or preparation tasks — intake summaries, session structure, research — where tool support would free attention for the work that requires your presence.

'Not unique to war': millions of Americans suffer from moral injury. What's causing it?

The Guardian takes the structural critique frame: moral injury as downstream of systems that routinely place individuals in positions of forced complicity with harm — institutional betrayal as a design feature, not a failure. This is the Shay and Litz lineage applied to civilian life. Read alongside the Time feature and you have both the diagnostic expansion and the structural explanation for how the concept is entering public discourse.

The Guardian piece examines moral injury's causes in civilian populations — healthcare workers forced to ration care, teachers required to enforce policies that harm students, workers complicit in institutional harm they cannot refuse without losing their livelihood. The piece names betrayal by leadership as a primary driver, distinct from combat exposure. Millions carry this wound with no framework to name it and no repair path offered by their institutions.

Source: The Guardian

Polyvagal Theory: How Our Vagus Nerve Controls Responses to our Environment

Verywell Mind is popular science, not primary research — but how polyvagal theory gets translated for a general audience matters when that audience includes your clients. The quality of public-facing summaries shapes the questions you get and the misconceptions you need to unwind. Worth a read to see what framing people arrive with.

A Verywell Mind explainer covering the core polyvagal framework: the three-state hierarchy of ventral vagal safety, sympathetic mobilization, and dorsal vagal shutdown; neuroception as the body's non-conscious threat detection; and the vagus nerve's role in mediating the shift between states. The piece targets a lay audience and covers co-regulation and basic nervous system literacy without the depth of Porges or Dana's primary work.

Try this: Check whether your own explanation of polyvagal states to clients maps clearly to what people are reading here. If they arrive with this framing, build from it rather than correct over it.

Spectator violence is a form of moral injury

The Christian Century applies the moral injury frame to spectator violence — the harm done to conscience by witnessing violence staged for entertainment or collective aggression. This is a theological and ethical publication, and it reads moral injury through categories of conscience and complicity rather than DSM criteria. The Fathers said the same thing about the arena. The frame has not aged.

The Christian Century argues that watching violence — whether in sport, media, or civic contexts — produces a form of moral injury in witnesses who do not act to stop it. The piece draws on the moral injury literature but applies it through theological and ethical categories: complicity, conscience, the weight of passive witness. It raises questions about what spectacle costs the soul of the one watching, and whether communities bear moral injury collectively.

Source: The Christian Century
