First responders with PTSD are turning to Arizona's psilocybin trial

An investigative report on Arizona first responders enrolled in psilocybin trials for PTSD. This is Mark's state, his population, and the intersection of two things he works closest to: first responder moral injury and psychedelic integration. Worth reading carefully for what it says about access, screening, and what integration support is actually being offered -- or not offered -- at the trial level.

Arizona is running one of the first psilocybin trials specifically recruiting first responders with PTSD. The investigative piece examines who is enrolling, how participants found out about the trial, and what the experience has been like. The reporting is light on integration protocol detail, which is a gap worth noting given how much the outcome depends on that container. The piece is primarily narrative, not clinical.

Try this: If clients are asking about clinical psilocybin access in Arizona, this is the piece to reference. Be clear this is trial context, not a treatment recommendation, and that integration support varies widely across sites.

Active-duty US soldiers to receive MDMA therapy for PTSD next year

The Guardian reports that active-duty US service members will begin receiving MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD within the next year. Read critically. This is a significant policy threshold -- active-duty rather than veteran populations -- but the MDMA evidence base remains under credibility stress following 2024 replication failures and the FDA's initial rejection. The speed of institutional uptake is outrunning the evidence review.

The piece covers an announcement that active-duty soldiers will be enrolled in MDMA-assisted therapy trials for PTSD, framing it as a landmark step. The Guardian acknowledges researcher skepticism about prior trial data integrity but treats the policy momentum as the primary story. The expansion signals bipartisan political will; whether the clinical case has caught up to the political case is a separate question the article does not fully press.

Source: The Guardian

FDA Fast-Tracks Psychedelic Therapies for Depression, PTSD, and Alcohol Use Disorder

Psychiatric Times covers the FDA's announcement of expedited review pathways for three psychedelic compounds targeting depression, PTSD, and alcohol use disorder. For practitioners working adjacent to this space -- coaches supporting clients navigating or considering clinical options -- the regulatory calendar is now accelerating. The political context matters: this follows a Trump executive order on psychedelic research, which adds complexity to what will be read publicly as a purely scientific determination.

The FDA has granted fast-track designation to psychedelic-assisted therapies, shortening formal review timelines for qualifying compounds. Psychiatric Times provides a measured read of what this means clinically versus politically. The designation does not guarantee approval; it signals priority review status. The piece is useful for its regulatory precision, though it does not engage the evidence debate in depth.

Try this: Worth tracking how the accelerated timeline affects the BTTY integration landscape and what coaches may field from clients exploring clinical pathways in the next 12 to 18 months.

'Hydraulic debriefing:' Alcohol, stress, and the hidden culture of EMS

EMS1 names something that runs through first responder culture without ever being formally acknowledged: the end-of-shift drink as informal debrief, emotional processing, and peer bonding all at once. Mark navigates this cultural pattern with Phoenix Fire clients. Understanding its function -- not only its risk -- is part of working honestly in this space. Scope-of-practice edge: if clients are using alcohol as primary coping, this is a referral territory, not a coaching lane.

The piece examines how alcohol became embedded in EMS culture as a coping mechanism and informal social ritual after difficult shifts. It traces the function alcohol serves, not just the harm, and asks what would have to be true for peer support structures to fill the same relational role. The framing is honest about why the culture resists change: the debrief is also the belonging, and separating them is not straightforward.

Source: EMS1

State of the Science: MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

The Journal of Traumatic Stress publishes a peer-reviewed state-of-the-science on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, situated against the backdrop of the 2024 FDA rejection and ongoing replication concerns. This is the kind of primary literature that should anchor any practitioner working adjacent to this field. Scope-of-practice edge: this is clinical literature; use it to calibrate your framing with clients, not to guide treatment decisions.

Published in April 2026, this review synthesizes current evidence for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, situated against MAPS Phase 3 trial controversies and the evidence disputes that followed. The authors assess where the evidence is solid, where it remains contested, and what clinical and methodological questions are still open. The paper is a useful corrective to both wholesale dismissal and uncritical advocacy -- which describes most of what circulates in this space.

No. 07 · First Responder MH · 2026-04-28

What's the Worst Thing You've Seen?

Chris Marella examines the 'What's the worst thing you've seen?' question as a recurring social event, arguing that responders need a rehearsed, boundaried answer that protects both parties. The piece touches on secondary traumatization, the impulse to discharge under social pressure, and the value of having language ready before the situation arises. Useful framing for coaching clients who haven't yet thought through their own disclosure patterns.

The worst thing a firefighter can do is dump trauma on an unsuspecting victim.

FirefighterNation takes on the social situation every firefighter knows: the civilian who asks the gore question at a dinner party or family gathering. The question beneath the question is about what responders do with their own story, and who has standing to receive it. Practically useful for session work around self-disclosure, trauma containment, and the difference between honoring an experience and unloading it.

Try this: Worth bringing into session with clients who report feeling caught off guard by the question, or who have discharged trauma in social settings and felt worse afterward.
Source: Firefighter Nation

Repairing the roots of moral injury

WHYY's coverage of moral injury focuses on repair rather than recognition. The language of 'roots' signals attention to the structural and relational origins of moral injury, not only its symptoms. Worth reading for how researchers and clinicians outside first responder literature frame the same wound -- and whether their repair frameworks translate into the occupational context.

The piece examines emerging frameworks for repairing moral injury, drawing on voices from medicine, military, and pastoral care. Repair, as distinct from symptom management, requires engaging the ethical violation and the relationship in which it occurred -- not just processing the affect. The piece is current and treats the topic with appropriate seriousness without flattening it into a wellness narrative.

Polyvagal Theory: How Our Vagus Nerve Controls Responses to our Environment

Verywell Mind publishes a primer on polyvagal theory and the vagus nerve for a general audience. For Mark, the practical value is not the content -- he knows the framework -- but the language: this is how clients arriving from a general wellness search will first encounter Porges' model. Knowing the popular framing helps with where to start, what to correct, and what expectations clients bring in.

The piece explains how the vagus nerve mediates the body's responses to threat and safety, using polyvagal theory to describe the hierarchy of autonomic states: ventral vagal, sympathetic, and dorsal vagal. It covers neuroception, co-regulation, and the body-first nature of safety responses. Accurate at the level of a primer; does not engage the ongoing empirical debate about the theory's scientific base.

Try this: Useful reference point for clients who arrive having encountered polyvagal concepts via wellness social media and carry a somewhat idealized version of the framework.

The Professor and the Firefighter: I Should Be Dead

Timothy Andrew Kussow holds two things at once: firefighter and professor, first responder and artist. The essay is about what it takes to stay alive in both senses of the phrase -- and what writing toward one's own survival looks like when the two identities are in tension rather than neatly resolved. A good close for a Friday.

Kussow meditates on surviving the dual pull of firefighting and academic life, and what each demands of the body, attention, and identity. The reflection on trauma and art is honest without being theatrical. The 'I should be dead' framing earns its weight in the piece rather than announcing itself as a headline and retreating.

Source: Firefighter Nation
