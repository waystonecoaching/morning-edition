Active-Duty US Soldiers to Receive MDMA Therapy for PTSD Next Year

The DoD is moving MDMA therapy into active-duty treatment, which represents a significant institutional shift. This matters less for clinical specifics than for what it signals: the stigma wall inside uniformed services culture is cracking. First responders track military culture closely; institutional adoption in one sector tends to accelerate the other.

The Guardian reports that active-duty US soldiers are set to receive MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD as part of a DoD-authorized program beginning next year. The article details the approval pathway and expected number of participants. Read critically: MDMA's regulatory path has been choppy, and Guardian health reporting sometimes outruns the actual regulatory text. Verify the program's exact authorization status before citing it.

Try this: The clinical evidence base for MDMA is under scrutiny following the 2024 FDA panel decision. Hold the cultural shift signal; set aside any clinical claims until confirmed by primary sources.
Source: The Guardian

Moral Injury Among Journalists: A Scoping Review

Moral injury research expanding into journalism is useful as a cross-sector lens. The mechanisms under study, witnessing harm without capacity to prevent it, institutional complicity, leadership failure to protect, are structurally identical to what firefighters and paramedics describe. The scoping review format maps the field rather than advancing a single argument, which makes it a useful reference structure.

A scoping review in JTS synthesizing what is known about moral injury in journalism, covering prevalence estimates, the domains most commonly reported (witnessing harm, institutional betrayal), and gaps in the literature. The journalism population is useful as a comparison because they share chronic exposure without operational control, which maps onto EMS and fire in ways worth examining.

America's Moral Injury Pandemic

Moral injury moving into mainstream coverage in Time signals a cultural moment, but the term loses precision when it leaves clinical and military contexts. Litz and Shay built the framework around specific betrayal and complicity mechanisms; popular use flattens it into generalized distress. Worth reading for what it shows about public uptake and worth reading critically for what gets lost.

Time Magazine's feature examines the spread of moral injury discourse beyond its military origins, citing rates of moral distress across occupational groups including healthcare workers and first responders. The piece profiles several individuals and references academic researchers. It is journalism, not research, but it signals where the cultural conversation is heading.

Source: Time Magazine

Hope as Moral Accompaniment: A Framework for Palliative Care Practice

This is the coda. Snyder's hope model, repurposed here for palliative care, draws a distinction the hesychast tradition knows in its own way: hope that accompanies and sustains versus hope that promises a particular outcome. The framework being built here is close kin to what you already carry. Worth reading on its own terms, not only as research.

A peer-reviewed theoretical paper in Frontiers in Psychology proposes framing hope as moral accompaniment in palliative care contexts, drawing on Snyder's hope theory. The argument is that hope's value lies not in predicting recovery but in sustaining the belief that life remains meaningful in adversity. The paper sketches a clinical framework but operates primarily at the conceptual level.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
