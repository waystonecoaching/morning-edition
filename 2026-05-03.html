Combat Veteran Turned Paramedic Launches Peer Support App

A combat vet who crossed over to paramedicine has built a peer support tool from the inside out. This is the lived-experience-to-practice pipeline at work, and it is exactly the kind of initiative that fills gaps departmental wellness programs cannot reach. Worth knowing about for the same reason peer support is worth knowing about: it is often the first safe contact before anything else becomes possible.

A combat veteran who became a paramedic has developed a peer support application designed specifically for first responders navigating trauma, burnout, and moral injury. The app connects users with trained peer supporters rather than clinical staff, lowering the threshold for initial contact. The founder's dual background in military service and EMS shapes the tool's language and framing. This is technology built by someone who understands why the phone call to a formal EMS has to feel different from a call to a buddy.

Try this: Review the app. If it is credible, add it to your referral awareness for clients who are not yet ready for formal coaching or therapy.

State of the Science: MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for PTSD

The Journal of Traumatic Stress just ran a state-of-the-science review on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in its April 2026 issue. Given that the MDMA PTSD trial data has been under serious credibility scrutiny since the Lykos FDA rejection, this is the field attempting to consolidate what is actually known. Read critically. The standard of care question is open, not settled.

This peer-reviewed review in JTS Vol. 39 surveys the current evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy as a PTSD treatment, covering efficacy data, safety profiles, and the state of the regulatory and research pipeline following FDA scrutiny of prior submissions. The piece attempts to distinguish what the data actually supports from what advocacy momentum has claimed. Methodological integrity concerns and the road forward for the research program are addressed. This is a useful orientation piece for practitioners watching the field closely.

Try this: Pull the full text. Note specifically how the authors characterize the unblinding problem and the FDA's concerns about trial design. That is the crux of the credibility stress.

The Professor and the Firefighter: I Should Be Dead

Timothy Andrew Kussow is a firefighter who is also an artist and intellectual, and this piece is him thinking on paper about survival and what it means to still be here. That is a different register than the wellness-program language that dominates this space, and it is worth reading. Stoicism-and-suffering patterns made visible from the inside.

Kussow meditates on the gap between firefighting identity and the interior life that survives it, drawing on his work as a professor and artist alongside his time on the line. The piece holds trauma and meaning together without resolving the tension, which is its value. It does not perform recovery or offer a protocol; it stays with the weight of having been close to death and continuing to live. That kind of first-person honesty is rare in fire service writing.

No. 05 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2025-11-11

Facing Trauma Under the Influence of Psychedelics: A Phenomenological Study with Nova Rave Survivors

This is a peer-reviewed study from a situation that has no precedent in trauma research: the October 7 Nova rave attack, in which most participants were under the influence of psychedelics during a mass casualty event. What happened to those nervous systems, and how did the presence of psychedelics shape the acute trauma response and its aftermath? This is not a therapy study. It is a forced natural experiment with real clinical and ethical implications for anyone working at the intersection of psychedelic use and PTSD.

Using a mixed-methods phenomenological approach, researchers investigated the psychological experience of Nova rave survivors who were on psychedelics at the time of the October 7 attack. The study examines how altered states shaped acute threat perception, dissociation, memory encoding, and subsequent trauma processing. Findings carry implications for understanding both the risks and the potential therapeutic dimensions of psychedelic states during extreme stress. Published in the Journal of Psychopharmacology; this is primary literature, not advocacy.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology (SAGE)

America's Moral Injury Pandemic

When Time runs a piece calling moral injury a pandemic, the construct has cleared the credibility threshold for mainstream discourse. For practitioners, that is a double-edged development: it increases recognition and client willingness to name the experience, but it also risks dilution. Worth reading for how the framing is traveling and where the pressure points are between the operational definition and the cultural one.

Time's piece surveys the spread of moral injury as a recognized psychological wound across populations beyond military and first responders, examining healthcare workers, teachers, and others whose institutional contexts have put them in impossible ethical binds. The article draws on researchers in the field to characterize what distinguishes moral injury from PTSD and depression. The scale language reflects growing public awareness that this is not a niche clinical category. The risk is that broad cultural adoption softens the precision the construct actually requires.

Coaching Is Human-Centered. Can AI Help?

The ICF is attempting to get ahead of AI integration in coaching practice before the questions become awkward. For a solo practitioner, the relevant question is not whether AI can replace the coaching relationship but what it can handle without compromising the quality of contact. The piece's ethical frame is worth knowing, even if the conclusions are predictable.

The ICF piece argues that AI tools can support administrative, preparatory, and reflective functions in coaching practice without displacing the empathic core of the coaching relationship. It surveys available tools and offers ethical guidance for integrating them within ICF standards. The framing positions AI as augmentation rather than replacement, which is honest about the technology's current ceiling. For a solo practice, the relevant use cases are session prep, note-taking, and continuing education rather than session delivery itself.

Try this: Audit where your current administrative load is highest. Those are the slots where AI tools are most likely to create time without compromising the work.

Spectator Violence Is a Form of Moral Injury

The Christian Century is doing theology, and in this piece the theology is doing what good theology does: naming what is true about human experience before the clinical vocabulary exists to hold it. The argument that witnessing violence as a spectator, including through media, constitutes a form of moral injury is worth sitting with. First responders who carry vicarious trauma from what they have seen but not caused live in this territory.

The piece extends the moral injury construct beyond direct perpetration or institutional betrayal to include the experience of spectators who witness violence without the capacity to intervene. Drawing on both theological and psychological sources, it argues that the wound of watching, of having seen something and not being able to act, has a distinct moral dimension that pure trauma language does not fully capture. The Christian Century is a serious journal of religion and public life, and this is a careful piece, not a devotional one.

Source: The Christian Century
