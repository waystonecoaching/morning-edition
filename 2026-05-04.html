FDA to Fast-Track Psychedelic Drugs for PTSD: What This Means

Federal fast-track designation is the structural lever that determines when and how your clients can access these treatments. This is not abstract policy: it reshapes the clinical landscape your practice exists inside. The Healthline piece is consumer health journalism, not primary literature, so read it for the policy summary and follow upstream to the FDA notice itself.

Healthline reports that the FDA has extended fast-track designation to psychedelic compounds for PTSD treatment, a regulatory move intended to accelerate the review timeline for promising therapies. Fast-track does not mean approval; it means the FDA commits to more frequent communication and rolling review. The piece explains what the designation means in practical terms for patients and providers.

Try this: Locate the FDA's actual fast-track notice to distinguish which compounds are included and what the realistic timeline looks like.

America's Moral Injury Pandemic

When Time uses the word 'pandemic' for moral injury, the construct has moved from academic research into the mainstream register. That shift matters for your practice in two directions: it reduces the explanation load with civilian clients, and it increases the risk of the concept being flattened. Read this for how the broader culture is receiving moral injury and where the frame is being stretched beyond what the research supports.

Time frames moral injury as a national phenomenon spreading beyond military and first responder populations into healthcare workers, teachers, and the general public. The piece draws on researchers including Jonathan Shay and Litz while arguing that institutional betrayal and systemic failures are the primary vectors. The mainstream treatment of the concept is accessible but trades some clinical precision for reach.

State of the Science: MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for the Treatment of PTSD

This is the piece to read before you say anything to a client about MDMA therapy. The Journal of Traumatic Stress is peer-reviewed; this is not advocacy. The field took real credibility hits from the MAPS Phase 3 controversies, and a state-of-science review from 2026 is where the clinical community is currently standing. Scope note: this is clinical literature touching PTSD treatment standards; read it as an informed practitioner, not as a clinician making treatment recommendations.

A peer-reviewed review published in the Journal of Traumatic Stress assesses the current evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, covering effect sizes from completed trials, open methodological questions, and the state of regulatory progress. The review situates the therapy within the broader trauma treatment landscape and notes both the promise in the data and the replication and integrity concerns raised by post-trial scrutiny.

The Guardian's framing adds something Time's piece does not: the structural critique lineage, not just the clinical description. The Professor and the Firefighter: I Should Be Dead

A firefighter who is also a professor writing about surviving, about art, about holding two selves. That tension is the work of integration. Not clinical language. Not a framework. Just a man trying to tell the truth about what it costs to have lived the way he lived. This is the close.

Timothy Andrew Kussow writes in first person about surviving, about the distance between the firefighter who ran toward things and the professor who writes about them afterward.
