FDA Plans Ultra-Fast Review of Three Psychedelics for Depression, PTSD

Federal-level news that resets the field. An ultra-fast track for three psychedelic candidates moves the conversation from advocacy to scheduling, which means scope-of-practice questions will arrive faster than most coaches and integration practitioners are ready for. Read the speed itself with some skepticism. Velocity in regulatory review is rarely about the data alone.

The FDA signaled an accelerated review pathway for three psychedelic compounds being studied for major depression and PTSD. The framing is therapeutic urgency around veterans and treatment-resistant cases. The piece does not detail the specific data packages under review, only the procedural shift toward ultra-fast review.

Try this: If you are integrating psychedelic-adjacent clients, write down now where your scope ends. Approval will not change that line. The line gets harder to hold once an FDA label is in the room.
Source: MedPage Today

Feasibility Randomized Controlled Trial of the Mobile Anger Reduction Intervention for Veterans with PTSD

Anger is the responder symptom that hides best inside the culture, mistaken for grit until it costs marriages and careers. A feasibility RCT testing a mobile delivery channel matters because it points toward interventions that bypass the office and meet shift workers where they actually are. Mobile is not magic. The question is whether the active mechanism survives the medium.

Researchers ran a feasibility RCT of a mobile-delivered anger reduction intervention with veterans diagnosed with PTSD. The study reports usability and engagement signal sufficient for a larger trial. Effect sizes at this stage are preliminary, and feasibility is not efficacy. The piece is a peer-reviewed early signal, not a recommendation.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

First Responders with PTSD Are Turning to Arizona's Psilocybin Trial

This is your backyard. Arizona running a state-funded psilocybin trial with first responders as the population means Phoenix Fire colleagues will start asking real questions. Some already are. The integration support side of this work needs people who know shift culture from the inside. That is the gap your practice is positioned to fill, and the place to be most careful about scope.

InvestigateTV+ reports on Arizona's psilocybin trial recruiting first responders with PTSD, profiling participants and the structural bet the state is making on novel treatment for treatment-resistant trauma. The piece carries the usual broadcast frame of hope and breakthrough. The harder work happens in the months after the medicine, which the segment touches only briefly.

Try this: Track the official trial site and protocol, not the broadcast version. If a Phoenix Fire colleague brings this up, you want to know who is doing the integration arm and what their intake looks like before you offer anything.

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

A state government and an advocacy organization rolling out psychedelic crisis assessment and de-escalation training to first responders is a direct read on where your behavioral crisis role is headed. Worth reading because Arizona will follow some version of this. Worth reading critically because MAPS is a movement actor with a position, not a neutral training body, and that shapes the curriculum.

MAPS announced a partnership with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to deliver MAPS' Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training, free, to first responders across the state. The program is video and assessment based and frames itself as evidence-informed de-escalation for psychedelic-related calls.

Try this: Look at the curriculum outline if MAPS publishes one. Compare against what your behavioral crisis team actually sees on the radio. The gap between training scenario and real call is where field-level skill lives.
Source: MAPS

State of the Science: MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for the Treatment of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

This is the kind of piece to read before forming an opinion about any of the headline news above. A peer-reviewed state-of-the-science review in a credible trauma journal is where the field gets honest about what the data does and does not yet support. Given the credibility stress on the MDMA program over the last cycle, this matters.

The review surveys clinical trial evidence for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in PTSD, including efficacy signals, methodological limitations, blinding and expectancy concerns, and adverse event reporting. It situates the work against the FDA's prior decision and the current regulatory and replication questions facing the field. It is a cautious read, which is what state-of-the-science pieces are for.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

America's Moral Injury Pandemic

Mainstream press is starting to use Litz and Shay's vocabulary without translating it down. The piece sits in the structural-critique camp: moral injury as something done to people by institutions that asked them to violate their own ethical sense, not just as a private wound to manage. That framing is the one your fire and EMS clients usually need first, before anything resembling treatment language lands.

Time examines the spread of moral injury as a clinical and cultural concept beyond combat veterans and into healthcare, education, and first response. The piece names betrayal-by-leadership and systemic failure as primary drivers. It treats moral injury as a pandemic-era diagnosis-of-the-times, with the limitations that framing carries.

Source: Time Magazine

Moral Injury Among Journalists: A Scoping Review

A scoping review on moral injury in a non-combat occupational population shows the construct is broadening on solid academic ground, not just in op-eds. Journalists, like firefighters and paramedics, are exposed witnesses with constrained agency. The methodological move here is the same one your field needs: building moral injury vocabulary for people who never wore a uniform but carry comparable weight.

The review maps the existing literature on moral injury in journalists, identifying themes of betrayal, witnessing, and constraint, and notes the field is early in measurement and intervention work. It calls for occupation-specific instruments and treatment frameworks rather than wholesale import from veteran research. The implication for first responder work is clear, even when not stated.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-05-02

How Connection Becomes a Pathway to Resilience Under Threat

A clean closing note. Connection is not a soft skill in the autonomic frame, it is the substrate. Co-regulation precedes any of the work the rest of this issue describes. After a day's worth of policy news and clinical reviews, this is the one to leave on the desk.

The piece argues that under sustained threat, perceived isolation amplifies physiological dysregulation while connection blunts it, and that resilience is less an internal trait than a relational condition. It draws lightly on polyvagal-adjacent ideas without overreaching. A reasonable summary of what most of the contemplative and trauma-informed traditions have said for a long time.

Source: Psychology Today
