State of the Science: MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

JOTS state-of-the-science reviews are the closest the field gets to a consensus document, and this one lands in the middle of an active credibility argument over MDMA-AT. Read it as the reference point you cite when a client or colleague asks where the evidence actually sits, not where the press release says it sits. If the standard of care for first responder PTSD shifts in the next eighteen months, this is the paper the clinicians arguing about it will be holding.

The review consolidates trial data, mechanism work, and methodological critique on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, including the limitations that pushed the FDA to reject Lykos in 2024. It treats functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and adverse-event reporting as central questions, not footnotes. The piece is more measured than the advocacy press but does not dismiss the signal in the underlying response data.

Try this: Pull the PDF and mark the section on functional unblinding. That is the single argument you will encounter most often in client and referral conversations.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

First responders with PTSD are turning to Arizona's psilocybin trial

This is the trial running in your backyard, with the population you work with. Whatever you think of the research design, the referral pathway from Phoenix Fire and surrounding agencies into psilocybin protocols is being built right now, and your clients are reading these stories. Worth knowing what they are seeing before they ask you about it.

InvestigateTV profiles the Arizona-based psilocybin trial enrolling first responders with PTSD, following participants through preparation, dosing, and integration. The reporting leans optimistic but includes cautionary notes about screening, adverse events, and the gap between trial conditions and unregulated retreats. Departments and unions are watching enrollment and outcomes closely.

Try this: If a Phoenix Fire member raises this in session, have a one-line scope-of-practice answer ready: you can hold integration support around their experience, but you do not screen, dose, or refer to extralegal access.

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

Free, state-backed Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training rolling out to Colorado responders is exactly the kind of program Arizona will copy if it works. As a behavioral crisis specialist embedded with PFD, this is the curriculum you should know cold, even if your role is upstream of the call. It also signals where the BTTY training lane and harm-reduction lane are converging in public-sector response.

MAPS announced a partnership with Colorado's Natural Medicine Division to provide its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention curriculum free to first responders statewide. The program covers de-escalation, evidence-based education, and screening for adverse psychedelic experiences in the field. Rollout is video-based with assessments, designed for shift-friendly completion.

Try this: Check whether the Phoenix Fire training office can request the MAPS modules now, ahead of any Arizona-level program. Even informal access would inform the kind of calls your team is already running.

When the Helpers Feel Helpless: First Responders Get a Boost in Mental Health Support

Trade press, but the framing matters: the piece names that responders still hesitate to use the resources their departments have stood up. That is the shift culture problem your work sits inside of every day. Pay attention to which interventions the article credits with closing that gap, and which it merely lists.

Firefighter Nation surveys recent additions to first responder mental health programming: peer support expansions, embedded clinicians, and behavioral health units in fire and EMS agencies. The piece acknowledges that program existence and program use remain different things, and points at stigma, scheduling, and confidentiality as the binding constraints. It reads as a status update on a slow-moving culture shift.

Source: Firefighter Nation

No. 05 · First Responder MH · 2026-04-23

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

A scoping review on a non-military, non-responder population that nonetheless shows the Litz and Shay structural frame travels. Useful as a reference when you push back against the operational frame's tendency to relocate moral injury inside the individual. Journalists, like responders, are positioned by their work to witness things that the institution then refuses to metabolize.

The review maps the emerging literature on moral injury in journalists covering conflict, trauma, and institutional failure. It identifies betrayal by employers, exposure to atrocity, and the inability to act on what is witnessed as the consistent injurious mechanisms. Methodologically the field is young, with few longitudinal designs and inconsistent measurement, which the authors flag as the next priority.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Feasibility randomized controlled trial of the mobile anger reduction intervention for veterans with PTSD

Anger is the symptom that often gets responders into your office before PTSD does. A feasibility RCT on a mobile, between-session anger intervention is exactly the kind of bridge tool worth tracking, both for what it suggests about between-session work in your own practice and for what your veteran-adjacent clients are likely to be offered through the VA in the next year.

The trial tested a mobile-delivered anger reduction protocol with veterans diagnosed with PTSD, measuring feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effect on anger expression and dysregulation. Engagement and retention met the feasibility thresholds, and signal on anger reduction was promising though underpowered. The authors call for a fully powered efficacy trial as the next step.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 07 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-04-07

Not unique to war: millions of Americans suffer from moral injury. What's causing it?

The Guardian profile follows researchers and clinicians making the case that moral injury is showing up in healthcare workers, teachers, and other roles structured around impossible obligations. It draws the line between moral injury and burnout, names institutional betrayal as the most consistent driver, and challenges the assumption that war is the natural home of the construct. Litz, Shay, and clinical voices feature.

Moral injury is what happens when an institution asks you to act against your own conscience, then refuses to acknowledge what it cost you.

The popularization of moral injury beyond combat is good for your client work and dangerous for the field at the same time. Useful because it loosens the grip of the operational frame; risky because it can dilute the construct into general distress. Read it as a cultural marker of how the term is travelling, then keep your own usage tight.

Source: The Guardian

Body-oriented approaches to healing stress and trauma

Local TV coverage, but the topic is the substrate beneath everything you do. When the somatic frame leaks into general media, your responder clients begin to recognize their own nervous systems in the language. That recognition is its own intervention. Read this as a temperature check on how widely the polyvagal vocabulary is now circulating.

The segment introduces somatic and body-based modalities for trauma to a general audience: breath, orientation, gentle movement, and titrated exposure to sensation. It leans on the now-standard claim that talk therapy alone underreaches trauma stored in the autonomic nervous system. Specific modalities are named lightly rather than evaluated.

No. 09 · Coaching Practice · 2026-05-04

Owning Your Story: The Soul of a Coaching Business

ICF rarely runs identity-first pieces, so when one lands it is worth reading for what the accreditation body is willing to validate as practice-building. The argument that visibility, money, and worthiness sit upstream of infrastructure is not new, but for a solo first responder coach the specific shape that takes is worth attention. Not a how-to. A mirror.

The piece argues that coaches who treat their business as a marketing problem before they treat it as an identity problem stall out. It walks through the recurring binds around visibility, pricing, and the story a coach tells about their own worth, and frames these as practice questions rather than business questions. Reflective rather than prescriptive.

Try this: Take twenty minutes this week to write the sentence that sits at the center of why you carry the work you do. Do not edit it for the website yet.
Source: ICF

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-05-02

How connection becomes a pathway to resilience under threat

A small piece, but it names what the desert tradition has always known: the regulating presence of another is not optional equipment. Co-regulation is the load-bearing wall of resilience, not its decoration. Sit with that before the next call.

The article frames connection itself, in the polyvagal sense, as the mechanism by which threat states become survivable rather than merely endured. It draws on Porges and Dana to argue that resilience is less an internal trait than a relational capacity, built through repeated experiences of felt safety with another nervous system. Brief, accessible, and squarely in the lineage you already work from.

Source: Psychology Today
