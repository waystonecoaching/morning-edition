Active-duty US soldiers to receive MDMA therapy for PTSD next year

The line between trial and standard of care is moving. If MDMA reaches active-duty soldiers in 2027, fire and EMS will not be far behind. Worth tracking: which programs, which therapists, what scope of practice the rollout assumes around integration.

The Guardian reports that the US Department of Defense is preparing to administer MDMA-assisted therapy to active-duty service members with PTSD beginning next year. The framing centers veteran and active-duty access, not civilian first responders, but the credentialing and protocol decisions will set the template. Readers should ask what changed in the regulatory or DoD sandbox after the FDA's 2024 rejection of Lykos, since the piece does not address that question head-on.

Try this: Watch for the rollout's published clinician training requirements. Compare against BTTY's Level 2 lane to gauge where the field is converging.

State of the Science: MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

Read this alongside the policy news. JTS publishes a sober review series; this is the academic counterpoint to the Guardian piece on the same week the standard of care is being negotiated in public. Critical reading is the assignment.

The Journal of Traumatic Stress State of the Science series synthesizes evidence on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD: efficacy signals, methodological limitations, blinding problems, and the gap between trial settings and real-world delivery. The review is not advocacy. It surveys what is known, what is contested, and what the field has not yet established. Useful as a reference when clients or colleagues bring uncritical enthusiasm.

The Room 16 Protocol: A Protocol for Supporting First Responders in Mental Health Crisis

Sits squarely in the BCMHS lane. A field-built protocol for the moment when a responder presents in crisis to a hospital or peer-receiving room. Worth reading not just for the steps but for what the protocol assumes about scope, transport, and the receiving clinician's role.

JEMS publishes a protocol named for the dedicated room used to receive first responders presenting in mental health crisis. The protocol covers intake, assessment, peer presence, and the handoff between peer support and clinical care. The point is to remove the ad-hoc element from a high-acuity moment that historically depended on whoever happened to be on shift. Note the scope-of-practice edges: peer presence is built into the receiving phase, but clinical assessment is held by licensed staff.

Try this: Compare the protocol's assessment language against the language Phoenix uses on the BCMHS side. Where they diverge, ask why.
Source: JEMS

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

Not first responders, but adjacent: a high-exposure profession with chronic moral injury and weak institutional supports. The scoping review's framework transfers cleanly to fire and EMS. Useful as scaffolding when describing moral injury to clients who confuse it with PTSD.

The review maps the literature on moral injury among journalists, identifying causes (witnessing atrocity, reporting on suffering, organizational betrayal), correlates (depression, substance use, withdrawal), and gaps (few interventions tested, little longitudinal data). The structural-critique frame dominates: moral injury is portrayed less as individual pathology and more as the predictable cost of chronic exposure inside institutions that do not protect their people. Worth contrasting against operational-resilience framings that locate the work inside the responder's regulation skills.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 05 · First Responder MH · 2026-05-06

Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Most prevalence work surveys active-duty personnel. This one looks at former first responders, the population that walks into coaching after the badge comes off. The meta-analytic finding becomes the baseline assumption when intaking a retiree.

JTS publishes a systematic review and meta-analysis pooling PTSD prevalence estimates across studies of former first responders. The work is methodologically conservative: heterogeneity is named, retirement-related selection effects are addressed, and the pooled estimate carries appropriate uncertainty. The review's value is as much in what it surfaces about the field's measurement gaps as in the headline number. Worth the full read before the abstract gets quoted out of context.

Try this: When a retired responder enters intake, treat trauma load as the default working assumption rather than a question to explore later.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

MAPS & State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

First state-level rollout of a psychedelic crisis intervention curriculum for first responders. This is the kind of operational move that arrives quietly and reshapes what fire and EMS personnel are expected to know on calls involving Natural Medicine clients. Phoenix is not Denver, but the curriculum will travel.

The Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies announced a partnership with Colorado's Natural Medicine Division to provide MAPS' Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide. The training is free, video-based, and includes assessments. It targets the gap between Colorado's regulated psilocybin program and the responders who will field calls involving its participants. The announcement does not specify uptake metrics or how the training intersects with existing CIT or behavioral health response protocols.

Try this: Pull the curriculum if open-access. Useful raw material for the Waystone integration framework even outside Colorado.
Source: MAPS

Dena Ali: How leaders shape firefighter wellness

Ali argues that firefighter wellness outcomes track leadership behavior more closely than program design. Her piece walks through what officers actually do that signals safety to a crew, and what they do that quietly closes the door. The argument lands inside the operational-resilience frame, not the structural-critique one, but she is not naive about institutional drag. Useful as a coaching artifact for officer-track clients.

Wellness rises and falls with what the officer does, not with what the program promises.

Ali has been one of the more honest voices on firefighter mental health for a decade. The leadership angle matters because most peer support programs collapse when the chief stops showing up. Worth reading if any current or prospective client carries officer rank.

Source: FireRescue1

No. 08 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-04-09

America's Moral Injury Pandemic

Time runs the term 'moral injury' into the mainstream. Mixed news: more recognition, more dilution. When the diagnostic word leaves the literature, it gets used for everything, which is the moment a coach has to be clear about what it is and what it is not.

Time's feature places moral injury in the cultural foreground, drawing on Litz, Shay, and the post-Iraq veteran lineage to argue that the construct now reaches into healthcare, education, and civic life. The piece is more persuasion than primary literature, but it captures the field's current center of gravity well. Use it as a starting point with clients who have heard the term and want to know if it applies to them, not as the definition.

Source: Time Magazine

Owning Your Story: The Soul of a Coaching Business

ICF rarely writes in this register. The piece names something solo coaches know and rarely articulate: the practice is built on identity before infrastructure. Worth reading slow. The Waystone story is already intact; the question is whether it is being told.

The ICF post argues that a coaching practice's external visibility, pricing, and offer all rest on the coach's relationship to their own story. Themes covered: worthiness, the trap of borrowed credentials, the cost of waiting until the practice is fully built before letting it be seen. The piece is reflective rather than tactical, written for the practitioner doing identity work rather than marketing strategy.

Try this: One question for the week: where in the Waystone presentation is the story being softened or held back, and what does that softening cost the people who would have recognized themselves in it.
