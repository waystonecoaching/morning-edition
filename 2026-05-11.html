Psilocybin in the treatment of cocaine use disorder: a randomized clinical trial

Primary literature in JAMA. Psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy tested as a treatment for cocaine use disorder, with a critical appraisal published alongside it (see slot 02). Mark's referral lane through BTTY touches substance integration; this is the closest thing the peer-reviewed record has produced this year on psilocybin and stimulant use. Read the trial first, then read what the editors decided needed appraising in the same breath.

JAMA published a randomized clinical trial of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy for adults with cocaine use disorder. The study compares psilocybin plus structured psychotherapy against an active control on a pilot-scale randomized design. JAMA's editorial team paired the publication with a critical appraisal, which signals the journal's posture: results are interesting enough to publish, methodological concerns are real enough to publish alongside.

Try this: Pull the trial and the appraisal together before referencing either to a client or to a BTTY peer. The pair is the unit, not the trial alone.
Source: JAMA

No. 02 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-05-07

Pilot results for psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy for cocaine use disorder: a critical appraisal

Companion piece to the JAMA trial. That JAMA chose to publish a structured appraisal alongside the pilot tells you the posture the field is in. This is what reading the literature critically looks like in real time; the appraisal is not opposition to the trial, it is the editorial discipline the psychedelic research space has been accused of lacking.

JAMA invited and published a critical appraisal of the psilocybin and cocaine pilot. The appraisal examines design choices, control conditions, blinding limitations, sample size, and the gap between pilot-scale signal and any claim about standard of care. Worth reading whether or not you read the trial first.

Source: JAMA

'We're on edge': 911 dispatchers, EMS crews feel strain in wake of Moxham shootings

Field-level signal from the days after a mass shooting in Johnstown, Pennsylvania. The piece holds the population that gets forgotten in incident debriefs: the dispatchers who heard it happen and the EMS crews who ran the scene. Worth holding because dispatcher trauma is structurally invisible to most peer support programs, including the ones Phoenix runs.

WJAC interviews 911 dispatchers and EMS crews in the aftermath of the Moxham Street shootings, where multiple people were killed and the call volume and emotional load on responders ran high for days. Voices in the piece name fatigue, hypervigilance, and the second wave that comes after the scene is cleared. Pieces like this are the ground truth that peer support programs are often slow to read.

Source: WJAC

Facing trauma under the influence of psychedelics: a phenomenological study with Nova rave survivors

Peer-reviewed phenomenological study examining what it was like to face a mass-casualty terror attack while under the influence of psychedelics. Sits directly in the territory BTTY teaches into: the integration of difficult experience as path rather than problem. Read it for the structure of the participants' meaning-making, not for advocacy. Scope-of-practice edge: high-acuity trauma in altered states, well outside coaching's primary lane.

The October 7, 2023 attack at the Nova rave produced an unprecedented research scenario in which most participants were under the influence of psychedelics when the violence began. This mixed-methods phenomenological study interviews survivors on how the substance shaped encoding, dissociation, and later integration of the trauma. Not a treatment study; a structural one. Useful for any practitioner working in mass-casualty terrain where altered states are part of the picture.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-04-28

Spectator violence is a form of moral injury

Closes the edition in the structural-critique camp. Christian Century essay arguing that the harm in observed violence is best read through moral injury rather than vicarious trauma alone. Theological register, which is your register. Read it slowly. Holds the same question Mark holds at every Phoenix Fire call: what does it mean to witness, and what gets injured in the witnessing.

An essay arguing that the language of moral injury, developed for combat veterans and adapted for first responders, extends naturally to witnesses of violence whose participation is involuntary or by proximity. The frame moves the conversation from individual coping to the question of what is owed to those who saw. Theological, not clinical.

Source: The Christian Century
