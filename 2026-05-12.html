MAPS and Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

First responders in Colorado now have a structured harm reduction pathway for handling psychedelic crises in the field. The training comes from MAPS in formal partnership with the state's Natural Medicine Division, which signals that Colorado is treating this as part of public safety infrastructure rather than fringe. For a Phoenix-side practitioner, the relevance is twofold: how Arizona will eventually need to answer the same questions, and how Being True to You graduates can speak to incident commanders without translating across worlds.

MAPS announced a partnership with Colorado's Department of Revenue Natural Medicine Division to deliver free Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide. The curriculum covers de-escalation, screening for emergent risk, and integration handoffs. Rollout begins with regional cohorts and is funded through Colorado's regulatory program.

PTSD Prevalence Among Former First Responders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

This is the systematic review the field has been short on. Most prevalence data on first responder PTSD covers active duty; what happens after retirement has stayed thinner and softer. Numbers from a synthesis at this scale become the language used to argue for transition coaching and retiree-focused peer programs.

A systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Traumatic Stress estimates PTSD prevalence among former first responders, pooling studies across police, fire, EMS, and dispatch. The authors compare prevalence by role and by time since separation, and flag heterogeneity in screening instruments. The paper sits in the EarlyView stack and warrants reading the methods section before citing the headline numbers.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

It's Time to Retire 'Mental Health'

Stephanie White names the bind explicitly. The choice between 'suck it up' stoicism and trauma-celebrity culture is a false one, and both keep firefighters in performative mode. She argues that the term 'mental health' has been worn smooth by repetition and now carries either too much shame or too much spectacle to be useful. Worth reading for the framing alone, and worth holding alongside the moral injury work rather than smoothing it.

Stephanie White, writing in Firefighter Nation, argues that the language of 'mental health' in the fire service has collapsed into two failure modes. One is the older silence that treats interiority as weakness. The other is the newer culture of trauma disclosure that performs vulnerability without producing change. She calls for retiring the phrase in favor of language that names the work without staging it.

Source: Firefighter Nation

Active-Duty US Soldiers to Receive MDMA Therapy for PTSD Next Year

The first formal program putting MDMA-assisted therapy in front of active-duty US soldiers is moving forward. This is a meaningful step on the PTSD standard-of-care question for combat veterans, and the responder field will inherit the protocols, the dosing arguments, and the supervision standards that come out of it. Read the policy mechanism, not just the headlines. The MDMA field remains under credibility stress after the 2024 FDA rejection.

The Guardian reports that active-duty US service members will begin receiving MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD next year through a Department of Defense pilot. The pilot structure builds on Veterans Affairs work and includes formal supervision protocols and outcome tracking. The piece situates the program against the political crosswinds and the unresolved methodological critiques on MAPS' phase 3 data.

Source: The Guardian

State of the Science: MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for the Treatment of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

A formal state-of-the-science synthesis on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, published in the Journal of Traumatic Stress. This is the source to bring into BTTY peer-supervision conversations and to cite when a client or referral source asks where the evidence actually stands after the FDA's 2024 rejection and the Lykos restructure. The MDMA-PTSD field sits on a scope-of-practice edge for coaching work; treat it as such.

The Journal of Traumatic Stress publishes a state-of-the-science review on MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, summarizing the trial literature, methodological critiques, and the regulatory environment following the FDA's 2024 rejection. The authors map the open questions on therapist effects, expectancy bias, blinding integrity, and durability of response. The piece is restrained where the advocacy press has been loud.

Try this: Save a copy with notes for the next BTTY peer-supervision call. This is the citation to have ready when a referring clinician asks for primary literature.

The Room 16 Protocol: Supporting First Responders in Mental Health Crisis

A named, repeatable protocol for handling a first responder in active mental health crisis at the station or firehouse, written by people who do the work rather than by academics. Worth comparing against what Phoenix Fire's behavioral crisis program already has on paper. This sits at the high-acuity edge: suicidality, substance use, and acute decompensation are in the room.

The Room 16 Protocol is a published procedure from First HELP and Safe Call Now for taking a first responder out of service when they present in mental health crisis. It covers immediate stabilization, supervisor notification, peer support activation, and clinical handoff. The protocol is designed to be operational and is being adopted as a station-level reference document by departments looking for a clear playbook.

Try this: Print the protocol and walk it through one peer support shift. Map the friction points before recommending it as a station standard.

Dena Ali: How Leaders Shape Firefighter Wellness

Dena Ali is one of the steadier voices on fire service mental health, and her argument here is that culture moves down from leadership, not up from individual firefighters. Useful when you are asked to consult or speak to a department: the lever sits with the officer, not the curriculum.

Dena Ali, writing for FireRescue1, argues that firefighter wellness outcomes are shaped most by what officers and chiefs model day to day, not by add-on wellness programs. She offers concrete leadership behaviors: how time off is talked about, who is allowed to ask for help, what humor sounds like at the kitchen table. The piece reads as a leadership tool more than an awareness piece.

Source: FireRescue1
