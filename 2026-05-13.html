State of the Science: MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for PTSD

The most consequential synthesis of the year for anyone working at the seam between psychedelic-assisted therapy and first responder PTSD. JTS is not advocacy press. When a flagship trauma journal publishes a State of the Science review, it shapes what training programs cite and what payers eventually fund. Read this before you take another integration session involving MDMA exposure history.

A peer-reviewed synthesis of the MDMA-AT evidence base covering completed Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials, methodological critiques raised during the 2024 FDA review, and the gaps that still block approval. The paper holds two things at once: meaningful symptom reduction signals across multiple trials, and unresolved questions about blinding, functional unblinding, and adverse event capture. It does not call the question closed in either direction.

Try this: Read the methodology section before your next BTTY cohort discussion. If a client asks where MDMA-AT stands in May 2026, this is the citation to know.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

PTSD Prevalence Among Former First Responders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Most PTSD prevalence work in the first responder space studies active personnel. This one studies the people who have left. That distinction matters for retired Phoenix Fire members, for medics who walked away, for officers who finished a career carrying something they never named. The post-career window is exactly where coaching, integration, and pastoral care are most needed and least staffed.

Meta-analysis pooling prevalence estimates for PTSD among former firefighters, police, paramedics, and dispatchers. The pooled estimate runs meaningfully higher than active-duty comparisons in several subgroups, with heterogeneity tied to time since separation, exposure type, and measurement instrument. The authors call for longitudinal follow-up beyond retirement rather than cross-sectional snapshots.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

US Health Experts Vote Against MDMA as Treatment for PTSD

A continuation of the credibility stress the field has been carrying since the 2024 advisory vote. Pair this with the JTS State of the Science piece in slot one. The contradiction is real. A serious practitioner needs to be able to hold both at the same time without flattening either side, especially when sitting across from a responder who has read headlines and wants to know what is true.

Coverage of an expert panel vote opposing MDMA approval for PTSD on grounds of trial design and adverse-event accounting. The vote is non-binding but signals continued institutional caution. Reporting is thin on which panel and which jurisdiction, which is itself a reminder to read the primary statement rather than the wire copy.

Try this: Track down the primary panel statement before quoting this in any client conversation. Wire copy on this topic has been unreliable.

The Room 16 Protocol: Supporting First Responders in Mental Health Crisis

Direct relevance to the Phoenix CAP role. A named, written protocol for what happens when the patient is one of our own is the difference between competent handoff and improvisation. Note where it sits relative to scope of practice. Crisis intervention is not therapy and not coaching. The protocol should make those lines clearer, not blur them.

JEMS publishes a structured protocol developed at a U.S. department for handling acute psychiatric emergencies in first responders, including peer-supported transport, privacy protections, and a separate intake pathway away from the public ED. The piece argues that the same crew dropping off a colleague in crisis should not have to wait in triage among civilians.

Try this: Worth pulling for the Phoenix peer support and CAP teams. Compare against current local handoff practice. Note any gaps that fall on the behavioral crisis specialist role specifically.

Physiological and Affective Mechanisms of Shame Among Veterans with PTSD

Shame is the affect that hides longest in the responder population and the one most likely to be misread as cynicism, withdrawal, or burnout. This paper grounds the experience in measurable autonomic and affective signatures, which means polyvagal language has empirical company. Useful for any framework conversation about why narrative-only interventions undershoot.

Lab study examining heart rate variability, skin conductance, and self-report affect during shame induction in veterans with PTSD symptoms versus controls. Findings suggest distinct autonomic patterns under shame compared with fear or anger, with implications for assessment and intervention design. The discussion connects to existing work on social-engagement system disruption.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Active-Duty US Soldiers to Receive MDMA Therapy for PTSD Next Year

DoD signal. Pair this with both the JTS synthesis and the panel-vote pushback. Federal trajectory and academic-clinical pushback are moving in opposite directions at the same time, which is exactly the field condition that asks coaches and integration practitioners to be more careful, not less.

The Guardian reports an active-duty pilot program at a U.S. military medical facility scheduled to begin treating soldiers with MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD next year, supported by a recent executive order. The piece notes ongoing FDA caution and frames the move as the federal branch routing around regulatory delay rather than because of resolved efficacy questions.

It's Time to Retire 'Mental Health'

A language argument that is really a framing argument. Stephanie White names the rhetorical trap between suck-it-up culture and what she calls trauma celebrity, and asks whether the term mental health itself has become so flattened that it no longer points at anything operational. Worth reading next to whatever language the Waystone site currently uses.

White argues that the phrase mental health in the fire service now collapses two opposite distortions into one container: a stoicism that refuses care and a performative vulnerability that mistakes disclosure for healing. She proposes language closer to readiness, recovery, and functional capacity. The piece is short, polemical, and gets the diagnosis right even where the prescription is thin.

Source: Firefighter Nation

When Your Workplace Doesn't Match Your Ethical Outlook: The Problem of Moral Injury

A structural-critique frame on moral injury, sitting closer to Litz and Shay than to the operational HPRC lineage. The argument is that the wound is not inside the worker, it is in the gap between what the worker holds as right and what the institution requires. That distinction changes what an intervention is even supposed to do. Hold the tension with the resilience-skills framing camp rather than smoothing it.

An accessible primer on moral injury that locates the harm at the institutional and ethical seam rather than treating it as an individual symptom set. The piece touches on healthcare and public safety contexts and argues that organizational accountability matters at least as much as individual treatment.
