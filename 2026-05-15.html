PTSD prevalence among former first responders: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Peer-reviewed meta-analysis, not advocacy press. This is the kind of number you can cite cleanly when a chief or a captain asks why a coaching engagement is even necessary. It also reframes the population: former responders, the ones who have already left the job, are where the highest PTSD signal lives.

A pooled analysis across multiple cohort studies of retired and separated firefighters, police, paramedics, and dispatchers, reporting elevated PTSD prevalence relative to active-duty samples. The authors argue post-career exposure to unresolved cumulative trauma is under-measured by the active-duty literature most departments rely on. The review also flags gaps in screening protocols for separated personnel and recommends prevalence-aware referral pathways.

Try this: If you are framing the Waystone problem statement, use post-career prevalence figures rather than active-duty ones. The gap between the two is the case for early intake.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Executive order fast-tracks psychedelics to treat mental health disorders in veterans

Federal action on the path BTTY-trained practitioners actually walk. Worth reading with the credibility ledger open: a fast-track order shortens timelines and also shortens the window for safety data on a high-acuity population. Veterans first, but first responders are the next obvious cohort.

The order directs HHS, the VA, and the DEA to coordinate on rapid evaluation pathways for psilocybin and MDMA in the treatment of PTSD and related conditions in veterans. It instructs agencies to identify regulatory bottlenecks and to expand access to clinical trials, while leaving rescheduling on its current path. Implementation specifics, including training requirements for facilitators and provider scope, are not yet defined.

Try this: Watch for follow-on guidance from the VA on facilitator credentialing. That guidance is the leading edge of what state licensing boards will absorb next.
Source: U.S. Medicine (via Psychedelic PTSD Responder Query)

Minneapolis firefighters taking PTSD leave in increasing numbers, driving up overtime costs

The financial framing is doing real work here. When a city budget office starts tracking PTSD leave as an overtime line item, that is the moment department leadership becomes serious about wellness. It also exposes the structural-critique side of moral injury: the cost lives downstream of the call, and someone has to pay it.

Roughly thirty Minneapolis firefighters are currently on PTSD-related leave, generating measurable overtime burden on the remaining force. City officials are weighing program responses, including peer support expansion and screening protocols, against ongoing staffing strain. The story names the feedback loop explicitly: more leave compresses shifts for the rest, which raises exposure, which feeds the next wave of leave.

Source: Firefighter Nation

Psilocybin for chronic suicidal ideation: Aaronson on safety, an efficacy signal, and the role of expectations

Scope-of-practice edge, named directly. Aaronson at Sheppard Pratt is deliberately recruiting the higher-risk patients most trials exclude, including those with chronic suicidal ideation. The piece is also honest about expectancy effects, which is the part of the literature most advocacy press hides.

Aaronson summarizes early findings from his group's psilocybin trials in patients with chronic suicidal ideation, a population usually screened out of psychedelic studies. He reports an early efficacy signal alongside a tractable safety profile under controlled conditions, while flagging that expectancy and set/setting bias the read on every endpoint. The interview also discusses the design implications of working with high-acuity patients and the staffing model required to do so responsibly.

Try this: If a client discloses chronic suicidal ideation, this is clinical territory. Coaching scope ends at the threshold. Use this piece as reading, not as a referral pathway.
Source: Psychedelic Alpha

No. 05 · First Responder MH · 2026-05-13

Daily-life PTSD symptoms following the processing of positive memories technique: an open-label case-control diary study

Most trauma research interrogates the wound. This one interrogates the surrounding tissue: positive memory and its capacity to shift daily-life symptom load. Small study, open-label, but the methodological move is worth following.

Participants completed a brief positive memory processing protocol and tracked posttraumatic symptoms, cognitions, and affect across multiple daily-life timepoints. The case-control diary design captured short-window changes that conventional pre/post measurement misses. Authors report symptom reductions and cognitive shifts that warrant a controlled trial, while acknowledging open-label bias.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Oklahoma city diverts over 6,000 mental health 911 calls in first year of program

This is functionally the same model you work inside at Phoenix Fire. A first-year diversion number of this size is a policy proof-of-concept. Watch which states copy the staffing template and which copy only the press release.

An Oklahoma municipal program diverted more than 6,000 mental health 911 calls away from traditional police and fire response in its first year, routing them to behavioral crisis specialists. Officials cite reductions in unnecessary hospital transports and lower per-call cost, alongside higher reported client satisfaction. The piece does not detail outcomes for high-acuity calls or how the program escalates when scene safety shifts.

No. 07 · Peer Support · 2026-05-07

Dena Ali: how leaders shape firefighter wellness

Ali argues that wellness in the firehouse is downstream of leadership behavior, not posters or annual trainings. She names the specific patterns that erode psychological safety: schedule capriciousness, silence around line-of-duty deaths, performative resilience talk that contradicts day-to-day staffing decisions. The piece closes on what leaders can actually do, including how they handle their own visible struggle.

Wellness is not a poster on the bay wall. It is what the chief does on the worst day of the year.

Ali is one of the most credible voices on firefighter mental health from inside the service. Her work is the operational frame for wellness leadership without the suffering-as-virtue veneer the culture often defaults to. Useful pairing for any captain or chief in a Waystone intake.

Source: FireRescue1

MAPS and State of Colorado partner to deliver free harm reduction training for first responders

This is the operational intersection of your two lanes: psychedelic literacy and first responder field readiness. Colorado is functionally beta-testing a model other states will copy. Worth watching whether Arizona absorbs the curriculum or builds its own.

MAPS and the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division are offering the Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention curriculum to first responders statewide at no cost. The training covers evidence-based de-escalation, distinguishing acute psychedelic distress from medical or psychiatric emergencies, and harm reduction protocols. Delivery is mixed video and assessment.

Try this: Forward this to the Phoenix Fire training lead. Even if Arizona is not ready to adopt, a referenceable curriculum is leverage.

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-05-09

Considering moral injury

A theological reading of moral injury rather than a clinical one. For a hesychast working with first responders, this is the frame the field most often refuses to hold: that the wound is not a malfunction of the nervous system but a faithful response of a conscience that recognized something. Read it as the close.

The essay positions moral injury inside a longer Christian conversation about conscience, witness, and the weight of doing what cannot be undone. It distinguishes guilt over an act from grief over what an act required of you, and resists the move that pathologizes the latter. The author argues that integration, not extinction, is the work, and that the work is liturgical as much as clinical.

Source: Patheos (via Moral Injury Research Query)
