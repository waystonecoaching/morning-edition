Short-Term and Late-Term Effects of Psilocybin on Symptoms in Major Depression: A Randomized Clinical Trial

JAMA-tier evidence on psilocybin in major depression is the kind of data that actually moves clinical posture. The late-term arm is where the questions live for integration work: what holds after the medicine wears off, and what the therapeutic frame around it is doing. Read it before the advocacy press translates it.

A randomized clinical trial examining both short-term symptom change and durability of response to psilocybin in adults with major depressive disorder. The paper reports effect sizes at the acute timepoint and at extended follow-up, and frames the role of preparation and integration context around the dose. Coverage of late-term data in this field has been thin, so the paper sets a reference line others will be measured against.

Source: JAMA

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Prevalence Among Former First Responders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

The retired and former-responder cohort is the population most coaches in this space underread. They are out of the truck, off the radio, and often out of any peer-support program that still tracks them. The base-rate numbers in this meta-analysis are the floor you should be working from.

Systematic review and meta-analysis pooling prevalence estimates of PTSD in former first responders across fire, EMS, and law enforcement. Reports a substantially elevated rate compared to general-population baselines, with notable heterogeneity by role, retirement context, and access to mental health care. The authors argue separation from service is itself a transition that compounds existing trauma load rather than resolving it.

Try this: When intake includes retired or separated first responders, treat the transition itself as load-bearing material, not background context.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 05 · First Responder MH · 2026-05-15

Occupational Outcomes of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Residential Treatment in Active Duty Service Members: Predictors of Military Separation

Residential PTSD treatment programs are increasingly the model people point to for fire and EMS. This paper looks at what actually happens to careers after intensive treatment in the active duty space, which is the closest analog the literature has to a career firefighter coming off a PTSD leave.

Examines occupational outcomes following residential PTSD treatment for active duty service members and identifies predictors of subsequent separation from military service. Finds that clinical improvement does not reliably forecast return-to-duty, and that pre-treatment occupational engagement and post-treatment social support carry more predictive weight than symptom reduction alone. Frames the gap between symptom-targeted treatment and career durability.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Setting the Standard: Oklahoma City Diverts Over 6,000 Mental Health 911 Calls in First Year of Program

This is the closest peer model in the country to the Phoenix Fire Behavioral Crisis and Mental Health Specialist role. The first-year diversion numbers are real and the operational lessons travel. Worth tracking how Oklahoma City structures handoff, follow-up, and disposition data.

Oklahoma City's mental health 911 diversion program reports more than 6,000 calls routed away from traditional fire and police response in its first year. The model embeds behavioral health specialists with the response system rather than building a parallel hotline. Coverage notes the program is being studied as a template by other mid-size cities, and the operational metrics around scene time, follow-up, and downstream emergency department avoidance are starting to publish.

Try this: Pull the program's published metrics and compare against Phoenix BCMHS internal numbers; the operational lessons on handoff and follow-up are directly portable.

Psilocybin for Chronic Suicidal Ideation: Scott T. Aaronson on Safety, an Efficacy Signal, and the Role of Expectations

Sheppard Pratt psychiatrist Scott T. Aaronson discusses preliminary results from a psilocybin trial that intentionally enrolled patients with chronic suicidal ideation, a population usually excluded from psychedelic research. He addresses safety signal, an efficacy signal worth taking seriously, and the role of expectancy effects in early-phase data. Frames the methodological tension between recruiting the patients who most need an intervention and protecting the integrity of a trial.

Psychedelic studies have largely excluded patients deemed higher risk. This trial deliberately did not.

Most psychedelic trials route around suicidality by exclusion criteria. This one walked toward it. The interview matters because the population it addresses is the one most likely to show up in first responder integration work. Scope-of-practice edge is sharp here: high-acuity suicidality is clinical territory, and the question of who holds it during and after dosing is unresolved.

Source: Psychedelic Alpha

A New Paper Says the Brain Keeps the Score. Here's What That Actually Means

Body-keeps-the-score language has become the lingua franca of trauma-informed work, which means it is now being used with less precision than the underlying research supports. This piece reviews new evidence on what brain-and-body trauma storage actually looks like in measurement terms. Useful for sharpening how you describe the polyvagal frame to clients without overclaiming.

Reviews a recent paper offering measurable brain-based correlates for traumatic memory storage and retrieval patterns, and walks through what the data does and does not support about somatic trauma claims. Distinguishes between research findings on autonomic regulation and the cultural shorthand the phrase 'the body keeps the score' has come to carry. Argues that the science is more interesting and more specific than the slogan version most clinicians and coaches use.

When a Passion Project Becomes a Calling

A profile of a trauma-informed leadership coach building a practice through a transition forced by federal layoffs. The contemplative thread is the distinction between something one chooses and something one is given to carry. The reader who has stayed near the work long enough will recognize the moment.

Former USAID employee Kyle Dietrich tells the story of how his coaching work evolved from a side practice into central vocation, accelerated by widespread federal layoffs that displaced him from his prior role. Frames the shift not as career pivot but as the recognition of a calling that had been forming underneath the day job. The piece reads as testimony rather than how-to and is the better for it.
