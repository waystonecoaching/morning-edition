Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

This is the kind of foundational data the field has needed. A systematic review and meta-analysis of PTSD prevalence among former first responders gives practitioners and departments a defensible empirical baseline. The 'former' framing matters: it captures what happens after the career ends, which is where a lot of the damage fully surfaces. Most prevalence data focuses on active-duty personnel and misses the cumulative load that often doesn't emerge until the structure of the job is gone.

Researchers pooled data across multiple studies examining PTSD rates in retired and former first responders. The review addresses a persistent gap in the literature: most prevalence figures focus on active-duty populations, leaving the post-career window poorly characterized. The meta-analytic approach allows pooled estimates with more confidence than any single study and surfaces the longer arc of occupational trauma exposure.

Try this: Use pooled prevalence figures from peer-reviewed meta-analyses when framing your client population to new clients, their families, or employer-referral partners. The numbers carry weight in those conversations in ways that anecdotal framing does not.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Minneapolis Firefighters Are Taking PTSD Leave in Increasing Numbers, Driving up Overtime Costs

The overtime-cost framing is how fire administrations often first notice a mental health problem, and that framing has direct implications for what support actually gets funded. About 30 Minneapolis firefighters on simultaneous PTSD leave is not an outlier; it is consistent with what Phoenix and other urban departments are seeing. The story is about infrastructure cost making the invisible visible, which is sometimes the only language that moves departments to act.

Roughly 30 Minneapolis firefighters are on PTSD-related leave simultaneously, creating significant overtime costs as the department scrambles to cover shifts. The piece frames this primarily as a budget problem, but the underlying driver is accumulated cumulative exposure and the cultural barriers that delay firefighters from seeking help until the injury is severe enough to require leave. The cost framing is a lever, not an explanation.

The executive order directing federal agencies to accelerate psychedelic therapy research and access is a significant policy shift. Read this alongside the ongoing MDMA credibility stress in the FDA process: federal acceleration can mean looser standards as much as faster timelines. For practitioners in training programs like BTTY, the question is whether expedited pathways strengthen or weaken the clinical and ethical floors that hold this work.

Healthcare Brew breaks down the practical implications of Trump's executive order on psychedelic-assisted therapy, which directs federal health agencies to prioritize research and explore regulatory pathways. Key uncertainties remain around what expedited approval actually means for training standards, practitioner scope, and insurance coverage. The order signals political momentum but does not resolve the scientific and regulatory questions that remain open.

Try this: Track whether BTTY and other integration training programs issue updated practitioner guidance in response to federal policy movement. The regulatory floor is in motion.

Short-Term and Late-Term Effects of Psilocybin on Symptoms in Major Depression: A Randomized Clinical Trial

JAMA-level publication for psilocybin in major depressive disorder is significant. The late-term effects component is what matters most for integration work: how durable are outcomes, and what predicts sustained benefit versus relapse? Critical reading note applies here: the field is publishing faster than replication can follow. Treat this as important signal, not settled clinical evidence.

This randomized clinical trial examined both immediate and longer-term outcomes for psilocybin treatment in major depressive disorder, tracking symptom changes at multiple follow-up points to assess durability of effect. Published in JAMA, it moves the conversation from pilot data toward more rigorous evidence. Sample sizes and follow-up durations remain limiting factors across the psilocybin literature; this study is a step forward, not a conclusion.

When your workplace doesn't match your ethical outlook: the problem of 'moral injury'

This piece sits in the structural-critique lineage of moral injury work, naming the organizational context rather than locating the wound entirely inside the individual. For first responders, that distinction is load-bearing: the injury often comes as much from what the institution demanded or failed to prevent as from what the call required. Naming that gap does not excuse the internal work of healing, but it changes where the work has to go. This framing is in active tension with the operational/applied frame; treat that tension as content, not a problem to resolve.

The piece frames moral injury through the mismatch between a worker's values and what their institution asks or permits, drawing on the broader MI literature to argue that the wound is relational and structural, not purely intrapsychic. Examples are drawn from outside the first responder world, which limits direct operational application but opens the concept for use with clients who don't identify with traditional responder-framed MI language.

Source: The Conversation

'You just stuffed it down': First responders, dispatchers confront mental health crisis

First responders and emergency dispatchers describe the cultural norm of suppressing emotional response to calls, with years of accumulation and no structured outlet. The piece profiles peer support programs working to interrupt that cycle and names the particular burden on dispatchers, who share the exposure without sharing the crew culture that provides any informal processing. The stuffing pattern is the entry wound; what follows it is the reason people find themselves in a coaching office.

You just stuffed it down.

Dispatchers get mentioned alongside field responders here, which matters. The dispatch chair absorbs enormous secondary exposure with almost none of the peer infrastructure that exists for crews. The cultural pattern the piece names, stuff it, move, stuff it more, is the same autonomic shutdown Mark works with at Phoenix Fire. This is dorsal vagal collapse hiding in plain sight as professionalism.

Owning Your Story: The Soul of a Coaching Business

Vocation is not merely a business concept. The desert tradition asks the same question this piece is circling: what is the story at the center, and is the life built around it honest? For a solo practice working with high-stakes populations, the answer to that question is load-bearing, not decorative. A practice built on a borrowed story does not hold when the work gets heavy.

The piece walks coaches through the identity layer beneath a solo practice: visibility, money, worthiness, and the narrative carried into the work. It argues that business infrastructure follows the internal story rather than the reverse, and that the clarity or confusion at the center shapes everything outward. Framed for coaches broadly, the questions are particularly pointed for practitioners whose work touches crisis, trauma, and transformation.
