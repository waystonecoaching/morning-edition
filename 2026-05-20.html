No. 01 · First Responder MH · 2026-05-06

Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta‐analysis

This is the field doing its arithmetic on the population you serve. A meta-analysis of former first responders puts a number to what the firehouse already carries in the body, and the word "former" is the point: it counts the cost that follows people out the door, not just active-duty rates. Useful when you need a citation in front of a skeptical chief, or a client who is sure he is the only one.

A systematic review and meta-analysis pooling studies on PTSD prevalence among former (retired or separated) first responders across police, fire, and EMS samples. It estimates how common posttraumatic stress remains after the career ends and maps the methodological spread that makes any single-study figure unreliable. Published as EarlyView, so read the method before quoting a headline number.

Try this: When you cite responder PTSD rates on the Waystone site or at intake, anchor to a meta-analysis like this rather than one department's number.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

State of the Science: MDMA‐assisted psychotherapy for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

A peer-reviewed state-of-the-science review of MDMA-assisted psychotherapy, published in a trauma journal rather than advocacy press. This is the document to read before you say anything about MDMA and responder PTSD, because it weighs the evidence the way a journal does, including the wreckage after the FDA's 2024 rejection of the Lykos application. It sits on your scope line: integration support is coaching, the dosing session is not.

A review surveying the current evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in PTSD, covering the efficacy signals from the phase trials and the criticisms that dogged them: functional unblinding, expectancy, and therapist conduct. It frames the modality as promising but unsettled, with the standard-of-care question still open after regulatory setbacks. For first responder PTSD specifically, the honest read is caution; this is not yet a referral path you can lean on.

Try this: Keep a current one-line position on MDMA-AT ready for clients who ask, sourced to a review like this rather than to headlines.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Ozarks, national groups working to prevent law enforcement suicide

Field-level reporting on local and national efforts to prevent police suicide. The signal is infrastructure, who is building what, not research. This is the acute end of your population, and it marks a scope edge plainly: suicidality is clinical ground. Your work sits upstream of it, in regulation and felt safety, not in crisis intervention.

Coverage of regional (Ozarks) and national organizations coordinating to reduce suicide among law enforcement officers, describing peer teams, training, and referral networks. It names the cultural barriers, stigma and fear of fitness-for-duty review, that keep officers from reaching for help. The piece is journalism, not data, but it maps the actors and the gaps in a way that shows where a coach fits and where one must hand off.

Try this: Know your hand-off path before you need it: which crisis line, which clinician, which peer team you would route an officer to.

Psilocybin for Chronic Suicidal Ideation: Scott T. Aaronson on Safety, an Efficacy Signal, and the Role of Expectations

Most psychedelic trials screen out the people at highest risk; Aaronson's group does the opposite, studying psilocybin in patients with chronic suicidal ideation. Read it as a credibility test for the field: does the safety story hold once you stop excluding the hard cases. The acuity here is well past any coaching container, so the value to you is comprehension, not application.

An interview with psychiatrist Scott Aaronson, whose research deliberately includes higher-risk patients usually barred from psychedelic studies. He discusses early safety findings, an efficacy signal for psilocybin in chronic suicidal ideation, and the uncomfortable weight of expectation on outcomes. His candor about expectancy effects is exactly the kind of honesty the field needs more of.

Source: Psychedelic Alpha

Short-Term and Late-Term Effects of Psilocybin on Symptoms in Major Depression A Randomized Clinical Trial - JAMA

A randomized clinical trial in JAMA reporting short- and late-term effects of psilocybin on major depression. JAMA is about as load-bearing as the citation gets, so it is worth knowing even though depression is not your lane. The late-term follow-up is the interesting part; durability is where many psychedelic claims thin out.

A randomized controlled trial measuring psilocybin's effect on depressive symptoms at both short-term and later follow-up points, published in JAMA. The inclusion of late-term outcomes speaks to the durability question that earlier, shorter studies left open. As always the real read is in blinding integrity and effect size, not the journal's masthead.

Source: JAMA Psychedelic

Human Coaching Boosts Adherence in AI Wearable Obesity Study

A study found that human coaching improved adherence in an AI-driven wearable weight program; the human beat the algorithm alone. The headline matters less than the mechanism: presence and relationship move behavior in ways an app does not. That is the quiet case for what you do, made in a journal rather than a brochure.

Research on an obesity intervention using AI wearables found that adding human coaching meaningfully improved participant adherence over the technology by itself. It lands in a moment when coaching is being pitched as automatable. The finding suggests the relational element, accountability, attunement, contact, is not a soft add-on but a driver of outcome.

Try this: When a prospect weighs human coaching against an app, this is a citable data point for relationship as the active ingredient.

No. 07 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-05-08

When your workplace doesn’t match your ethical outlook – the problem of ‘moral injury’

An explainer on moral injury as the distress that arises when one is required to act against one's own ethical sense, or watches leadership do so. It locates the cause in structure and institution rather than in the sufferer, and distinguishes moral injury from PTSD. The argument is that no quantity of individual resilience training resolves a wound whose source is organizational.

No amount of individual resilience training resolves a wound whose source is organizational betrayal.

This sits in the structural-critique lineage of moral injury, the Litz and Shay frame, where the wound comes from systems and leadership failure rather than personal weakness. The Conversation extends it to ordinary workplaces, which is useful precisely because it refuses to locate the injury inside the individual. For a fire-service population, betrayal-by-institution will ring truer than any resilience curriculum.

A Psychology Today piece weighing a new paper against the popular claim that the body keeps the score, asking what the science actually supports. For you this is housekeeping on your own frame. Polyvagal and somatic-trauma language carries real weight with clients and also real overreach in the culture; knowing where the evidence is firm and where it is metaphor keeps you honest when you describe the nervous system to a responder.

The article examines recent research bearing on the widely repeated idea that trauma is stored in the body, separating what the new paper supports from what has hardened into slogan. It neither dismisses somatic approaches nor endorses the popular phrasing wholesale, pointing instead to the narrower, defensible version of the claim. The takeaway is a plea for precision in how nervous-system language gets used.
