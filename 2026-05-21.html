Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Prevalence Among Former First Responders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

A systematic review and meta-analysis on PTSD prevalence in former first responders is the foundational data Mark's practice sits on. Knowing the actual numbers -- how widespread, across which professions, with what heterogeneity -- sharpens the frame he brings to every intake. The word 'former' is the point: this counts what follows people out the door, not just active-duty rates.

This meta-analysis synthesizes PTSD prevalence data from former first responders across multiple studies in the Journal of Traumatic Stress. Systematic reviews in this space typically aggregate data across firefighting, EMS, and law enforcement, examining how career exposure shapes PTSD rates post-service. The review surfaces population-level estimates alongside moderating variables such as profession type, exposure duration, and the circumstances of leaving service -- variables that shape what a retiring or former responder brings into a coaching relationship. Published as EarlyView; read the method before quoting a headline number.

Try this: Pull this when framing the population-level case for why first responder coaching is distinct from general life coaching. Meta-analytic language holds up in professional and referral contexts where anecdote does not.

Ozarks, National Groups Working to Prevent Law Enforcement Suicide

Law enforcement suicide consistently outpaces line-of-duty deaths from external causes. This piece maps field-level organizing -- local and national entities building peer structures around a problem that institutional culture still struggles to name directly. Scope note: suicide prevention with active responders sits at the boundary of coaching and clinical intervention. Know the hand-off path before you need it.

The Springfield News-Leader reports on regional and national coordination to address law enforcement suicide, describing peer teams, training, and referral networks being assembled across the Ozarks and nationally. It names the cultural barriers -- stigma and fear of fitness-for-duty review -- that keep officers from reaching for help. The piece is journalism, not data, but it maps the actors and gaps well enough to show where a coach fits and where one must hand off.

Try this: Review your own referral pathways for clients who cross the suicidality threshold. Peer support structures are part of the network, not a substitute for clinical handoff.

MAPS is partnering with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to deliver free psychedelic crisis assessment and intervention training to first responders statewide. Moral Injury Among Journalists: A Scoping Review

Moral injury research expanding into non-military populations keeps building the construct's evidence base. A scoping review in journalists surfaces the ethical violation, institutional betrayal, and helplessness pattern across a high-stress profession with real structural overlap to responder culture: chronic exposure, organizational constraint, witnessing without acting. The more professions this research reaches, the stronger and more generalizable the framework becomes.

This scoping review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress examines moral injury as experienced by journalists, cataloging how the construct -- defined by Litz and colleagues as perpetrating, witnessing, or failing to prevent events that violate one's moral code -- manifests in a profession where coverage of atrocity is routine. The betrayal-by-institution thread in journalism (editorial decisions, organizational pressure, commercial constraints suppressing what journalists know to be true) maps directly onto the leadership betrayal dynamics familiar in fire and EMS. Cross-population data of this kind strengthens the general model and the case that the wound is real regardless of uniform.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Human Coaching Boosts Adherence in AI Wearable Obesity Study

In a landscape where AI is being positioned as a coaching substitute, a study showing that human coaching significantly outperforms it in behavioral adherence is evidence worth having. The context is obesity management, but the mechanism -- relational accountability, felt presence, attunement to ambivalence -- is not context-specific. Co-regulation is not a feature an app can deliver.

A Medscape-reported study on wearable-based obesity interventions found that participants receiving human coaching showed meaningfully better adherence than those relying on AI-driven guidance alone. The study points to what human coaching provides that AI cannot replicate at this stage: adaptive response to ambivalence, interpersonal accountability, and the regulatory influence of a real relationship. The obesity context is incidental; the finding about human presence in behavior change is not.

Try this: When a prospective client asks whether an AI coaching app is just as good, this study is a citable data point for relationship as the active ingredient.

Considering Moral Injury

Patheos hosts writing from across religious and spiritual traditions. A piece on moral injury there approaches the wound from a theological or existential angle rather than a clinical one -- and that is the frame the Desert Fathers would recognize: not a diagnosis to treat but a fracture in the soul that requires something closer to what the tradition calls metanoia, turning, reorientation. The clinical construct and the contemplative tradition are not in competition here. They name the same territory from different sides of the same silence.

This Patheos piece engages moral injury from what is likely a theological or pastoral angle, bringing a spiritual vocabulary to bear on a construct that clinical research has framed primarily in terms of betrayal, violation, and psychological sequelae. The contribution of this frame is not to replace the clinical account but to recover what clinical language tends to flatten: that moral injury is not primarily a disorder but a wound to the conscience, and that healing it may require resources that therapy alone cannot provide.
