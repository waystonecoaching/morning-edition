Neuroplastogen Treatment for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

JAMA publishing on neuroplastogens signals that the field is seriously examining plasticity-promoting compounds that do not require a full psychedelic experience. This matters for the first responder therapy pipeline: if TrkB agonism drives the therapeutic effect rather than the subjective state, the integration model shifts considerably. Worth tracking as the neuroplastogen framing may be where regulatory and clinical attention migrates post-MDMA.

JAMA reviews the emerging class of neuroplastogen compounds as potential PTSD treatments, focusing on agents that promote synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis without the full hallucinogenic profile of classical psychedelics. The review examines preclinical and early clinical data, including BDNF-TrkB pathway agonists. The framing separates the plasticity mechanism from the experiential component, raising the question of how much the subjective journey contributes to therapeutic outcomes versus how much the molecular effect alone can do.

Try this: File this for the framework conversation with any partner clinic contact asking about non-psychedelic pathways in the PTSD space.
Source: JAMA

State of the Science: MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for the Treatment of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

JTS publishing a state-of-the-science review post-FDA rejection tells you the field is trying to consolidate what it actually knows. Read critically: the blinding problems, the expectancy effects, and the MAPS-adjacent authorship concerns have not been resolved. This is the kind of piece that gets cited as authoritative while the underlying methodology questions remain open.

A peer-reviewed review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress synthesizes the current evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy in PTSD treatment, covering Phase 2 and Phase 3 trial data, proposed mechanisms, and the regulatory landscape post-FDA rejection. The authors address ongoing debates about therapeutic alliance confounds, blinding limitations, and the difficulty of separating the pharmacological effect from the intensive therapy container. The paper attempts to establish what the evidence actually supports versus what advocacy has claimed.

Try this: If you have a partner clinic conversation coming, know the blinding and expectancy critique before walking in. The field is credibility-stressed and the counterarguments matter.

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem with Polyvagal Theory

Mad in America is not a disinterested source, but this critique of polyvagal theory has been building in the neuroscience literature for several years. The core challenge is that the vagus nerve anatomy does not map cleanly onto the three-tier hierarchy Porges describes, and the predictive validity of the model has not held up the way popularizers have claimed. You use polyvagal language with clients and on the Waystone site. Know what the empirical floor is.

The piece argues that polyvagal theory overstates the role of the vagus nerve in regulating social engagement and emotional states, citing neuroanatomical research that challenges the evolutionary sequence Porges posits. The author draws on critiques from neuroscientists who note that the ventral vagal pathway does not function as a distinct regulatory system the way the theory describes. The argument is not that the autonomic nervous system is irrelevant to emotional regulation, but that polyvagal theory packages that insight in a framework that does not hold up to scrutiny.

Try this: Read Porges's published responses to the anatomical critiques before any session where you use polyvagal language explicitly. A framework you can defend is stronger than one you have not examined.
Source: Mad in America

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Prevalence Among Former First Responders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

A meta-analysis on former first responders is more useful than prevalence studies on active-duty personnel because it captures what accumulates after the career ends. That is the population you are most likely to see: people who have left the job or transitioned roles and are only now processing the load. The data here gives you current numbers to reference when making the case for early intervention and sustained support infrastructure.

This JTS meta-analysis aggregates prevalence data across studies of former first responders, including firefighters, law enforcement, and EMS, after career separation. It finds substantially elevated PTSD rates compared to the general population, with variation by profession, years of service, and type of exposures. The authors note that separation from the department often removes the peer support structures that partially buffer against symptom onset, making the post-career window a high-risk period.

Try this: Pull the prevalence numbers from the abstract. They belong in a Waystone intake conversation or a program proposal.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Psilocybin for Chronic Suicidal Ideation: Scott T. Aaronson on Safety, an Efficacy Signal, and the Role of Expectations

Aaronson deliberately works with high-risk patients that most psilocybin trials exclude. His group's findings on chronic suicidal ideation are clinically significant and directly relevant to the first responder population you serve. Scope note: this is clinical research, not a coaching pathway. Know it as context for when your clients are weighing treatment options, not as a protocol you would implement.

Psychiatrist Scott Aaronson at Sheppard Pratt discusses his research on psilocybin for patients with chronic suicidal ideation, a population typically excluded from psychedelic trials. His group found a safety profile and an efficacy signal that warrant further study. He discusses the role of patient expectations in outcomes, the challenge of obtaining accurate safety data in a population with high baseline risk, and what a responsible trial design looks like when standard exclusion criteria would leave high-need patients without research coverage.

Try this: Keep this for conversations with clients who mention suicidal ideation and ask about psychedelic-assisted treatment. It is the most current data on high-risk inclusion.

Researchers Ask Probing Questions About Methylone Study, and the Complications of Psychedelics Access Under the Right To Try Act

The methylone study questions are part of a broader pattern: when access outpaces methodology, the credibility of the entire field takes the hit. Right to Try Act access for psychedelics creates a data collection window that can produce noise rather than signal if the research design does not hold. Worth tracking as an indicator of where the field is cutting corners under political and commercial pressure.

Researchers have raised methodological concerns about a methylone study, questioning its controls, patient selection, and outcome reporting. The piece also examines the complications arising from psychedelic access through the Right to Try Act, which allows terminal patients to access unapproved drugs but creates an uncontrolled environment that generates anecdotal claims difficult to integrate into the evidence base. The tension between access advocacy and research integrity is the recurring theme.

Wellness center infrastructure for first responders represents the institutional layer that peer support programs depend on. When Your Workplace Doesn't Match Your Ethical Outlook: The Problem of 'Moral Injury'

The Conversation's treatment of moral injury in organizational contexts is useful because it applies the framework outside the combat and responder lane, clarifying what is specific to first responder moral injury and what is structural. The Litz and Shay lineage names the betrayal element clearly: moral injury is not just exposure to atrocity, it is the experience of being required to act against your own moral code or witnessing institutional failure to protect what should have been protected. That distinction matters for how you frame it in coaching.

The piece examines moral injury in workplace settings, drawing on the research tradition that defines moral injury as the wound that results from perpetrating, failing to prevent, or bearing witness to acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs. In organizational contexts, moral injury often arises from institutional betrayal: being directed by leadership to act in ways that conflict with professional or personal ethics, or watching the organization fail to hold itself accountable. The author distinguishes this from burnout, noting that the intervention required is fundamentally different.

Try this: The betrayal-versus-atrocity distinction is worth having sharp before any session where moral injury is the presenting theme.
Source: The Conversation

Can Rehab Work If She's Not Ready? A Mom Asks.

The readiness question in substance use is technically a clinical conversation, but the underlying issue, what motivates change before external pressure is sufficient to force it, is core motivational interviewing and BTtY territory. Scope note: this piece is framed around rehab, which is clinical. Where it crosses into your work is the family-system dynamics and the pre-contemplation stage, where the coach's role is holding space for ambivalence rather than driving toward readiness.

An advice column piece explores whether a mother's adult child can benefit from rehabilitation treatment when the child is not internally motivated to change. The response draws on the motivational interviewing literature to explain the difference between externally-imposed treatment entry and self-determined change, while acknowledging that waiting for readiness as a prerequisite is sometimes a rationalization for inaction. The tension between honoring autonomy and acting on a duty of care is the through-line.

Try this: The pre-contemplation framing is worth having ready for family members of first responders who come to you asking how to help someone who is not asking for help themselves.
Source: Hartford Courant

Considering Moral Injury

Patheos sits at the intersection of religion and public discourse. A piece engaging moral injury from that vantage is worth the read for how the theological and pastoral traditions are receiving this concept. The hesychast tradition does not have a category called moral injury, but it has something more precise: the rupture between the person's deepest orientation toward God and the actions they were forced to carry or witness. The fathers named this as a wound in the nous. Worth sitting with.

A Patheos essay engages moral injury as a theological and pastoral concept, examining how religious communities might understand and respond to the wound of conscience experienced by military, first responders, and others who act against their deepest moral commitments under institutional or situational pressure. Draws on both contemporary moral injury research and older spiritual direction traditions.
