Neuroplastogen Treatment for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

JAMA covering neuroplastogen treatment for PTSD is a signal worth registering. This is the high-tier literature catching up to the neuroplasticity hypothesis: that certain compounds work not by sedating symptoms but by reopening developmental windows for learning and fear extinction. Relevant for any coach working adjacent to clients in ketamine or psilocybin programs who ask why this worked when SSRIs did not.

The piece examines neuroplastogens as a treatment class for PTSD, distinct from both conventional pharmacology and the serotonergic psychedelic framing. The argument is that compounds promoting neuroplasticity, including BDNF-pathway agonists and certain psychedelic derivatives, may act by restoring synaptic plasticity that trauma has foreclosed. JAMA coverage at this level signals the neuroplasticity hypothesis is entering mainstream psychiatric literature, ahead of the wider clinical adoption curve.

Try this: Neuroplasticity is now a defensible, literature-backed framing when clients in your integration work ask why ketamine or psilocybin moved what years of SSRIs did not.
Source: JAMA

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

The JTS is where the MDMA/PTSD case is actually being argued, not in advocacy press. A piece titled 'From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline' is doing exactly what the field needs: stress-testing the Phase 3 data with the rigor applied to any other treatment. Read it in both directions, not dismissive, not credulous. The credibility stress in this space is real, and this is the right venue to track it. Scope note: clients following the FDA pathway may bring this to a session. Knowing the evidentiary landscape is coaching literacy, not clinical work.

The paper conducts a critical reassessment of the evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, examining methodological questions around blinding, expectation effects, trial conduct concerns from the Phase 3 process, and what the existing data can and cannot support. It calls for the field to move from advocate framing toward evidentiary standards and identifies where the literature is genuinely strong versus where it is overstated. The piece represents a maturing of the academic conversation beyond the earlier promotional phase.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

A systematic review and meta-analysis of PTSD prevalence among former first responders is the kind of epidemiological anchor you want when making the case for why this population needs focused support. The post-service window matters here: not line-of-duty incidence, but the cumulative picture of what happens after the badge comes off. That period is where a substantial portion of coaching clients land.

The meta-analysis aggregates prevalence data across studies of former first responders past the point of active service, quantifying the PTSD burden across police, fire, and EMS populations relative to general population baselines. Key findings address how prevalence varies across occupational groups and what the timing of onset looks like in relation to career end. The post-career period carries elevated risk, likely tied to the loss of structure, identity, and the peer regulation that shift culture provides.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

MAPS & State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

MAPS training first responders on psychedelic crisis response is the operational intersection of two worlds Mark works in. This is not clinical advocacy; it is a concrete training program for the people who show up at 2 AM when a session goes sideways. Knowing this program exists matters for Phoenix Fire network conversations and for any partner clinic conversations about what trained community response looks like.

MAPS and the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division are partnering to deliver Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training free to first responders across Colorado. The curriculum covers evidence-based education, de-escalation protocols for individuals in psychedelic distress, and crisis intervention practices. Training is delivered via video and assessment format, making it accessible across departments without requiring in-person coordination.

Try this: Worth tracking for Arizona. Colorado building this infrastructure state-by-state means Arizona may be in the queue. Potentially relevant to partner clinic conversations about community response capacity around sessions.

Peer Support: The Front Line of Mental Resilience

DVIDS framing peer support as the front line of mental resilience is notable less for content than for what it signals: peer support is gaining official institutional traction, not as a wellness add-on but as a primary resilience mechanism. That institutional legitimacy changes how coaches position their work relative to peer support programs. It also reinforces the polyvagal argument: co-regulation within the culture is more accessible than clinical intervention outside it.

The DVIDS report covers the framing of peer support as a primary mental health resilience strategy in military and emergency service contexts, highlighting its role as a first-response layer before clinical intervention is needed. It addresses training models, accessibility, and how peer support infrastructure reduces stigma by keeping the initial conversation within the culture rather than routing to outside clinical systems. The piece reflects growing command-level recognition of peer support as a structural rather than supplementary resource.

Source: DVIDS

PERSPECTIVE: How Understanding Moral Injury Could Save Veterans' Lives

Homeland Security Today carrying a moral injury perspective piece signals the concept is moving from academic niche into operational policy conversation. The argument that understanding moral injury could save lives is the operational frame, not the structural critique, and it matters because it gives commanders and program directors language they can bring inside the institution. Read it alongside The Conversation piece and note the difference: one speaks to leaders, one speaks to systems.

The perspective argues that moral injury, defined as the damage done by perpetrating, witnessing, or failing to prevent acts that violate one's moral code, is underrecognized in veterans' mental health care and is contributing to preventable deaths. The piece calls for training clinicians and leaders to distinguish moral injury from PTSD and to treat them differently, including through meaning-making approaches rather than trauma-protocol-only responses. The operational framing positions leaders as capable of intervening before clinical thresholds are crossed.

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem with Polyvagal Theory

The piece argues that polyvagal theory's central claim, that the vagus nerve hierarchically regulates autonomic states in a three-circuit system, overstates what the neuroscience actually supports. Neuroscientists have questioned the three-circuit hierarchy and specific claims about dorsal vagal shutdown as a distinct state. The critique is not that autonomic regulation is irrelevant to trauma and behavior; it is that the specific mechanism polyvagal theory proposes is not well-supported by comparative anatomy or electrophysiology. The clinical application may remain useful even where the underlying theory requires revision.

The vagus nerve is not in control

Mad In America carries the critical psychiatry frame, and this polyvagal critique has real neuroscience literature behind it. The specific claim, that the vagus nerve is not the hierarchical regulatory hub the polyvagal narrative presents it as, is worth taking seriously. Mark uses polyvagal language with clients and in Waystone's framing. Know the strongest version of this critique before you need to answer it. The autonomic regulation framework survives; the vagus-nerve-as-conductor story does not travel the same way after you read this.

Source: Mad In America

When your workplace doesn't match your ethical outlook: the problem of 'moral injury'

The Conversation takes the structural-critique approach to moral injury, situating it in the workplace ethics literature rather than the combat-trauma literature. That framing matters for first responder coaching: the injury often comes not from what a responder did in the field but from what the organization did afterward, the cover-up, the blame transfer, the failure of leadership. This is the betrayal-injury track, and it travels under-identified in the culture.

The piece examines moral injury through the lens of workplace ethics misalignment: when an organization requires people to act in ways that conflict with their personal values, the resulting injury is structural, not purely psychological. Drawing on research from healthcare, military, and emergency services contexts, it argues that treatment approaches focused on the individual miss the systemic drivers, and that organizational accountability is part of the repair. The structural framing is the Litz-and-Shay lineage reaching the general reader.

Source: The Conversation

Owning Your Story: The Soul of a Coaching Business

ICF publishing a piece centered on identity, visibility, money, and worthiness in the coaching business context is unusual and worth the time. For a solo practitioner serving high-stigma populations, how you show up publicly and what story you tell about the work is structural, not decorative. The question of what Waystone says about itself before it says anything about services is live business territory.

The piece argues that building a coaching business requires starting with identity rather than infrastructure: the story you tell about who you are and why you do this work precedes every practical decision about pricing, marketing, or positioning. It examines the ways practitioners avoid visibility, undercharge, and carry worthiness wounds into their business, and offers reflective questions for reconnecting with the original impulse behind the practice. The tone is less management-speak than you would expect from the ICF blog.

Try this: One question worth sitting with: what is the story you tell about Waystone to someone who has never heard of coaching? Does it come from the original impulse, or from what you think they want to hear?

Considering Moral Injury

Patheos situates moral injury in a religious and spiritual context, which makes it the right close for an edition with this much moral injury content. The clinical construct and the workplace ethics frame are both useful and both partial. At its root, moral injury is a question about integrity, which in the hesychast tradition means the state of being undivided. The Desert Fathers had language for this long before Litz or Shay coined the term.

The Patheos essay takes moral injury out of the clinical and operational frames and situates it in a broader spiritual and philosophical context, examining it as a wound to the soul's sense of order rather than strictly a psychological event. It draws on religious and contemplative thought to suggest that repair may require something closer to confession, witness, and reintegration than symptom management or meaning-making in the secular therapeutic sense. The piece speaks to the repair of the moral self, not only the regulation of the traumatized nervous system.

Source: Patheos
