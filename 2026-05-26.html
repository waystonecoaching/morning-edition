From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

JTS published a formal reassessment of the MDMA-PTSD trial program. This is the field doing its own accounting after the FDA rejection and data integrity questions. Worth reading as a measure of where the evidence actually stands, not where advocates said it would stand. Read critically: this is peer-reviewed self-correction, not advocacy.

A Journal of Traumatic Stress paper examines the evidentiary claims behind MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, assessing the gap between early therapeutic promise and what controlled trial data actually demonstrated. The authors consider what rigorous review of the MAPS Phase 3 trials reveals about effect sizes, expectancy effects, and the challenges of blinding in psychedelic studies. The piece argues the field needs tighter evidentiary standards before MDMA-assisted therapy can be defended as a proven treatment. It stops short of dismissal but marks a significant pull-back from the advocacy-adjacent framing that dominated the field before 2024.

Try this: When a client or referral source asks about MDMA therapy, lead with this: the trial data is contested, not settled. Know what that means before you recommend a referral pathway.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Systematic review and meta-analysis from JTS on PTSD prevalence in the population Mark works with. The operational question is whether published prevalence rates match what shows up in practice, and what the gap says about how many people are not seeking help.

How understanding moral injury could save veterans' lives

DAV frames moral injury as a life-and-death distinction from PTSD. Scope-of-practice note: the piece likely moves into clinical territory when it addresses intervention. The diagnostic question is coaching-adjacent; the treatment question is not. Moral injury misidentified as PTSD and treated accordingly produces predictable results.

The Disabled American Veterans examines how misidentifying moral injury as PTSD leads to inadequate treatment and increased suicide risk among veterans. The piece covers the difference between fear-conditioned trauma and the wound of violated conscience, why standard PTSD protocols often fail for moral injury, and the growing research on meaning-making approaches. It makes the case that getting the distinction right is a survival issue, not an academic one.

Try this: Review your intake questions for moral injury screening. If you are not asking about betrayal, failed leadership, and acts of commission or omission, you may be missing what is actually in the room.

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem with Polyvagal Theory

Mad In America runs a critical reading of polyvagal theory: the claim that the autonomic nervous system operates in a three-part hierarchical sequence is not well-supported by current neuroanatomy. Worth knowing what the critique looks like before a client or colleague asks. The science underneath Dana's clinical applications is more contested than the coaching world typically acknowledges.

The piece argues that polyvagal theory, as popularized by Porges and applied widely in trauma and coaching contexts, overstates what the neuroscience actually shows. The vagus nerve plays a significant role in autonomic regulation, but the specific three-tier hierarchy does not map cleanly onto how the nervous system actually functions. Critics cite problems with the evolutionary claims and the myelinated/unmyelinated distinction that anchors the model. The piece is not hostile to nervous-system-informed practice; it calls for more precision in how practitioners describe the mechanisms to clients.

Try this: Frame polyvagal language in sessions as a map, not a mechanism. 'This is a useful way to track what your nervous system is doing' holds better under scrutiny than 'here is how your nervous system actually works.'
Source: Mad In America

Investigating the safety and tolerability of single-dose psilocybin for post-traumatic stress disorder: A nonrandomized open-label clinical trial

Actual trial data on psilocybin for PTSD, not mechanisms speculation. Open-label and nonrandomized, so it cannot speak to efficacy, but it is early safety and tolerability work from a peer-reviewed source. Scope-of-practice note: this is clinical research, not a framework for coaching-context preparation or integration.

A Journal of Psychopharmacology trial investigated single-dose psilocybin in a PTSD population, tracking safety endpoints, side effect profiles, and tolerability across the session and follow-up period. Being nonrandomized and open-label, the study cannot support efficacy conclusions, but it documents what a structured psilocybin protocol looks like in practice and what adverse events were observed. The authors note the absence of serious adverse events as a preliminary signal and flag the need for controlled comparison before stronger claims can be made.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

Facing trauma under the influence of psychedelics: A phenomenological study with Nova rave survivors

Unusual dataset: the October 7, 2023 Nova rave massacre happened when most attendees were on MDMA or other psychedelics. What does mass-casualty trauma look like when it lands in an already-opened nervous system? The phenomenological data from survivors offers something no lab protocol can produce. Read critically; it cannot be generalized. The question it asks is the right one.

Researchers conducted a phenomenological study with survivors of the October 7, 2023 Nova rave massacre, most of whom were under the influence of psychedelics when the attack occurred. The study examines how psychedelic states shaped the experience, encoding, and integration of the traumatic event. Some survivors report intensified terror and loss of reality testing; others describe a kind of protective dissolution. The authors do not draw efficacy conclusions; the goal is to understand the phenomenology, not advocate for or against psychedelic use in acute threat contexts.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

Considering Moral Injury

Patheos publishes across religious traditions. A piece on moral injury from that platform is likely to approach the wound from conscience, meaning, and spiritual repair rather than symptom management. That is closer territory for integration work, and closer to how the Desert Fathers understood compunction.

The piece examines moral injury as a wound to conscience and moral self-understanding, distinct from the psychological framing that dominates clinical literature. It draws on the experience of people whose core ethical commitments were violated by what they did, witnessed, or failed to prevent. The contemplative tradition has always held space for this kind of wound: compunction, penthos, the grief of moral clarity rather than moral confusion. The author approaches integration not as symptom reduction but as the restoration of right relationship with one's own conscience.

Source: Patheos
