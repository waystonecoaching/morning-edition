Addressing Psychological Hazards in the Fire Rescue Service

Dr. I. David Daniels names what most wellness programs treat as invisible: bullying, harassment, organizational trauma, and moral injury as structural hazards, not individual failures. That framing matters for coaching work. When a client's nervous system is dysregulated, the question is not always what happened on a call. Sometimes it is what the culture has been doing to them for years.

The piece argues that psychological hazards in fire and rescue are systemic, not episodic. Daniels covers chronic stress, moral injury, workplace harassment, and organizational dysfunction as occupational health risks requiring the same institutional response as physical hazards. The through-line is that departments address behavioral health only after crisis rather than as preventive infrastructure. The article treats organizational trauma as a category on equal footing with incident trauma.

Try this: Worth reviewing whether your intake process distinguishes between acute incident stress and chronic organizational exposure. Clients carrying the second often present like the first.
Source: Firefighter Nation

Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

A systematic review with pooled prevalence data for PTSD in former first responders puts numbers to what has been quoted loosely in the field. For a practice working exclusively with first responders, that number anchors the scope-of-practice conversation and informs how you triage who is appropriate for coaching versus who needs a clinical referral first.

This meta-analysis aggregates PTSD prevalence data across studies of former first responders, a population distinct from active-duty responders in ways that matter: departure from the job, loss of identity and structure, reduced access to peer support. The review addresses methodological heterogeneity across studies and provides pooled estimates with confidence intervals. The cumulative load of a career in fire, EMS, or law enforcement shows up differently post-service than it does during active years.

Try this: Pull the full text for intake framing when working with retired or transitioning first responders. Having a peer-reviewed citation strengthens any case you make to department wellness coordinators about scope.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

Colorado is the first state to formalize psychedelic crisis response training through a state agency. First responders are encountering natural medicine calls without preparation for them, and the gap between legalization and field readiness is real. This is the practical interface between the psychedelic therapy world and fire and EMS culture -- and it is coming to other states whether departments are ready or not.

MAPS and the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division have partnered to deliver free Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide. The curriculum covers evidence-based harm reduction, crisis assessment, and de-escalation practices for responders who encounter individuals in difficult psychedelic states. The delivery model is video and assessment-based for reach across diverse department sizes.

Try this: If you are building any capacity with Phoenix Fire around mental health or partner development, this Colorado model is worth filing. Arizona's legal landscape differs but the training gap is the same.
Source: MAPS

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

The field has been living on the MAPS Phase 3 data and the REMAP study longer than it should. This JTS paper represents the evidentiary-discipline side of the psychedelic research conversation doing its job: subjecting the MDMA pathway to the same scrutiny applied to everything else. Scope-of-practice note: this is clinical literature, not coaching literature. Read it so you can hold the uncertainty clearly when clients ask.

The paper reassesses the evidence base for MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD, examining methodological concerns raised by FDA reviewers and the scientific community. Topics include unblinding problems inherent to active compounds, therapist allegiance effects, adverse event reporting gaps, and the limitations of open-label enthusiasm in early trials. This is the literature correcting for its own optimism, which is what peer review is for.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

VA Launches MDMA Clinical Trial to Measure Effects on PTSD, Mental Health

The VA formally entering the MDMA trial space is significant for veteran clients and for the broader legitimacy question. Post-FDA rejection, this is a different pathway than the private-sector route. Veterans with PTSD in your caseload will be asking about it, and the VA imprimatur changes how the conversation lands inside department culture.

The VA has launched a clinical trial examining MDMA-assisted therapy for veterans with PTSD and related mental health conditions, including alcohol use disorder. The trial follows the FDA's 2024 decision not to approve MDMA-assisted therapy, citing inadequate data. The VA is proceeding under its own research authority and the design is intended to address some of the methodological gaps identified in prior trials.

Investigating the safety and tolerability of single-dose psilocybin for post-traumatic stress disorder: A nonrandomized open-label clinical trial

While MDMA is absorbing regulatory scrutiny, the psilocybin-for-PTSD pathway is being built quietly in the literature. This is an early-phase safety trial, not an efficacy claim. That distinction matters when talking with clients or colleagues about what the science actually supports right now. The field moves faster than the evidence; this is the evidence.

This open-label Phase 1-style trial assessed single-dose psilocybin in a PTSD population, focusing on safety and tolerability rather than efficacy. Psilocybin has substantial evidence in depression and end-of-life anxiety but limited prior data in PTSD specifically. Nonrandomized open-label design means any efficacy signal needs to be read carefully; the primary value here is establishing whether it is safe enough to study further, which it appears to be.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology (SAGE)

How understanding moral injury could save veterans' lives

The piece makes the case for moral injury as a distinct construct from PTSD, with different roots, different presentation, and different pathways toward healing. The article reviews current understanding of moral injury in veterans and advocates for screening approaches that address the moral and spiritual dimensions rather than treating it as a PTSD subtype. Misclassifying moral injury as PTSD leaves the actual wound untouched.

Moral injury is not PTSD. The mechanism is different: it is not about fear but about the fracture of conscience, the wound that opens when what a person did, witnessed, or failed to prevent violates what they hold sacred. The DAV bringing this into its advocacy language signals the concept is mainstream enough to drive policy. The coaching work is to go deeper than the label.

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem with Polyvagal Theory

Mad In America is not a neutral venue and this piece has an agenda, but the scientific critique is worth engaging on its own terms. The core argument is that polyvagal theory overstates what the anatomy and physiology actually support. If you are grounding client work in Deb Dana's framework, you should be able to hold the strongest objection to it alongside the clinical utility.

The piece argues that polyvagal theory as popularized in trauma and therapy contexts makes claims about vagal anatomy and physiology that exceed current evidence. The critique focuses on whether the ventral vagal complex operates as a clean hierarchical social engagement system in the way the standard model implies. The author draws on neuroscience literature to challenge the mechanism without necessarily dismissing the clinical observations the framework is built on. Stephen Porges has responded to earlier versions of this critique; the conversation continues.

Source: Mad In America

Considering Moral Injury

Patheos covers religion and spirituality seriously, which makes it a credible venue for the contemplative dimension of moral injury that clinical frameworks often flatten. The Desert Fathers called it logismoi -- the disordered thought-pattern that distorts conscience and corrodes the inner life. Contemporary moral injury literature calls it something else and misses the tradition that has worked this territory for fifteen centuries.

A Patheos piece examining moral injury through a lens that brings in spiritual, ethical, or religious dimensions absent from most clinical literature. Moral injury at its root is about the conscience and its wounding, which is why it responds poorly to purely cognitive interventions. Traditions that know something about conscience, contrition, and restoration have something to offer that the DSM does not, and the piece appears to take that seriously.

Source: Patheos
