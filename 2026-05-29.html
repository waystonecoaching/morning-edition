Addressing Psychological Hazards in the Fire Rescue Service

Dr. I. David Daniels names the full taxonomy of psychological hazards in the fire service: bullying, harassment, moral injury, chronic stress, organizational trauma. These are not incidental to the culture; they are structural features of it. Mark works with the people who carry that structure inside their bodies. The framing here is occupational safety, not personal pathology.

Daniels catalogs psychological hazards in the fire rescue service that extend well beyond operational trauma, including bullying, harassment, organizational dysfunction, and moral injury rooted in leadership failure. The piece frames these not as individual weaknesses but as systemic hazards requiring systemic response. It parallels the shift in safety culture from blaming the firefighter to auditing the organization. The argument has direct traction for any practitioner helping first responders distinguish what they are responsible for from what was done to them.

Try this: Use Daniels's taxonomy when helping clients distinguish self-inflicted wounds from organizationally inflicted ones. The list is a conversation starter, not a diagnostic.
Source: Firefighter Nation

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

The credibility stress in the MDMA-assisted therapy field is not abstract. This Journal of Traumatic Stress paper moves from inside the field to name it: the gap between what the early trials claimed to prove and what they actually proved under scrutiny. Mark sits at the intersection of BTTY training, first responder PTSD, and clinical-adjacent integration work. How this evidence base stabilizes or does not directly shapes the ground he stands on.

The paper argues that early MDMA-assisted psychotherapy trials operated with strong therapeutic optimism but methodological vulnerabilities: unblinding, expectancy effects, inconsistent outcome measurement, and small sample sizes. The authors call for the field to apply the same evidentiary discipline used in other psychiatric treatment research. They stop short of dismissing MDMA-AP but insist the gap between what is claimed and what is demonstrated needs to close before clinical scaling. This is a peer-reviewed corrective from inside the field, not hostile dismissal from outside it.

Try this: Know this paper before your next conversation with a partner clinic or referral source tracking MDMA research. It is now part of the landscape they are reading.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Baseline numbers for the population Mark works with. A meta-analysis on PTSD prevalence among former first responders pins down what the field often discusses anecdotally. The data matters for intake conversations, partner protocols, and any honest framing of what this practice is responding to.

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes PTSD prevalence data across former first responder populations, establishing a quantitative baseline for how frequently first responders carry diagnosable PTSD into post-service life. The methodology covers multiple responder categories and examines variance by occupation, service duration, and study design. Peer-reviewed and methodologically rigorous, the findings give practitioners a credible figure for the scope of the problem rather than relying on figures derived from active-duty samples that may not reflect long-term burden.

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem with Polyvagal Theory

Mad In America runs structural and critical psychiatry pieces, and this one takes direct aim at polyvagal theory's neuroscientific claims, specifically the ventral vagal circuit as a uniquely mammalian evolutionary development governing social engagement. The critique is not new, but the field keeps producing it. Mark uses polyvagal theory as a load-bearing frame with clients. Knowing the strongest version of the critique is part of carrying that frame with integrity.

The piece challenges the mechanistic claims at the center of polyvagal theory, arguing that Porges's neuroanatomy is selectively read and that the three-tier autonomic hierarchy is not empirically grounded in the way clinical advocates present it. Critics have pointed out for years that the ventral vagal circuit as described does not map cleanly onto the actual neuroanatomy of the vagus nerve. The Mad In America piece surfaces those critiques for a non-specialist audience and calls into question whether the clinical utility of polyvagal framing can be separated from its contested neuroscientific scaffolding.

Try this: Read this alongside Porges directly. The clinical utility of the framework and the accuracy of the neuroanatomy are separable questions. Know where the scaffold holds and where it is argued rather than established.

How understanding moral injury could save veterans' lives

The DAV piece positions moral injury not as a theoretical refinement of PTSD but as a distinct wound with distinct treatment implications. Veterans and first responders who do not respond to standard trauma protocols often fit the moral injury profile more accurately: betrayal by leadership, violation of moral code, perpetration-related shame. Treating the wrong wound is not a minor error.

Disabled American Veterans examines how moral injury, distinct from PTSD in both origin and treatment, contributes to veteran suicide and treatment dropout. The piece draws on current research to argue that betrayal by leadership, violation of personal moral code, and perpetration-related shame require treatment oriented toward meaning-making and ethical repair rather than symptom reduction alone. Providers who approach these presentations purely as PTSD are often missing the actual wound, with significant consequences for engagement and outcome. The piece is advocacy-adjacent but the underlying research it draws on is solid.

Source: DAV : Disabled American Veterans

Mechanisms of psilocybin on the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder

The mechanistic question for psilocybin and PTSD is distinct from the efficacy question, and this paper addresses the former. Understanding how psilocybin is thought to work on PTSD-related neural circuits matters for anyone doing integration coaching because the mechanism shapes what integration is actually doing at the level of the nervous system.

This Journal of Psychopharmacology paper reviews proposed mechanisms through which psilocybin may affect PTSD symptom clusters, including intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal. The authors examine psilocybin's effects on the default mode network, fear memory reconsolidation, and neuroplasticity as candidate mechanisms. The paper is mechanistic rather than a trial report, and is more useful for building a technically grounded understanding of what psychedelic-assisted work is thought to do than for tracking efficacy outcomes. Primary literature, peer-reviewed.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

This Journal of Traumatic Stress scoping review maps the emerging literature on moral injury among journalists covering war, disaster, violence, and institutional corruption. The review identifies the same core wound found in first responder populations: the gap between what a person is called to do and what they are structurally permitted to do, or what they witness without capacity to prevent. The findings reinforce the argument that moral injury is occupation-shaped but not occupation-specific, which has implications for how moral injury frameworks translate across client populations.

The wound opens where duty and permission diverge.

Moral injury in journalism is not the same as moral injury in fire, but the scoping review is worth reading for the structural parallels: institutional betrayal, perpetration-by-proxy, helplessness to act in alignment with values. Cross-occupational comparisons reveal what is universal in the wound and what is occupation-shaped.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Early promise and major evidence gaps in AI psychological training tools

AI tools for psychological training are moving faster than the evidence base supporting them. For a solo coaching practitioner, this matters: tools being marketed for client support, skills training, and session preparation are running ahead of what can be credibly claimed for them. Knowing where the evidence is thin is the practitioner's job.

A research review identifies significant evidence gaps in AI-assisted psychological training tools despite early promising signals. While AI tools show potential for skills practice, psychoeducation delivery, and simulation-based training, the review notes that rigorous outcome data remains thin across nearly all categories. The caution is relevant for practitioners evaluating where to integrate AI support into a coaching practice and where skepticism is warranted before adoption.

Try this: Before adopting any AI-assisted coaching or training tool, ask the vendor for outcome data. 'Early promise' is not the same as evidence of effect.
