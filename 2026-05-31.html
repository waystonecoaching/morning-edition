VA Launches MDMA Clinical Trial to Measure Effects on PTSD, Mental Health

The VA's formal entry into MDMA trial research is a policy marker worth tracking. For a practice built on first responder integration work, the VA setting an institutional baseline for what counts as evidence in this space has downstream effects on client expectations, referral options, and practitioner credibility. The trial design will matter as much as the outcome. Scope note: this is clinical treatment infrastructure, not coaching; the relevance is in understanding what clients may be moving toward.

The Department of Veterans Affairs has launched a clinical trial examining MDMA's effects on PTSD and mental health outcomes in veterans. The trial represents the VA's first formal institutional engagement with MDMA-assisted therapy, arriving after years of advocacy from researchers and patient communities. The design and scope of the trial will shape how the broader mental health and benefits system evaluates MDMA as a treatment option for combat and operational trauma. Indigenous Knowledge Systems and Psychedelic Science: Towards Ethical and Reciprocal Collaboration

The ethical and reciprocal collaboration argument in psychedelic science is directly relevant to any practitioner doing integration work: the knowledge being deployed in current therapeutic models was extracted from living traditions that did not consent to that extraction. This is not abstract. It bears on how the work is represented to clients and on the integrity of the framework itself.

Published in the Journal of Psychopharmacology, this paper argues that indigenous peoples have custodied psychoactive medicines through ceremony, kinship, and spiritual responsibility across generations, and that current psychedelic research has too often treated that knowledge as raw material. The authors propose criteria for ethical and reciprocal collaboration rather than continued extraction. The argument applies to practitioners in integration work who draw on or implicitly sanction these frameworks.

Nevada Peer Support Network Helps First Responders in the Line of Duty

Peer support infrastructure at the state network level is the organizational layer above what individual departments build. Nevada's model is worth understanding for its structure: who funds it, how it connects to dispatch and command, and what the activation threshold looks like. The peer support space is moving from volunteer-driven to institutionalized, and the sustainability model matters.

The Nevada Peer Support Network provides mental health and crisis support resources to first responders across the state, with a focus on making peer support accessible during active duty rather than only after incident. The network connects responders to trained peers and professional support in a way that does not require formal clinical referral. State-level networks like this are part of the evolving infrastructure that individual departments increasingly rely on rather than building in-house.

Human Coaching Boosts Adherence in AI Wearable Obesity Study

The AI-plus-wearable context is incidental. The finding is the point: human coaching contact drives adherence in ways that technology alone does not replicate. This is clean evidence for the value proposition of a solo human coaching practice in an increasingly automated wellness market.

A study on wearable-based obesity intervention found that participants receiving human coaching alongside the device showed substantially better adherence than those using the technology alone. The coaching contact did not need to be intensive; consistent human follow-through was the active ingredient. The finding generalizes beyond the obesity intervention context to any behavior-change coaching practice.

"Do Indians Still Exist?" On Intergenerational Trauma and Indigenous Resilience

The question underneath this essay is the same question your clients carry: what survives, and how. Intergenerational transmission of suffering is not metaphor here; it is the mechanism under examination. The contemplative tradition would recognize the pattern: what is not met becomes what is handed on.

The essay traces how Indigenous identity and culture persist across generations that carry both historical trauma and deliberate efforts at erasure. The author examines the mechanisms of intergenerational trauma alongside the equally present mechanisms of cultural resilience, neither sentimentalizing the wound nor the recovery. What transmission means when what is transmitted is both the injury and the knowledge that survived it is the question the essay holds open.
