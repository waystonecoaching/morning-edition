Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

The first peer-reviewed meta-analysis to aggregate PTSD prevalence data specifically for former first responders, those who have left the job, not the active-duty samples that dominate this literature. This is the baseline Mark's entire practice works against. The number that comes out of this will shape how departments, insurers, and clients understand long-term occupational risk.

Systematic review and meta-analysis published in the June 2026 issue of Journal of Traumatic Stress (Vol. 39, pp. 564-575) pooling PTSD prevalence estimates across former first responders, retired, separated, or career-ended personnel. The former-responder population has been largely invisible in the research record; active-duty samples skew the picture because the most affected people are often already out. This paper attempts to remedy that gap with pooled estimates across occupational categories.

Try this: Watch for the occupational-category breakdown if available. Fire versus EMS versus law enforcement prevalence differences are clinically meaningful when structuring intake screening and scope-of-practice conversations.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem with Polyvagal Theory

Source flag first: Mad in America has an explicit anti-psychiatry editorial stance, which shapes how this critique lands. That said, the mechanistic critique of polyvagal theory has been circulating in peer-reviewed neuroscience for years, Grossman and Taylor (2007) being the canonical paper. The vagal nerve's role in social engagement is more constrained than Porges' popularized model claims. Mark works from Deb Dana's polyvagal framework with clients; this piece is a prompt to know the critique and hold the model accurately, not abandon its clinical utility.

Mad in America piece challenging the mechanistic claims of polyvagal theory, specifically the assertion that the vagus nerve directly governs social and emotional regulation through a clean three-tier hierarchy. The underlying neuroscience critique, distinct from Mad in America's anti-psychiatry framing, targets vagal anatomy: the myelinated ventral vagal pathway is less dominant in mammalian social behavior than the popularized model suggests. The critique does not invalidate therapeutic use of autonomic state awareness; it challenges the neurobiological explanatory frame that practitioners often cite to clients.

Try this: Read the Grossman and Taylor neuroscience critique directly rather than relying on this secondary framing. Know the limits of the model you teach, especially on polyvagal's specific anatomy claims versus its broader clinical usefulness.
Source: Mad In America

Facing trauma under the influence of psychedelics: A phenomenological study with Nova rave survivors

The October 7, 2023 Nova attack created a dataset that did not previously exist: mass casualty trauma experienced during active psychedelic use. This phenomenological study examines what that intersection produced. It is not psychedelic-assisted therapy research; it is trauma phenomenology under psychedelic influence at the moment of injury, a clinically underexplored question with implications for how integration coaching approaches acute-onset cases. Scope-of-practice edge: the study touches acute trauma, psychosis risk, and dissociation in high-acuity contexts.

Mixed-methods phenomenological study in Journal of Psychopharmacology examining how Nova rave survivors experienced the October 7 attack while under the influence of psychedelics. Published ahead of print November 2025. The study asks whether psychedelic intoxication at the moment of trauma shaped the nature, intensity, or integration trajectory of posttraumatic stress. Findings are likely preliminary given the study design and sample, but the dataset is unprecedented and the question is not theoretical.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology (SAGE)

No. 05 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-05-26

How understanding moral injury could save veterans' lives

DAV content, advocacy press rather than peer-reviewed, but the veteran moral injury frame has direct applicability to first responder coaching. The betrayal-of-moral-expectations mechanism (Litz et al.) surfaces in fire and EMS in distinct patterns: following questionable orders on scene, systemic abandonment post-incident, and the moral weight of decisions made under pressure without adequate support. This is applied clinical framing, not primary research.

DAV piece making the case that moral injury, the violation of deeply held moral beliefs and expectations, is a distinct construct from PTSD and requires different clinical attention to address veteran suicide and mental health outcomes. Likely references the Litz framework and argues for treatment approaches targeting meaning, culpability, and betrayal rather than fear-extinction-based trauma processing. Advocacy framing but grounded in established conceptual territory.

Source: Disabled American Veterans (DAV)
