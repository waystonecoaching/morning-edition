VA Launches MDMA Trial for PTSD and Alcohol Use Disorders

The VA moving MDMA into a federal trial for combined PTSD and alcohol use disorder is the kind of policy signal that reaches your clients before the science settles. Veterans and first responders share enough of the same injury pattern that what the VA sanctions tends to set expectations across the responder world. Worth tracking less for the result than for what it does to the conversation in the firehouse. This sits on the scope-of-practice edge: substance use plus PTSD is high-acuity territory, and the integration work you do is downstream of clinical treatment, not a substitute for it.

The Department of Veterans Affairs announced a clinical trial studying MDMA-assisted therapy for veterans carrying both PTSD and alcohol use disorder. The combined diagnosis is notable because comorbid substance use has often been an exclusion criterion in earlier psychedelic trials. Coverage frames it as part of a broader VA push to test psychedelics for treatment-resistant veteran mental health. The trial is early-stage and outcomes are not yet reported.

Try this: If a first responder client raises the VA trial as a reason to pursue MDMA therapy, keep your lane clear: you can support integration, not direct them toward or away from a clinical treatment decision.

Can MDMA Cure PTSD? A New Review of the Evidence Says It Is Too Early to Tell

Read this one against the VA story above. A new review of the MDMA-for-PTSD literature argues the evidence base is thinner and more uncertain than the advocacy press suggests. This is the credibility-stress signal the field keeps generating, and it deserves the same weight as the trial announcements. For a coach working integration, the honest posture is to hold the promise and the doubt at once rather than picking the more exciting side.

A published review surveying the MDMA-assisted therapy evidence concludes that claims of efficacy outrun what the trials have actually demonstrated. The authors point to methodological problems that recur across the field: functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and small samples. The piece is a corrective to the cure framing that dominates popular coverage. It does not argue the therapy is useless, only that certainty is premature.

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

This is the rare psychedelic story that lands squarely in the responder lane rather than the clinic. MAPS is training first responders in psychedelic crisis assessment and de-escalation, which is operational ground you stand on, not advocacy. As more states open legal access, the call volume for people in altered states rises, and the responder showing up needs assessment and de-escalation skills, not pharmacology debates. Track this as a model Arizona could follow.

MAPS announced a partnership with Colorado's Natural Medicine Division to provide its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide. The program uses video and assessment modules teaching de-escalation and evidence-based response to people in acute psychedelic distress. It is framed as harm reduction tied to Colorado's legal natural-medicine framework. The training is offered at no cost to participating responders.

Try this: Look at whether the MAPS crisis assessment curriculum maps onto your Phoenix Fire behavioral crisis work, and whether any of it is portable to Arizona responder training.

When Your Workplace Does Not Match Your Ethical Outlook: The Problem of Moral Injury

This piece sits in the structural-critique lineage of moral injury, the Litz and Shay frame rather than the performance-psych one. It locates the wound in the gap between a person's ethics and what the institution asks of them, which is precisely the territory most first responders never get language for. When a client describes the betrayal of being ordered to act against their own conscience, this is the vocabulary that fits. The applied resilience camp would push back that this risks pathologizing ordinary friction, and that tension is the content worth sitting with rather than resolving.

The article frames moral injury as the damage done when people are required to act against their moral convictions by the systems they work inside. It draws the concept out of its military origins and into ordinary workplaces, arguing that institutional failure, not personal weakness, is the source. The author distinguishes moral injury from burnout and from PTSD. The emphasis falls on organizational responsibility rather than individual coping.

Human Coaching Boosts Adherence in AI Wearable Obesity Study

A study finding that human coaching lifts adherence above what the app alone delivers is quiet validation of the thing you actually sell: presence. As AI floods the wellness and behavior-change market, the differentiator is not information but the relationship that gets a person to keep showing up. The contact, in Campbell's sense, is the intervention. Useful evidence to have in your back pocket when a prospective client wonders why they would pay for a person.

Researchers studying an AI-driven wearable for weight management found that participants who also received human coaching stuck with the program at higher rates than those using the technology alone. The coaching condition improved engagement and adherence, the variables that predict outcomes. The finding suggests human contact remains the active ingredient in behavior change even when the tracking is automated. The study was specific to an obesity intervention but speaks to the broader question of what coaching adds.

Your Vagus Nerve Is Not in Control: The Problem With Polyvagal Theory

Read this with both hands open. Polyvagal theory is load-bearing in how you describe autonomic state to clients, so a serious critique of its neuroscientific claims is not an attack to repel, it is a stress test of your own framework. The strongest objections target whether the anatomy and evolutionary story hold up, not whether the felt experience of state shift is real. You can keep the clinical usefulness of ventral, sympathetic, and dorsal as a map of felt safety while staying honest that the underlying biology is contested. That honesty is what separates a practitioner from a true believer.

The article lays out the scientific case against Porges's polyvagal theory, arguing that several of its core anatomical and evolutionary claims are not supported by current neuroscience. Critics contend the theory has spread through trauma and somatic therapy circles faster than the evidence warrants. The piece does not deny that nervous system states shift, only that polyvagal theory correctly explains the mechanism. It is part of a longer-running debate about the gap between the theory's clinical popularity and its empirical footing.

Try this: Audit how you describe polyvagal mechanics on the Waystone site and in sessions. Keep the felt-state map; soften any language that asserts the contested biology as settled fact.

Moral Injury Is Not Just for Soldiers Anymore

A fitting close. Moral injury is moving out of the war frame and into ordinary moral life, and this piece reaches for the older language of conscience and repair that the clinical vocabulary cannot quite hold. The Desert tradition understood the wound of acting against one's own heart long before it had a diagnostic name, and the work was confession, attention, and the slow restoration of the soul. Read it as a reminder that some of what your clients carry is not a disorder to treat but a moral weight to be witnessed and integrated.

The essay argues that moral injury, long associated with combat veterans, describes a wound now visible across many walks of life. It frames the experience in moral and spiritual terms rather than purely clinical ones, pointing toward conscience, guilt, and the need for repair. The piece suggests that naming moral injury outside the military opens room for forms of healing that therapy alone does not reach. It treats the injury as a question of meaning, not only symptoms.
