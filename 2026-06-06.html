VA Launches MDMA Clinical Trial to Measure Effects on PTSD

This is the institution that owns the largest PTSD caseload in the country putting MDMA inside its own walls. For a coach who works first responders alongside veterans, a VA trial is the closest signal we have to where the standard of care is actually headed, not where advocacy says it should go. Watch the trial design more than the headline.

The Department of Veterans Affairs has opened a clinical trial studying MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in veterans, running the work through its own research apparatus rather than relying solely on outside sponsors. The move follows the FDA's earlier rejection of the first MDMA application and signals the VA wants its own data before committing. It places the federal government on both sides of the question at once, regulator and researcher.

Can MDMA Cure PTSD? A New Review Says It Is Too Early to Tell

Read this next to the VA story, not apart from it. A review counseling caution is exactly the corrective the field needs while the institutional money turns on. The credibility stress in psychedelic research is real, and functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and small samples remain unsolved. This is the sober voice in the room.

A new review of the MDMA-for-PTSD literature concludes the evidence base is not yet strong enough to call the treatment proven. The authors point to methodological problems that have dogged the field, including the near-impossibility of blinding participants to a drug with obvious subjective effects, and the role of expectancy in reported outcomes. The verdict is not that MDMA fails, but that the science has not yet earned its confident headlines.

When Your Workplace Does Not Match Your Ethics: The Problem of Moral Injury

This sits squarely in the structural-critique lineage, Litz and Shay rather than HPRC. The claim is that moral injury is not a personal failure of resilience but the residue of being placed in conditions where right action was foreclosed. Naming it correctly changes everything about how a coach holds it, since you cannot resilience-train your way out of a betrayal.

The piece frames moral injury as what happens when a person is forced to act against their own ethical code, often by the institution employing them. It distinguishes the wound from PTSD, locating its source in violated conscience and broken trust rather than fear-based threat. The implication is that the repair is moral and relational, not merely symptomatic.

How Understanding Moral Injury Could Save Veterans' Lives

The perspective argues that recognizing moral injury as distinct from PTSD could reduce veteran suicide by directing care toward the actual injury. It contends that conscience-based wounds need moral and communal repair, work like restoration of meaning and belonging, rather than exposure protocols built for fear conditioning. The framing is advocacy-adjacent but rooted in the established moral injury literature.

You cannot extinguish a wound of conscience with a protocol built for fear.

Where the Conversation piece names the wound, this one argues that naming it accurately is itself protective. The thesis is that mislabeling moral injury as PTSD sends people toward treatments aimed at the wrong target, and that the corrected frame opens a path that fear-based therapy cannot reach. The crossover from veterans to responders here is direct.

MAPS and Colorado Partner on Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

This is the rare item that lands on both Mark's psychedelic axis and his first responder axis at once. As Colorado's regulated natural medicine system comes online, responders are the ones who will meet people mid-experience in the field. Crisis assessment and de-escalation training built for that exact moment is the practical, unglamorous infrastructure the policy debate usually skips.

MAPS partnered with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to offer its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide, free of charge. The program uses videos and assessments to teach evidence-based de-escalation for people in difficult psychedelic experiences. It is harm reduction aimed at the field encounter, not the clinic, as Colorado's regulated access expands.
