Posttraumatic stress disorder prevalence among former first responders: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Most of the wellness conversation aims at people still on shift. This meta-analysis looks at the ones who left, the retired and separated, where the rituals of crew and call sign fall away and the symptoms stay. That is squarely the population that lands in a transition coaching practice, and it gives you peer-reviewed ground under what you already see in the room.

The authors pool prevalence estimates across studies of former first responders to ask how much PTSD persists after the career ends. The work treats separation from the job as its own risk window rather than a clean exit from exposure. As a systematic review and meta-analysis, it weighs study quality and heterogeneity rather than leaning on any single sample, which makes it a more durable reference than a one-off survey.

Try this: Pull the prevalence figure and keep it on hand as a calibration point when a separated responder tells you they should be over it by now.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

A systematic review of the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors of posttraumatic stress disorder among paramedics

This one sits on your own lineage. Paramedics carry a different exposure profile than fire or law enforcement, repeated proximity to death without the discharge of suppression. The review separates risk factors from protective factors, which is the more useful cut for coaching: you cannot undo the call volume, but protective factors are where a client actually has leverage.

The authors review the paramedic-specific literature on PTSD, sorting findings into prevalence, what raises risk, and what buffers against it. Holding risk and protective factors apart keeps the picture from collapsing into fatalism. For a coach, the protective-factor column is the workable surface, the place where felt safety, connection, and meaning can be named without drifting into treatment.

Early promise and major evidence gaps in AI psychological training tools

AI training tools for psychological skills are arriving fast, and the practitioner question is whether they teach contact or just simulate it. For non-directive inquiry, the risk is plain: a tool optimized for plausible responses can drill the opposite of presence. Read this for where the evidence is thin before any of it touches how you train or supervise.

The piece surveys AI systems built to train psychological and counseling skills and finds early promise undercut by serious gaps in the evidence base. Enthusiasm is running ahead of validation, with little rigorous proof that these tools build durable competence. The honest takeaway is caution: useful as scaffolding, unproven as a substitute for live, supervised practice.

Try this: If you are tempted by any AI roleplay tool for practicing inquiry, name the one skill you would still only trust to live practice, and keep it there.

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

This is the critical read the field needs right now, and it sits in a peer-reviewed journal rather than advocacy press. After the FDA setbacks, the question has shifted from does it work to can the evidence withstand scrutiny. For your BTTY path and any integration work, this is the standard to hold: promise disciplined by data. Note the scope edge, this is treatment-of-PTSD territory, where coaching only ever sits downstream as integration.

The authors step back from the advocacy framing around MDMA-assisted psychotherapy and ask whether the evidence meets the bar the enthusiasm assumes. They press on methodology, blinding, expectancy effects, and the gap between dramatic early results and rigorous proof. The argument is not that MDMA fails but that the field owes itself evidentiary discipline before treating the case as closed.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Perspective: How understanding moral injury could save veterans' lives

This is the operational, applied face of moral injury, the frame that treats it as a problem to recognize and act on. Useful, and worth reading alongside the structural critics who locate the wound in betrayal and leadership failure rather than the individual. The two camps are in live tension; that tension is the content, not a flaw to resolve.

An opinion piece arguing that recognizing moral injury, distinct from PTSD, could change outcomes for veterans at risk. It leans into the applied case: name the wound, train people to see it, intervene earlier. It does not deeply engage the structural critique that moral injury often originates in institutional betrayal, which is the conversation to hold beside it.

Try this: When a client describes a violation of their own moral code on the job, notice whether you reach for the applied frame or the betrayal frame, and which one they need.

When your workplace doesn't match your ethical outlook: the problem of moral injury

Here is the structural counterpart to the previous story. This frame puts the wound in the gap between a person's ethics and the institution they serve, which is exactly where first responders live, asked to act inside systems that do not share their values. For coaching, it relocates the work: not fixing the person, but helping them see clearly the bind they are in.

This piece frames moral injury as what happens when a workplace forces conduct that violates a person's ethical outlook. The emphasis is organizational rather than individual: the harm lives in the mismatch, not a personal deficiency. That reframe matters for responders, who routinely carry the cost of institutional decisions they did not make and cannot change.

Try this: Keep one question ready: is this a wound in them, or a wound the system is handing them to carry?
