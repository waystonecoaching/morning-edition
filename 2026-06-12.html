Chicago's VA Hospital Just Enrolled Its First Veterans in a Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy Program

The VA system just crossed a line it has never crossed before. Edward Hines Jr. VA Hospital in suburban Chicago enrolled the first veterans in a federally authorized psilocybin-assisted therapy trial. This is not a research footnote. It is a policy shift inside the federal healthcare infrastructure that serves the population you work with. Scope note: these remain clinical research trials, not approved treatments, and the integration layer in VA protocols differs materially from coaching.

Hines VA joins trial sites in Houston, New York, and San Francisco, creating the most geographically distributed psilocybin evidence-generation program in federal healthcare history. The protocol pairs two high-dose psilocybin sessions with preparatory and integrative psychotherapy, with 12-month follow-up. The trial was catalyzed by a Trump executive order in April 2026 accelerating psychedelic research for serious mental illness in veterans.

Try this: If any clients are veterans asking about VA psychedelic access, the honest answer is: research only, not approved treatment, enrollment is site-specific. Know the geography of who is running trials now.

Conceptualizing Moral Health for Military Moral Injury Care: Theoretical Foundations of the Moral Engagement Group

The Moral Engagement Group reframes moral injury not as a problem to fix but as a moral challenge that demands growth. That framing maps directly onto integration work. The co-facilitation model, mental health provider alongside chaplain, names the interdisciplinary seam where coaching and clinical care meet. Worth knowing for how you structure any group format with first responders.

Researchers articulate the theoretical backbone of the Moral Engagement Group (formerly Moral Injury Group), drawing on Judith Herman's trauma theory, Gomez's betrayal trauma theory, and Robillard and Strawser's theory of moral exploitation. The group aims to build a coherent model of moral health and moral functioning. Community-based facilitation, shared responsibility, and the chaplain's role are central. The framework treats the injury as a moral event requiring moral engagement, not symptom management.

Try this: The chaplain co-facilitation model is a practical template for structuring peer-level spiritual care alongside clinical care. If you are developing a group format for first responder moral injury work, this is the closest theoretical grounding in current literature.
Source: Frontiers in Psychiatry

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

First responders are now being trained to de-escalate people in psychedelic crises, statewide, for free, backed by a state government and MAPS. This is the policy frontier catching up to what you already know. As someone working at the intersection of Phoenix Fire and psychedelic integration coaching, the structure of this training is worth knowing. What Colorado is building today, Arizona will likely face tomorrow.

MAPS partnered with Colorado's Department of Revenue Natural Medicine Division to provide its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to law enforcement, EMS, and firefighters across the state at no cost. The curriculum covers common effects of natural medicines, interacting with people in altered states, and best practices for de-escalation and crisis management. Colorado is the first state to license this MAPS training for statewide first responder use.

Try this: Review the MAPS Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention curriculum. If Phoenix Fire does not have a protocol for psychedelic crisis response yet, this is the template that will get cited when they build one.

No. 05 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2025-11-11

Facing Trauma Under the Influence of Psychedelics: A Phenomenological Study with Nova Rave Survivors

There is no other dataset like this. A mass casualty event in which most participants were actively under the influence of psychedelics when it happened. The researchers named a dissociative response pattern they call adaptive psychedelic dissociation: emotional detachment, derealization, and automatic behavior that may have served a survival function in the moment but creates a specific post-event integration problem. Scope note: the integration sequelae described here sit near the clinical line, particularly for clients with active dissociative symptoms.

Forty-five Nova festival survivors were interviewed using a mixed-methods phenomenological design, categorized by substance type: classic psychedelics (n=24), empathogens (n=19), ketamine (n=2). Researchers identified adaptive psychedelic dissociation as a distinct post-event phenomenon combining traumatic and psychedelic dissociative features. Survivors who used classic psychedelics reported significantly lower anxiety and post-traumatic symptoms than those who used empathogens or nothing. The study raises new questions about the interaction between acute psychedelic state and traumatic encoding.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

A Systematic Review of the Prevalence, Risk Factors, and Protective Factors of PTSD Among Paramedics

One in five. Nearly seven times the general population rate of 3%. You work with this population at Phoenix Fire. This review is the current literature baseline for risk and protective factors in your specific client population.

The review consolidates prevalence data across multiple cohorts, arriving at a 20.5% PTSD prevalence (95% CI: 10.4-36.5%) for paramedics and EMTs. Risk factors include cumulative low-threshold traumatic exposure across routine shifts, not just critical incidents. Organizational stressors, personality and coping style, and social support function as both risk and protective factors. The population-level number is seven times the general working-age population rate.

Try this: Use 20.5% as your cited baseline when discussing the scope of need with Phoenix Fire leadership or when writing about your target population in any Waystone materials.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Mercy Health Launches Protocol 99 to Support First Responders in Crisis

Mercy Health launched Protocol 99 at its Dayton-Springfield Emergency Center on June 4, 2026, available 24/7 to active and retired fire, EMS, and law enforcement. Activation happens through a peer support member, who assesses, transports if needed, and remains present through the ED visit. First responders presenting alone can invoke Protocol 99 directly at registration. The model emphasizes confidentiality, continuity, and specialized follow-up.

A first responder in crisis can simply state Protocol 99 as their chief complaint at registration to initiate the process.

The barrier to care for first responders in crisis has always been the front door of the emergency department. Protocol 99 removes it. A peer support member activates the pathway, stays alongside the person through the whole visit, and connects them to follow-up. This is a peer-support model doing what peer support is supposed to do: holding the relational frame while the clinical system engages.

Try this: This model is replicable. If you are advising Phoenix Fire on peer support infrastructure, Protocol 99 gives you a concrete template for what a hospital-integrated peer activation pathway looks like.

Published in the June 2026 issue of the Journal of Traumatic Stress, this systematic review and meta-analysis reports PTSD prevalence rates in former first responders, distinguishing post-career outcomes from in-service rates across fire, law enforcement, and EMS. The work builds on the growing evidence that occupational trauma effects persist well past active service, with implications for career transition coaching and post-service integration work.
