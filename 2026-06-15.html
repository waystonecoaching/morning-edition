A systematic review of the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors of PTSD among paramedics

Mark came up through EMS and now coaches the people who are still in it. This is the most current peer-reviewed synthesis of what the PTSD burden actually looks like for that population, which matters both for Waystone marketing claims and for how he frames risk with clients who minimize their exposure history.

The review aggregates prevalence data on PTSD in paramedics across studies, identifies occupational exposure load, shift culture isolation, and inadequate peer support as the primary risk amplifiers, and flags social support and psychological safety as the clearest protective factors. The picture is consistent: PTSD rates in this population run two to four times the general-population baseline, and career length without structured support correlates with worse outcomes. Protective factors are modifiable, which is where coaching can legitimately operate.

Try this: When paramedic clients minimize their cumulative exposure load, this paper's aggregate data is a clean reference point. Not for diagnosing, but for reframing what normal actually looks like in the job.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

This is the critical literature arriving. MDMA-assisted psychotherapy has been running on advocacy momentum; this peer-reviewed reassessment in JTS asks whether the evidentiary standards match the regulatory and public confidence placed in it. Mark sits at the intersection of psychedelic integration coaching and first responder PTSD, so the credibility of the underlying science is a professional concern, not just academic.

The paper argues that early MDMA trial data, while promising, was collected under conditions that make rigorous evaluation difficult: unblinded participants, high expectancy effects, and heterogeneous outcome measurement. The authors call for evidentiary discipline before clinical scaling, not rejection of the therapy, but insistence on the same standards applied to other PTSD interventions. The piece lands in the same issue as clinical trial data, positioning itself as a corrective frame.

Try this: When a client or partner clinic cites MDMA therapy as established treatment, this piece gives Mark the language to hold a more precise position: promising, not proven, still contested in peer review.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Redesigning trauma-focused interventions to address capacity challenges in mental health systems

Mental health system capacity is the structural context Waystone operates inside. When therapists are unavailable or have six-month waitlists, first responders either go without or land with a coach. Understanding how the system is trying to redesign itself matters for knowing where coaching sits in the gap.

The paper examines why evidence-based trauma interventions stall at the system level despite strong individual-level efficacy data. It identifies workforce shortfalls, reimbursement misalignment, and training dissemination failures as the main bottlenecks. The proposed redesign framework centers stepped care and task-shifting, meaning lower-acuity cases are intentionally routed to non-clinical supports. That is a structural argument for coaching legitimacy, not as therapy, but as an intentional rung in the care ladder.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Mercy Health launches 'Protocol 99' for first responder mental health

Health systems are starting to build dedicated intake pathways for first responders rather than routing them through standard behavioral health access. Protocol 99 is an institutional signal worth tracking. It names a structural problem, slow access and stigma, and attempts to solve it with a dedicated channel. That is the same gap Waystone addresses from the coaching side.

Mercy Health's Protocol 99 creates a confidential, first-responder-specific entry point into behavioral health care, bypassing standard intake processes that are perceived as stigmatizing or administratively cumbersome for people in uniform. The program provides rapid access to providers with first responder background or training. These institutional programs rarely grow without organizational pressure, and their launch points to how far responder wellness has moved from a side conversation to a line item.

Conceptualizing moral health for military moral injury care: theoretical foundations of the moral engagement group

The moral injury field is developing its own internal architecture, and this paper is part of that construction project. The distinction between moral injury, moral distress, and moral health matters for Mark because it shapes what coaching can address versus what belongs in clinical care. The moral engagement group model is explicitly non-clinical in its framing.

The paper proposes 'moral health' as a positive construct alongside moral injury, drawing on virtue ethics and psychological resilience literature to argue that moral injury care should include not just wound repair but capacity-building. The moral engagement group is framed as a structured peer process that works with moral meaning, not symptom reduction. The theoretical foundation pulls from Litz, Shay, and moral philosophy rather than pure psychopathology. This is the field trying to grow beyond a deficit model.

Try this: The moral engagement group format, structured peer process around moral meaning, is worth reading as a potential coaching analog for peer cohorts in the first responder space.
Source: Frontiers in Psychiatry

Anchorage Firefighters Grapple with Burnout and Injuries as Call Volumes Climb

The Anchorage situation is Phoenix in a colder climate. Call volume doubling in ten years without proportional staffing increases is a structural burnout accelerator. Mark's behavioral crisis role at Phoenix Fire puts him inside exactly this system. This is not a hero story; it is a load-bearing problem.

Anchorage firefighters are fielding roughly double the call volume they handled a decade ago with a headcount that has not scaled to match. The result is shortened recovery time between high-acuity calls, increasing injury rates, and growing reports of burnout and secondary trauma. Department leadership acknowledges the staffing gap but faces budget constraints. The piece includes direct quotes from line firefighters describing the cumulative weight, not dramatic incidents, but the steady grind of routine calls with no room to reset.

Patient-Driven Care in Psychedelic Therapy

The commentary argues that psychedelic therapy has largely been structured around protocol fidelity and therapist guidance at the expense of patient-driven preferences and self-directed processing. The authors draw on shared decision-making literature to call for greater patient agency in session design, music selection, intention-setting, and post-session integration planning. The piece is not anti-protocol; it is pushing back against the implicit assumption that patients should be passive recipients in altered states. This has real implications for how integration coaches relate to the clinical session.

The field has structured psychedelic therapy around protocol fidelity. The patient was not always the center of it.

The NEJM publishing on psychedelic therapy is a marker. This piece, focused on patient agency and decision-making in clinical psychedelic settings, sits on the coaching-clinical line: questions of patient autonomy, informed consent, and the therapeutic relationship in altered states. Scope-of-practice note: the clinical framing is deliberate here. This is not integration coaching territory; it is the medical and ethical architecture of the guided session itself.

Source: New England Journal of Medicine

No. 08 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-06-02

Moral injury among journalists: A scoping review

Moral injury research has historically centered combat veterans and first responders. Extending the framework to journalists is not mission drift. It is a test of the construct's portability and sharpens the definition by putting it in a different occupational context. What holds across populations is what is actually essential to the concept, which matters for how Mark explains moral injury to clients who are not veterans.

The scoping review identifies moral injury literature in journalism, finding that war correspondents and trauma reporters experience the same core construct: transgression of deeply held moral beliefs, perpetration, and betrayal, without the military-specific framing that has historically defined the field. The review surfaces bystander guilt, complicity in harm through publication decisions, and institutional betrayal as the dominant mechanisms in this population. The authors call for journalism-specific instruments rather than adapting military scales. The cross-occupational pattern strengthens the broader construct.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 09 · Coaching Practice · 2026-06-02

Religion and spirituality as pathways to resilience: The role of positive coping and postmigration stress in displaced populations

The contemplative frame is not decoration in Mark's practice. This peer-reviewed piece in JTS treats religion and spirituality as active psychological resources in trauma recovery, not soft variables to control for. The displaced-population context is different from first responders, but the mechanism, meaning-making through structured spiritual practice, is directly relevant to a hesychast practitioner working with moral injury.

Across a sample of displaced individuals dealing with postmigration stressors, positive religious coping, specifically the use of prayer, spiritual community, and meaning-making frameworks, predicted lower PTSD symptom severity and greater psychological flexibility. Negative religious coping, spiritual struggle and abandonment, was associated with worse outcomes. The study treats spirituality as a multidimensional variable rather than a binary, which is more useful clinically. The authors argue that culturally competent trauma care needs to ask about spiritual life directly, not treat it as outside scope.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 10 · Polyvagal · 2026-06-06

The Dharma of the Nervous System

Polyvagal theory and contemplative practice are not parallel tracks. They are describing overlapping territory: the relationship between bodily state and the quality of attention. Tricycle publishing on the nervous system is worth a read not because Buddhist psychology needs neuroscience to validate it, but because the conversation about what sits below cognition is getting broader.

The piece explores how Buddhist meditation practices, particularly breath-centered and body-scanning techniques, intersect with polyvagal theory's account of autonomic state regulation. The author argues that the dharma has always attended to nervous system phenomena without using that vocabulary, and that Dana and Porges give Western practitioners a language for what contemplatives have mapped through practice. The tone is integrative without being syncretic. The Desert Fathers would recognize the basic claim: the body knows things the mind has to catch up to.

Source: Tricycle: The Buddhist Review
