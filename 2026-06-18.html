Differentiating moral injury from posttraumatic stress disorder: Implications of the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

This one sits directly on your scope line. The DSM-5-TR Z-code for moral problems creates a formal opening to track moral injury separately from PTSD, and this paper works out what that distinction demands from practitioners. For a coach working near the clinical boundary with first responders, the moral injury versus anxiety-based PTSD distinction is not academic. The presenting wound, the intervention path, and the referral threshold all look different depending on which it is.

A JTS EarlyView paper examines what the DSM-5-TR's moral problems Z-code means for differentiating moral injury from PTSD in clinical and applied settings. Moral injury has historically been conflated with PTSD because the presentations overlap significantly, but the new Z-code creates formal pressure to track them separately. The paper works through the screening, diagnostic, and treatment-selection implications for practitioners across clinical and coaching-adjacent roles.

Try this: Review your intake screening language. When a client's presenting wound centers on betrayal, guilt, or fractured moral self-concept rather than fear-based re-experiencing, that is a different coaching lane and a different referral calculus.

Redesigning trauma-focused interventions to address capacity challenges in mental health systems

The mental health system cannot absorb the demand coming from first responders, and this paper names the structural gap explicitly. When the clinical tier is backlogged, coaching and peer support bear more of the load by default. That is the environment you are operating in, and this paper sharpens the case for what the non-clinical tier needs to do well.

This JTS paper argues that the shortage of trauma-trained clinicians is not a temporary staffing problem but a structural feature of current mental health systems, and that trauma-focused interventions need to be redesigned with that constraint in mind. The authors examine task-sharing models, stepped-care approaches, and coaching-adjacent modalities that can extend reach without requiring full clinical training. The argument has direct implications for how peer support and coaching programs are scoped and positioned.

Try this: The case for your practice is in here. The system is not going to fix its capacity problem before your clients need help. Know what you can hold and what to hand off.

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

This is the piece the field needed but is not universally welcome. A JTS commentary from the same issue as the moral injury paper, it takes a hard look at the evidentiary problems in MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD. Given the FDA rejection, the replication failures, and the ongoing credibility pressure from the blinding problem in randomized trials, the enthusiasm you see elsewhere in the feed deserves calibration. Read this before any clinical conversation about MDMA with clients, partners, or referral sources.

JTS Vol 39 Issue 3 publishes this three-page commentary reassessing the MDMA-assisted psychotherapy literature for PTSD. The authors trace the field's trajectory from early promise to current evidentiary scrutiny, focusing on blinding failures in randomized trials, the FDA's rejection of the MAPS NDA, and what more rigorous standards for therapeutic psychedelic research would actually require. It is not a dismissal of the modality but a call for evidentiary discipline that the current enthusiasm cycle has largely bypassed.

Try this: Scope note: if clients or partners ask you about MDMA trials, the honest answer is that the evidence base is under active reassessment and no approved treatment exists. Refer to this piece if they want the peer-reviewed read on why.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

New research: MDMA-assisted therapy for veterans with PTSD and alcohol use disorder

New research on MDMA for veterans with comorbid PTSD and alcohol use disorder matters because the comorbidity is the common presentation, not the clean PTSD-only profile used in earlier trials. If this holds, it is a meaningful expansion of the population that might benefit. Read alongside story three, not instead of it.

A new study examines MDMA-assisted therapy for veterans presenting with both PTSD and alcohol use disorder, a population that has been largely excluded from prior trials due to the substance use comorbidity. The findings suggest potential benefit across both conditions. The study has not been peer-reviewed at publication, and the usual cautions about single-trial claims and blinding limitations apply.
