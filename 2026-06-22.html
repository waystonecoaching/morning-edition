From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

The peer-reviewed literature is catching up to the credibility problems that shadowed MAPS' FDA submission. This JTS paper makes the field-level argument for stricter evidentiary standards -- an important read for anyone working with first responders in the psychedelic integration space. The critique is principled, not dismissive. Read it before a client asks.

Published in the June 2026 issue of Journal of Traumatic Stress, this paper argues MDMA-assisted psychotherapy must be held to the same evidentiary discipline as other interventional treatments for PTSD. The authors examine blind integrity failures and trial design problems from the MAPS Phase 3 data and call for a reassessment of therapeutic claims. They frame this not as a rejection of the modality but as a necessary correction for a field that moved faster than its evidence base. The paper calls for replication under rigorous independent conditions before MDMA therapy can be treated as standard of care.

Try this: When integration clients ask about MDMA therapy, this paper gives you the principled skeptical framing the field needs -- not dismissal, but calibrated uncertainty grounded in published literature.

Differentiating moral injury from posttraumatic stress disorder: Implications of the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

The DSM-5-TR added a Z-code for moral problems -- not a diagnosis, but a clinical marker. This paper works out what that distinction means in practice. For a coach working near the clinical line with first responders who carry both moral injury and PTSD symptoms, understanding the conceptual separation matters for what you name and what you refer.

The paper examines the clinical and conceptual line between moral injury and PTSD in light of the new DSM-5-TR Z-code for moral problems. The authors argue that moral injury involves a distinct causal structure -- a violation of deeply held moral beliefs -- that current PTSD criteria do not adequately capture. They trace implications for assessment, care pathways, and how practitioners should describe what a client is carrying. The Z-code provides a formal hook for naming moral injury in clinical contexts without requiring a PTSD diagnosis.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

A systematic review of the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors of posttraumatic stress disorder among paramedics

Systematic review of what the literature actually shows about PTSD in the paramedic population. Useful as a reference point for any intake or ongoing work with EMS clients, and as a corrective to the framing that call volume alone explains the risk.

This JTS systematic review aggregated studies on PTSD rates among paramedics, finding elevated prevalence compared to the general population across multiple national contexts. Key risk factors include cumulative trauma exposure, organizational stress, and low social support within the work unit. Protective factors include peer support access, psychological safety in station culture, and a sense of meaning or calling. The review surfaces that structural factors -- not just incident exposure -- account for much of the variance in outcomes.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

The framing has clear implications for how integration practitioners position client autonomy in session design and in the work that follows. NEJM's engagement signals that psychedelic therapy is now inside mainstream clinical discourse.

Redesigning trauma-focused interventions to address capacity challenges in mental health systems

The MH system's capacity problem is structural -- not enough trained providers, not enough access points. This paper addresses intervention redesign as a clinical necessity, which bears directly on where coaching fits in the continuum of care for first responders who cannot get what they need from the clinical system.

This JTS paper examines how the gap between demand for trauma-focused care and system capacity to deliver it can be addressed through intervention redesign -- stepped care, peer-delivered models, and abbreviated protocols adapted for non-specialist delivery. The authors argue this is not a compromise but a clinical necessity given the scale of need. For coaches working at the edge of the clinical system, the piece implicitly legitimizes the role that exists between the clinical and non-clinical ends of the care continuum.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Religion and spirituality as pathways to resilience: The role of positive coping and postmigration stress in displaced populations

Spiritual practice as a regulatory architecture rather than a comfort mechanism. Read through a polyvagal lens, religious and spiritual coping maps onto ventral vagal access through ritual, community, and embodied prayer. For a hesychast practitioner working with first responders who carry deep but unarticulated spiritual frameworks, this is practical rather than peripheral.

This JTS study found that positive religious and spiritual coping served as a significant protective factor against trauma symptoms in displaced populations experiencing ongoing postmigration stress. The mechanism appears to operate through regulation of the stress response and social connectedness rather than purely cognitive reappraisal. The findings suggest spiritual resources deserve explicit attention in resilience-building work -- not as an add-on but as a primary regulatory pathway. The population studied is displaced communities, not first responders, but the nervous system mechanism transfers.

For a practitioner whose clients often hold deep but unarticulated spiritual frameworks, the piece suggests the healing map may already exist within their tradition -- awaiting invitation rather than construction.
