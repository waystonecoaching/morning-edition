Patient-Driven Care in Psychedelic Therapy

The New England Journal of Medicine weighing in on who drives the therapeutic encounter in psychedelic therapy is a meaningful signal. As this field works to establish legitimacy, the power dynamics between guide and client will shape both outcomes and ethics. For coaching adjacent work in psychedelic integration, this lands close. A NEJM piece arguing for patient agency will carry weight in clinical and regulatory conversations for years.

The piece examines how psychedelic therapy models have historically centered the therapist's protocol over client preference. The NEJM argument is that patient-driven care, where clients carry more agency in session structure and set-and-setting decisions, may produce better outcomes and fewer ethical incidents. It draws on emerging literature suggesting that over-controlled therapeutic environments can undermine the very conditions that make psychedelic experiences integrable. The piece does not reject structure but argues for meaningful client voice in the process.

Try this: Read this alongside the BTTY facilitation framework and note where client autonomy is named explicitly and where it is assumed.

Behavioral Health in the Workplace: Supporting Fire Fighter Well-Being

Institutional betrayal as a driver of mental health outcomes is appearing in fire service literature in ways it has not before. This IAFF webinar positions organizational factors, not just individual trauma exposure, as a shaping force in first responder mental health. That is a meaningful shift in how departments frame the problem, and it has direct implications for how coaching clients interpret their own distress.

The IAFF hosted a webinar titled 'Psychological Safety, Workplace Incivility, and Institutional Betrayal: Organizational Factors Shaping Mental Health in First Responders.' The content addresses how department culture, leadership behavior, and organizational dysfunction compound the psychological burden firefighters carry from operational stress. The institutional betrayal angle is the sharpest edge here: when the department itself becomes a source of harm, normal recovery pathways can close. The framing moves the locus of the problem from the individual to the system.

Try this: If you have active clients at Phoenix Fire or comparable departments, map how institutional betrayal may be operating as a stress layer beneath the presenting PTSD or moral injury picture.

The patchwork of state and federal psychedelic regulation is generating real risk for practitioners and clients alike. An NEJM interview on oversight inconsistency signals that the credibility stakes around psychedelic therapy governance are climbing. Mark works near this space through first responder integration work; knowing where the regulatory guardrails are and where they are absent is part of scope-of-practice hygiene.

The NEJM platform gives this critique unusual reach in clinical and policy circles.

How Understanding Moral Injury Could Save Veterans' Lives

The DAV piece does something the medical press often avoids: it holds the distinction between PTSD and moral injury without collapsing them. That distinction matters for how you approach a client who carries wounds of conscience versus wounds of fear. Getting the map wrong at the start sets the work in the wrong direction.

The article draws on current moral injury research to argue that veterans who die by suicide are often driven not by fear-based trauma but by moral injury, a wound from participating in or witnessing events that violated their sense of right and wrong. Standard PTSD treatment pathways are poorly matched to this kind of wound. The piece advocates for care models that address meaning, guilt, and moral repair rather than threat extinction. The tone is measured and the citations are solid.

Source: DAV: Disabled American Veterans

Rethinking Directiveness in AI Coaching Chatbots

The paper examines whether non-directive coaching, widely held as best practice in human-to-human coaching, translates to AI-powered chatbot environments. It finds that AI coaching tools tend toward directive postures by necessity, since they must respond with something actionable, and questions whether this undermines the fundamental conditions for client insight. The research does not resolve the tension but raises the question of what coaching loses when the guide must always have an answer ready.

Non-directive approaches rest on the client's own knowing. Remove that as the compass and something else has to navigate.

The non-directive coaching debate has a new entrant: AI chatbots, which by design must choose a directive posture. The paper asks whether that architectural choice compromises coaching outcomes. For someone working inside the BTTY framework, where the client's inner knowing is the compass, this is a sharp question about what gets lost when directionality is hardwired into the tool.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Perceived Betrayal Tied to Chronic Pain Intensity and Decreased Mobility

The finding that perceived betrayal correlates with chronic pain intensity and decreased mobility is a somatic data point that fits the polyvagal frame. When the nervous system registers betrayal as a persistent threat signal, the dorsal vagal shutdown response can become chronic; pain and immobility are among its presentations. For work with first responders who carry both operational trauma and institutional betrayal wounds, this connection is worth holding.

A study in Physician's Weekly found that patients scoring higher on perceived betrayal scales reported significantly greater chronic pain intensity and reduced physical mobility. The relationship held across multiple conditions and appears independent of primary diagnosis. The authors suggest that the psychobiological response to betrayal may sustain pain pathways in ways analogous to mechanisms described in trauma-related somatic research. It is a clinical-adjacent finding; the coaching application is in framing, not treatment.

Try this: When a client presents with chronic pain alongside institutional or relational betrayal history, consider that the two may be interacting at the nervous system level rather than running in parallel.
Source: Physician's Weekly
