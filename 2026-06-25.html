A Sea Change, With Caveats: A Closer Look at Definium's Phase 3 LSD for MDD Readout

The first Phase 3 psychedelic trial for MDD posted topline results today, and Psychedelic Alpha is reading it critically. This matters for anyone coaching clients who are tracking the regulatory pipeline -- the credibility of psychedelic-assisted therapy lives or dies with Phase 3 data, and market enthusiasm consistently outruns evidentiary weight.

Definium Therapeutics announced positive topline data from its Phase 3 trial of DT120, an LSD candidate for major depressive disorder. The company's stock moved sharply on the news and a capital raise followed. Psychedelic Alpha examines the trial architecture, acknowledges the significance of reaching Phase 3 at all, and surfaces the caveats: the FDA pathway for LSD remains uncharted, MDD is not PTSD, and one topline readout does not a treatment paradigm make.

Try this: If clients are watching the psychedelic pipeline and asking what this means, the answer is: meaningful milestone, long road. Phase 3 data for MDD does not translate directly to approval for trauma indications.

Facing Trauma Under the Influence of Psychedelics: A Phenomenological Study with Nova Rave Survivors

The October 7 Nova rave attack produced a research scenario that did not exist before: mass casualty trauma onset in people under active psychedelic influence. How does acute trauma land differently when the nervous system is in that state? This is scope-edge territory, but it bears directly on integration work and the coaching conversation about what psychedelic-assisted therapy can and cannot do. Note: the phenomenological data here sits close to clinical terrain -- read it as research context, not protocol.

Using a mixed-methods phenomenological approach, researchers examined how survivors of the October 7, 2023 Nova rave massacre experienced trauma while under psychedelic influence. The study investigates how the psychedelic state intersected with acute threat response, memory encoding, and subsequent trauma symptoms. It surfaces questions about integration pathways that standard PTSD models were not built to handle -- specifically, whether the psychedelic-amplified sensory imprint of the event alters the trajectory of trauma processing.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

Relocating the Root: Expanding Psychedelic Care Beyond the Veteran-and-Survivor Frame

The PSIP literature review asks who psychedelic-assisted therapy actually serves and who has been systematically excluded. The field was built on veteran and first responder populations, which makes it familiar ground. But the equity argument matters as the practice matures -- care model assumptions that work for one population do not transfer cleanly to another.

The MAPS Psychedelic Social Impact Project reviews how care models in psychedelic-assisted therapy have been shaped by the demographics of early research populations: veterans, first responders, and survivors of sexualized violence. It examines what structural changes the field needs to serve people impacted by the criminal legal system and other underserved groups, and raises questions about whether the integration model as currently designed can hold the full range of experience it is being asked to address.

Facility dogs in fire departments work below the threshold of formal help-seeking -- the dog is there whether you come in for that reason or not. Reno's deployment is worth watching as a model. Facility dogs in first responder settings reduce barriers to help-seeking by providing low-stakes contact with a wellness resource -- personnel who would not initiate a formal peer support or counseling contact will engage with the dog, which opens a door. The model is being adopted in departments nationally as a complement to existing peer support programs.

Stepped-Care Treatment via Telehealth Reduces Alcohol Use in Patients with Liver Disease

A motivational interviewing efficacy study in a high-stakes medical population. Alcohol use in a liver disease context carries clear consequence -- this is the kind of study that demonstrates MI as a real clinical tool, not just a rapport framework. The telehealth delivery finding also matters for accessibility in first responder populations where scheduling is a real barrier.

Researchers found that stepped-care treatment delivered via telehealth, with motivational interviewing as a core component, significantly reduced alcohol use in patients with liver disease. The study supports remote delivery for MI-based interventions and demonstrates effectiveness even in medically complex patients where behavior change is often assumed to be unlikely. The stepped-care design -- matching intensity to need -- aligns with how coaching and clinical services can coordinate without overlapping.

How Motivational Interviewing Supports Neurodivergent People

Non-directive inquiry -- the same move at the center of BTTY work -- maps naturally onto neurodivergent communication styles. This piece makes that case for MI specifically, but the argument holds for coaching more broadly. Worth a read if any clients have disclosed neurodivergence, or if this territory comes up in the practicum.

The piece examines why motivational interviewing works particularly well with neurodivergent clients: its non-prescriptive stance, tolerance of ambivalence, and avoidance of confrontational change pressure align with how neurodivergent people tend to process and act on information. The structural features that make MI effective for behavior change may make it especially well-suited for clients whose nervous systems resist directive instruction.

Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy 'Changed My Relationship' with Trauma, Veteran Says

Notice the language: not 'healed' or 'cured,' but 'changed my relationship with trauma.' That is integration language. The event does not change; the orientation toward it does. A useful frame for talking about what transformation actually looks like without overclaiming what any single modality can deliver.

A CBC profile of a veteran who underwent psilocybin-assisted therapy describes a fundamental shift in how he experiences his trauma. The account emphasizes the therapeutic relationship and ongoing integration work that followed the session, rather than the session itself -- which aligns with the understanding that set, setting, and integration support are where the real work happens. The story closes the edition on a human note rather than a research one.
