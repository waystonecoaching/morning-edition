Differentiating moral injury from posttraumatic stress disorder: Implications of the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

The moral injury / PTSD distinction has always mattered clinically, but now there is a diagnostic code scaffolding it. For a coach working near the line, this sharpens how you frame the difference between what belongs to treatment and what belongs to integration work.

A peer-reviewed paper in the Journal of Traumatic Stress examines how the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code creates a new framework for distinguishing moral injury from PTSD. The authors argue that conflating the two distorts both treatment planning and outcome measurement, and that the Z-code offers a path toward more precise clinical and research differentiation.

Try this: This is scope-of-practice boundary material. Worth reading before your next session where moral injury vs. trauma symptomology is live in the room.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: Reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for posttraumatic stress disorder

Read this before the next two stories. The Journal of Traumatic Stress is not a hostile venue — this is a credibility reckoning from within the field. The MDMA space is under methodological stress and the FDA rejection has not resolved the underlying evidentiary questions.

A commentary piece in the Journal of Traumatic Stress arguing that the MDMA-assisted psychotherapy field needs to move from a posture of therapeutic advocacy to rigorous evidentiary discipline. The authors call for methodological tightening, replication standards, and clearer separation of blinding confounds — a direct response to the field's credibility problems following the FDA non-approval.

Try this: This is the frame you need when clients or partners ask about MDMA therapy. Know the state of the evidence, including its limits.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Crisis Response, Community Led peer support teams have answered 20,000 calls since 2023

Community-led models routing around the clinical system are moving volume. 20,000 calls is not a pilot — it is a service. Worth tracking as a model and as evidence for the peer-support argument.

A community-led crisis response peer support program in British Columbia has handled 20,000 calls since launching in 2023 — without clinical staff as the primary responders. The program routes calls to trained peers rather than 911 or hospital emergency services, and the volume suggests demand that the clinical system is not meeting.
