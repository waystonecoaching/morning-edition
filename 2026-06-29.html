Comorbid PTSD and alcohol use disorder is the clinical reality for a significant slice of the veteran population Mark works with. Read the methodology before citing it -- comorbidity trials are harder to interpret than single-diagnosis outcomes, and the field is under credibility pressure right now.

New research out of a Rhode Island station report examines MDMA-assisted therapy for veterans presenting with both PTSD and alcohol use disorder. The combination represents one of the most treatment-resistant profiles in the veteran mental health space. Early data suggests clinically meaningful reduction in both symptom clusters, though the study's scope and design details need direct review before drawing conclusions.

It does not fully reckon with the relational nature of moral injury repair.

Exploring Ecologies of Resilience in Filipino Frontliners' Experiences of Stress and Coping

A qualitative study published today in Frontiers in Psychology examines how Filipino frontline workers (healthcare and emergency response) construct and maintain resilience during sustained high-stress operations. The research challenges individualistic resilience frameworks and finds that resilience is experienced as a product of dynamic interactions between the individual and broader collective, relational, and environmental contexts. Cross-cultural but theoretically generative.

Dominant resilience literature has viewed resilience as an individual psychological capacity or internal resource -- this study explores resilience as a product of dynamic interactions of the individual with broader collective, relational, and environmental contexts.

Published today. The 'ecologies of resilience' framing challenges the dominant model in first responder training: resilience is not an internal resource you build in a person, it is a phenomenon that emerges from the relational and environmental system around them. This reframes what coaching intervention is actually targeting.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Stepped-Care Treatment Delivered via Telehealth Reduces Alcohol Use in Patients with Liver Disease

Stepped-care telehealth with a Motivational Interviewing backbone is producing meaningful alcohol use reduction even in medically compromised patients -- a population with low treatment uptake. For Mark's work with substance use in first responders, the mechanism matters: stepped care plus telehealth removes the access barrier that kills uptake in shift workers who won't walk into a treatment center.

A study found that a stepped-care treatment model delivered via telehealth significantly reduced alcohol use in patients with liver disease, a population that typically shows low engagement with behavioral interventions. Motivational Interviewing was central to the model. The telehealth delivery format and low-barrier entry structure are the transferable elements for first responder populations.
