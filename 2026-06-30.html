Psilocybin Could Become the First FDA-Approved Psychedelic Therapy Within Months

If psilocybin clears FDA approval this year, it redraws the map for every coaching client asking about legal access to psychedelic-assisted therapy. The timeline matters for informed conversations before clients make decisions based on rumor.

Medical Daily reports that Compass Pathways' psilocybin product for treatment-resistant depression could receive FDA approval within months, following Phase 3 trial data submission. If approved, it would be the first federally sanctioned psychedelic therapeutic agent. Legal access would likely be confined to clinical settings with a formal diagnosis, not open to coaching or informal use.

Try this: Read critically. Compass's Phase 3 data had mixed results in earlier analyses. FDA approval would expand legal access but also narrow the therapeutic context to clinical treatment with a diagnosable condition, not a coaching context. Know the distinction before advising clients on options.

Many Veterans in the Dark About Disclosure of Psychedelic Therapy to VA, Study Finds

This is the disclosure question that will arrive in coaching conversations. Veterans who use psychedelic therapy outside VA systems do not know whether telling their provider creates a record that affects benefits, security clearances, or standing. First responders face the same ambiguity with department EAPs and fitness-for-duty structures.

Stars and Stripes reports on a study finding that most veterans who have pursued psychedelic-assisted therapy outside VA systems have no clear guidance on whether, when, or how to disclose this to their VA providers. Many veterans opted not to disclose out of fear of consequences to their benefits or medical record. The study calls for clearer VA guidance and provider education on how to receive this information without punitive documentation.

Try this: If a client is weighing disclosure, this piece validates the ambiguity they are navigating. It is not avoidance. There are structural reasons to be careful. Refer them to a veteran-focused advocate or attorney if the stakes are high.
Source: Stars and Stripes

MAPS is bringing psychedelic crisis assessment training directly to first responders. The content covers de-escalation for adverse psychedelic reactions. This is the intersection of Mark's two practice areas: fire service culture and psychedelic-assisted integration coaching.

Behavioral Health in the Workplace: Supporting Fire Fighter Well-Being

Institutional betrayal is gaining traction in fire service research, and this IAFF webinar frames psychological safety as an organizational factor shaping mental health. For coaching work inside Phoenix Fire, that framing matters when a client's distress is systemic rather than individual.

IAFF announced a webinar on psychological safety, workplace incivility, and institutional betrayal as organizational factors shaping mental health in first responders. The framing names the department itself as a factor in member mental health, distinct from the inherent hazards of the work. Institutional betrayal, when command structures fail members at critical moments, is treated as a specific harm requiring specific acknowledgment rather than general resilience training.

Try this: When a client's distress is rooted in what the department did or did not do during a critical incident, naming institutional betrayal specifically can open a different conversation than moral injury alone. The two can coexist but call for different responses.

Eight Weeks of Guided Slow Breathing Alters Stress Responses in Veterans

Eight weeks of paced breathing produced measurable physiological change in veterans' stress response. This is the polyvagal argument made empirical: autonomic state is trainable, and the window for meaningful change is eight weeks of consistent daily practice.

PsyPost covers a study in which veterans completed an 8-week guided slow breathing protocol. Researchers measured changes in heart rate variability and self-reported stress reactivity before and after. Results showed significant shifts in parasympathetic activation markers and perceived stress. The effect size was clinically meaningful without pharmacological intervention.

Try this: When a client asks about concrete regulation practices, this study supports slow paced breathing at 4 to 6 breaths per minute as a tier-one intervention with measurable HRV benefit over 8 weeks of consistent daily practice.
Source: PsyPost

Compassion Fatigue Confronting Moral Injury: Healing Clinicians

Compassion fatigue and moral injury are often conflated, and that conflation costs people. Compassion fatigue is depletion from sustained giving. Moral injury is the fracture from witnessing or participating in something that violated a person's moral framework. The treatment paths diverge, and the distinction matters for coaching conversations.

Pharmacy Practice News covers growing clinical literature on how compassion fatigue and moral injury overlap and differ in healthcare workers. The piece examines how systems are beginning to differentiate the two in response programs, noting that moral injury requires structured acknowledgment rather than self-care interventions. The scope is clinical but the conceptual framework transfers to first responders. Scope note: the interventions discussed are clinical in nature. Use the framing for coaching conversations; refer clients to clinical resources for treatment.

Source: Pharmacy Practice News

No. 10 · First Responder MH · 2026-06-27

A Bench Beneath a Shady Tree Is a Lifeline for Firefighters

Retired firefighters in Orlando did not build a program or write a protocol. They put a bench outside and sat down with people. That is about the oldest form of human care there is, and the Desert Fathers would recognize it as the original of what we now call peer support.

Retired Orlando firefighters established The Bench 988 as an intentionally low-stakes peer space for active firefighters. No forms, no documentation, no appointment required: a bench, a presence, and the 988 number posted nearby. The founders describe the goal as reducing the distance between a firefighter in distress and the moment of reaching out, by making contact feel like sitting down with someone rather than seeking help.

Source: Firefighter Nation
