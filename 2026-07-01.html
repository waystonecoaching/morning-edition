New VA Trial of MDMA Psychedelic for PTSD: What Veterans Need to Know

The VA is running a new MDMA trial for PTSD, and Military.com is briefing veterans on what it means. Read this with your coaching hat on, not your advocate hat: the Lykos phase 3 data problems are not resolved, and a new VA-sponsored trial does not erase them. Worth tracking because your clients will ask about it and you need the current picture.

The VA has launched a new clinical trial testing MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in veterans, representing a federal re-engagement with psychedelic-assisted treatment after the FDA rejected Lykos's 2024 application. The piece explains the trial structure, eligibility, and what participation would mean for veterans. It is written for a lay audience and does not critically assess the methodology or prior data integrity concerns in the field.

Try this: If any current or prospective client asks about MDMA for PTSD, frame it accurately: the VA trial is promising but early, regulatory approval is not imminent, and there are outstanding questions about the prior data.

Behavioral Health in the Workplace: Supporting Fire Fighter Well-Being

The IAFF webinar framing is significant: psychological safety, workplace incivility, and institutional betrayal in the same sentence. Institutional betrayal is not a soft HR concept -- it is a documented trauma mechanism. When a firefighter's department fails to protect or mishandles a critical incident, the betrayal compounds the original injury. This framing is clinical territory, but the pattern is something you see routinely in coaching.

The IAFF announced a webinar examining how organizational factors shape mental health outcomes in first responders, with particular attention to psychological safety, incivility, and institutional betrayal as distinct but overlapping mechanisms. The framing signals a shift in how the union is approaching behavioral health, moving away from individual resilience programs and toward structural accountability. The webinar is designed for department leadership and peer support teams.

Try this: Scope edge: institutional betrayal work with individual clients can tip quickly toward trauma reprocessing rather than coaching. If this comes up in a session, name the structural piece and the personal response clearly, and assess whether a clinical referral is warranted before doing deep work on the betrayal wound.
Source: IAFF

Eight Weeks of Guided Slow Breathing Alters Stress Responses in Veterans

Slow breathing research sits at the edge of polyvagal theory's clinical applicability. Eight weeks is a short but meaningful window. The question from a coaching standpoint is whether this produces autonomic change that sustains outside the practice, or whether it is a skill that requires continued use. That distinction matters for how you introduce breathwork to clients.

A study found that eight weeks of structured slow breathing practice produced measurable changes in stress response physiology in veterans, including altered heart rate variability and self-reported stress reduction. PsyPost covers the findings for a general audience; the original methodology and sample size details would need to be checked before citing in client-facing work.

Try this: Paced breathing at roughly 5 to 6 breaths per minute is the consistent signal in the literature for HRV improvement. If you are not already using a structured breathing protocol with clients who present in chronic sympathetic or dorsal states, this is another data point supporting that it works.
Source: PsyPost

Many Veterans in the Dark About Disclosure of Psychedelic Therapy to VA, Study Finds

This is the piece to read today. Veterans pursuing psychedelic therapy outside the VA often do not know what they are required to disclose, what could affect their benefits, or what the actual risk is. When you are working with first responders who are federal employees or carry security clearances, this is not a theoretical concern. It is a real coaching boundary issue that requires a referral, not an opinion.

A study covered by Stars and Stripes found that many veterans pursuing psychedelic-assisted therapy are unaware of potential disclosure obligations to the VA, and that guidance from VA representatives on this question has been inconsistent. The lack of clarity creates real risk for veterans who may inadvertently jeopardize benefits or face complications in their care relationship. The researchers recommend clearer federal guidance.

Try this: Scope boundary: if a client who is a federal employee or veteran asks about psychedelic therapy and their job or benefits, name what you do not know, flag that this is legal territory, and refer them to a VA benefits counselor or attorney before they proceed.
Source: Stars and Stripes

Healing the Soul: FLW Chaplains Receive Important Moral Injury Training

The Army training chaplains in moral injury recognizes that some wounds require a soul framework to name them, not just a symptom framework. The Desert Fathers called a related state acedia -- the distress of the inner life when it cannot reconcile what the hands have done or what the eyes have seen. That the Army is naming this as moral injury and sending chaplains to work it is not a small thing.

Fort Leavenworth chaplains participated in moral injury training designed to equip them to support soldiers struggling with the ethical and spiritual wounds of service. The training emphasizes the role of spiritual care in addressing moral injury, which clinical models often underaddress. Army.mil describes the program as filling a gap between clinical mental health services and the pastoral role.

Source: Army.mil
