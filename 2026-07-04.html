The Psychedelic Therapies Executive Order On Approval and Clinical Readiness

The federal machinery is now officially ahead of the clinic. JAMA weighing in on the April executive order matters because it names the real bottleneck: not approval speed but clinical readiness. Who delivers these treatments, in what settings, with what training? Integration support sits directly downstream of that gap.

A JAMA viewpoint examines the executive order accelerating psychedelic therapy review. The order directs FDA priority vouchers for qualifying psychedelics with breakthrough designation, compressing review from roughly a year to one or two months, and opens a Right to Try pathway for compounds like ibogaine. FDA has since issued vouchers to companies studying psilocybin for depression and methylone for PTSD. The authors' focus is whether the delivery system can be ready when approval lands: trained practitioners, appropriate settings, and monitoring.

Try this: Track the clinical readiness debate, not the approval headlines. The workforce question is where integration coaching gets defined, or defined out.

MDMA and psilocybin therapy rules have been relaxed. Here’s what might happen next

Australia is three years into authorized psychedelic prescribing and just loosened supervision requirements. It is the closest thing the field has to a preview of post-approval America. The training-adequacy concern the authors raise is the same one the JAMA piece names from the other direction.

The Conversation reports that Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration relaxed its rules so the authorizing psychiatrist can leave the room after administering the drug, with a therapist remaining with the patient. Australia became the first country to authorize psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression and MDMA for PTSD in 2023. The authors note evidence for both remains limited and mixed, the treatments are still experimental in character, and it is unclear whether authorized practitioners are adequately trained for the loosened supervision model.

Source: The Conversation

Chicago mental health response teams see 911 calls plummet

A peer city's alternative response program is faltering on dispatch integration, which is the least glamorous and most decisive piece of the model. Chicago's CARE teams lost access to police dispatch terminals and their call volume collapsed. For anyone working a behavioral crisis role inside a fire department, this is a case study in how these programs die: not from bad clinical work but from severed plumbing. Worth watching against how Phoenix routes its calls.

EMS1 reports Chicago's CARE (Crisis Assistance Response and Engagement) dispatch volume has plummeted. Reporting around the program shows dispatches fell roughly 70 percent in its second year, dropped again after Chicago PD reclaimed the portable dispatch terminals the teams used to spot psychiatric calls, and CARE ultimately reached fewer than 1 percent of the 96,000 calls dispatchers flagged as potentially mental health related last year. The city announced a citywide expansion in May even as the underlying dispatch problem persists.

Try this: Compare against Phoenix's dispatch pathway for the behavioral health units. The failure mode here is upstream of the crews.

More states expand PTSD treatment options for first responders

State-level policy is moving on first responder PTSD faster than the federal clinical picture. Note the spread: cost coverage, employment protection, and psychedelic research access are all being treated as one problem. Scope note: everything here is treatment territory. Coaching sits alongside these pathways, not inside them, and clients will start arriving with these options on the table.

Stateline surveys 2026 legislation expanding PTSD treatment options for first responders. Ohio created a Post-Traumatic Stress Injury Commission to help cover treatment costs. Maryland now protects firefighters and EMS workers from employment discrimination over lawful off-duty medical marijuana use. Connecticut expanded Yale's psilocybin-assisted therapy pilot to any state resident 18 or older who meets clinical criteria. Missouri advanced, but did not pass, a bill allowing veterans and first responders in approved studies to receive psilocybin and ibogaine under medical supervision.

Try this: Check where Arizona sits in this wave. If a client asks about these programs, the referral map should already exist.

No. 05 · Polyvagal · 2026-06-28

Eight weeks of guided slow breathing alters stress responses in veterans

Small study, but it lands exactly on the autonomic substrate claim: the intervention changed sympathetic reactivity under stress, not resting state. That distinction matters when describing to clients what breath practice actually does. It does not sedate the system; it changes how the system meets load.

PsyPost covers a randomized trial of 25 veterans with PTSD assigned to eight weeks of daily device-guided slow breathing or a sham device. Resting heart rate, blood pressure, and resting sympathetic nerve activity were unchanged. Under a mental stress test, though, the breathing group's sympathetic nerve activity spiked far less than at baseline, and the effect held even when the device was not in use. Published in the American Journal of Physiology. The sample is small; read it as converging evidence, not proof.

Try this: Clean client-facing language: the practice trains the stress response, not the resting state. Pairs well with the polyvagal framing already in use.
Source: PsyPost

A&E pressures putting nurses at risk from ‘moral distress and moral injury’

Nursing Times reports on warnings that sustained emergency department pressures in the UK are putting nurses at risk of moral distress and moral injury: being forced by conditions to deliver care they know falls short of what patients need. The piece treats the injury as a predictable output of the system's operating state rather than a failure of individual coping.

The injury is a system output, not a character flaw. Where that framing lands decides whether the fix is a wellness app or a staffing model.

This sits squarely in the structural-critique lineage of moral injury work: the Litz and Shay line where the wound comes from betrayal and impossible conditions, not individual fragility. Emergency nursing is naming what fire and EMS culture often still codes as personal weakness. The framing fight matters because it determines whether the fix is resilience training or system repair.

MAPS & State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

This is the exact intersection of the behavioral crisis role and the psychedelic field: MAPS and Colorado's Natural Medicine Division training first responders to assess and de-escalate psychedelic crises. As state programs expand, these calls land on fire and police first. A curriculum like this is worth reading closely, both for the Phoenix role and for how Waystone talks about crisis-adjacent integration work.

MAPS announced a partnership with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to deliver its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training free to first responders statewide. The program uses videos and assessments covering evidence-based education and de-escalation practice for responders encountering people in psychedelic crises, timed to Colorado's regulated natural medicine rollout.

How Motivational Interviewing Supports Neurodivergent People

MI shares a spine with the non-directive stance: the client's own reasons for change carry the work, and the practitioner's job is evocation, not persuasion. The neurodivergence angle is a useful stress test of that stance, since directive approaches fail fastest with clients whose processing does not match the script. Reads as craft maintenance for anyone working inquiry-first.

Psychology Today looks at how motivational interviewing serves neurodivergent people. The core argument runs through MI's founding commitments: autonomy, collaboration, and drawing out the person's own motivation rather than imposing an agenda, which fit clients for whom standardized directive approaches routinely misfire.

Opinion: Patriotism after moral injury

An Independence Day essay on what love of country means after the country has been the instrument of your wound. This is the oldest question in the moral injury literature, older than the term: Shay found it in Achilles. For the holiday, a slower read. The Desert Fathers would recognize the shape of it: the wound and the love held in the same hand, neither canceling the other.

A Times Leader opinion essay for July 4th on holding patriotism after moral injury. The author works the tension between genuine love of country and the betrayals, personal and institutional, that veterans and others carry home. The piece argues the two can coexist, and that the coexistence itself, not the resolution, is the honest position.
