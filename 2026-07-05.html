New VA trial of MDMA for PTSD: what veterans need to know

The VA is now running its own MDMA-assisted therapy trial rather than waiting on outside sponsors, which is the institutional shift that matters more than any single result. Note the design choices: treatment-resistant PTSD with co-occurring alcohol use disorder, both required, randomized against an active placebo with structured therapy. That is a harder and more honest test than the MAPS phase 3 designs that drew FDA criticism. Clinical territory throughout; the coaching-relevant part is what members will be reading and asking about.

The VA began enrolling veterans in May in its first-ever clinical trial of MDMA-assisted therapy, targeting treatment-resistant PTSD with co-occurring alcohol use disorder. Roughly 80 veterans will be randomized to MDMA-assisted therapy or an identical therapy structure with an active placebo, led from the Providence VA Medical Center with a second site in Connecticut. It sits inside a wider VA psychedelic research push of 19 active studies, with results not expected until around 2030.

Try this: Note the 2030 results date. When a client cites this trial as a reason to seek MDMA now, that timeline is the grounding fact.

Bipartisan congressional bill would codify the psychedelics executive order into law

Executive orders die with administrations; statutes do not. A bipartisan move to write the April order's accelerated-approval machinery into law would make the current trajectory durable regardless of who holds the White House in 2029. Marijuana Moment is an advocacy-adjacent outlet, so treat the bill's prospects as reported enthusiasm until it moves through committee.

A bipartisan congressional bill would codify the April executive order on psychedelic therapies, locking its fast-track review provisions into statute rather than leaving them as revocable executive action. The order it would preserve directed FDA to prioritize psychedelics with breakthrough designations for serious mental illness, a mechanism that produced three priority vouchers within a week of signing.

Source: Marijuana Moment

N.M. 911 authority named in lawsuit over veteran's suicide

This is the failure mode your unit exists to prevent, laid out in a court filing. A veteran called 911 stating he had a gun and a plan, the call was classified priority-high suicidal subject, and the responding officers allegedly cancelled fire and EMS, shut off body cameras, and left without contact. A second warning call produced no response at all. Suicidality and crisis intervention are squarely clinical-adjacent ground; the coaching-relevant layer is system design, accountability, and what co-response models are for.

A wrongful death and civil rights lawsuit names the city of Las Cruces, two police officers, and the Mesilla Valley Regional Dispatch Authority in the April 2025 suicide of Army veteran Jacob Garcia, one month home from Iraq. Garcia told dispatchers he had a gun and planned to kill himself; the suit alleges the dispatched officers never made contact, cancelled incoming fire and ambulance units, and idled in a parking lot before leaving. A friend's follow-up report of a suicidal social media post allegedly generated no additional response before Garcia's brother found him dead.

Try this: Worth a close read against your own dispatch-to-contact protocol. Every allegation in the complaint maps to a checkable step in a behavioral crisis response chain.

Burnout, moral injury, and the healthcare system: clinical reflections on July 4th

This sits in the structural-critique lineage, Litz and Shay rather than resilience programming: moral injury as a wound the system inflicts, not a deficit in the worker. The healthcare frame transfers cleanly to fire and EMS, where the betrayal is usually departmental rather than clinical. Useful language for the distinction coaching can hold, meaning, values, and vocational identity, versus what needs clinical repair.

A Psychiatric Times reflection uses the July 4th holiday as an entry into burnout and moral injury among healthcare workers, locating the injury in institutional constraints and betrayals of what practitioners believe medicine owes patients rather than in individual failure to cope. It presses the distinction between burnout and moral injury because the remedies differ: one calls for rest and boundaries, the other for repair of meaning and trust.

Source: Psychiatric Times

How motivational interviewing can reduce self-doubt in social work

The interesting move is turning motivational interviewing inward: the practitioner's own ambivalence and self-doubt as the target rather than the client's. That maps onto the non-directive spine of BTTY work, where trusting the client's inner knowing requires the coach to have met their own doubt first. Useful framing for L2P practice.

A Community Care piece argues that motivational interviewing techniques can help social workers address their own self-doubt, not just client ambivalence. The MI stance of evoking rather than instructing and treating resistance as information transfers to the practitioner's inner dialogue, loosening the paralysis that self-criticism produces in high-stakes casework.

AI's prescription for moral injury

Watch this one skeptically. The pitch is that AI can relieve moral injury by taking administrative burden off clinicians, which quietly reframes moral injury back into workflow burnout, exactly the move the structural-critique lineage warns against. If the injury is betrayal of what care owes people, no documentation tool touches it. The piece is useful as a specimen of how institutions prefer to talk about this wound.

A Medscape piece explores the argument that AI tools, by absorbing documentation and administrative load, could reduce the conditions producing moral injury in medicine. The framing treats moral injury largely as an efficiency and burden problem, a reading that sits in tension with the research tradition treating it as a wound of conscience and institutional betrayal.

Source: Medscape

Healing the soul: Fort Leonard Wood chaplains receive moral injury training

The Army teaching its chaplains to work with moral injury is a quiet admission that some wounds present at the altar rather than the clinic. The Fathers knew this lane: the wound of conscience needs confession, witness, and meaning, not only treatment. Read it for how an institution formally divides soul care from clinical care, a division your own work lives inside every week.

Chaplains at Fort Leonard Wood received training in recognizing and responding to moral injury, the wound that follows from actions or omissions violating one's moral convictions. The training positions chaplains as a first line for soldiers whose distress is spiritual and moral in character, working alongside rather than in place of behavioral health.

Source: army.mil
