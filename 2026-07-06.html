Defense Department Would Report on How Psychedelic Mushrooms Can Benefit Military Members Under New Congressional Bill

Congress asking the Pentagon to produce a formal report on psilocybin for service members is the slow institutional machinery turning, distinct from the executive order fireworks of the past week. Report language in a bill is how skeptical institutions buy time while acknowledging pressure; it commits no one to treatment but forces the department to put findings on paper. Watch whether the report requirement survives markup, since that is where these provisions usually die quietly.

A new congressional bill would require the Department of Defense to issue a report on how psychedelic mushrooms could benefit military members, adding to a string of legislative moves nudging military medicine toward psychedelic research. The provision is a study mandate, not an authorization of treatment, and its fate depends on surviving the committee process.

New Jersey Recognizes Open Water Lifeguards as First Responders

Who counts as a first responder is not a semantic question; it decides who gets access to presumptive coverage, peer support infrastructure, and behavioral health benefits when the bad call finally lands. Lifeguards work drownings, pediatric codes, and body recoveries with none of the cultural scaffolding a firehouse provides. Every expansion of the definition widens the population your field is responsible for, and most of the newly included have no idea what cumulative exposure will do over a career.

New Jersey has formally recognized open water lifeguards as first responders, a statutory change that brings them under the umbrella of protections and benefits attached to that designation. The recognition follows years of advocacy noting that ocean lifeguards routinely perform rescues and medical interventions comparable to EMS work.

Stepped Alcohol Treatment via Telehealth Reduces Alcohol Use

Stepped care is the interesting mechanism here: start light, escalate only when the lighter intervention fails, deliver it all remotely. That architecture matters for responders who will not walk into a clinic but will take a video call from their truck. Scope line, plainly: alcohol treatment is clinical territory. The coaching-relevant part is the referral map, knowing what the first step of stepped care looks like so you can point a client toward it early instead of after the DUI.

Coverage of a study finding that a stepped alcohol treatment model delivered through telehealth reduced alcohol use among participants. The stepped design begins with lower-intensity intervention and escalates to more intensive treatment only for those who do not respond, making it a resource-efficient model for populations reluctant to engage traditional treatment settings.

Try this: Check whether any Arizona telehealth providers run a stepped model. A concrete first-step referral that does not require walking into a clinic lowers the barrier for the members most likely to refuse help.

An Art-Based Labyrinth Activity Workshop: A Qualitative Study with Counselor Candidates

The labyrinth is older than the research on it; the church at Chartres put one in the floor eight centuries before acceptance and commitment therapy borrowed the shape. What this study adds is practitioner formation: counselors-in-training using embodied, nonverbal practice to meet their own interior material before they sit with anyone else's. That ordering, work on your own heart first, is the desert's rule and BTTY's, and it is worth noticing when the academic literature rediscovers it.

A qualitative study in Frontiers in Psychology examines counselor candidates' experiences of an art-based labyrinth workshop framed through acceptance and commitment therapy. Participants used art-making and the labyrinth structure as a nonverbal medium for exploring emotions, values, and inner experience, with the authors positioning the practice as a tool for practitioner self-awareness and formation.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology

AMR Nurse Triage Line Reduces Low-Priority 911 Calls in California County

Same family as the Chicago story from earlier this week: route the call to the right resource and the system stops grinding its people down on runs that never needed a truck. Call volume is a mental health variable, even though nobody files it under behavioral health. Every low-acuity run deferred is sleep protected, and chronic sleep debt is upstream of half the behavioral health caseload in any busy system.

EMS1 reports that an AMR nurse triage line in a California county is successfully diverting low-priority 911 calls away from ambulance dispatch, with nurses assessing callers and routing them to appropriate alternatives. The model reduces unnecessary transports and frees EMS units for higher-acuity responses.

No. 07 · First Responder MH · 2026-07-06

They Are No Longer Really Flashbacks; They Are Memories: A Brief Intervention for Intrusive Memories

A qualitative study examines women's experiences of a brief single-session intervention for childbirth-related intrusive memories, combining structured memory recall with a visuospatial task. Participants described their intrusions losing their present-tense, reliving quality and settling into ordinary autobiographical memory, and the paper traces how they made sense of that shift.

They are no longer really flashbacks; they are memories.

The title quote is the whole finding: the shift from flashback to memory is the shift from reliving to remembering, which is what integration means at the level of the nervous system. The population is postpartum mothers, not responders, but intrusive memory is intrusive memory, and the phenomenology transfers. Scope line, plainly: this is a clinical intervention targeting traumatic memory, therapy territory, not coaching. Its value to you is the language and the mechanism, not the technique.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Veterans Seek Help for Moral Injury After War

Mainstream press picking up moral injury as a category distinct from PTSD is worth tracking, because national-paper coverage is how the language reaches families and kitchen tables before it reaches department policy. The piece sits in the Litz and Shay lineage: the wound as violated conscience rather than fear conditioning. When a client's spouse has read this framing in USA Today, the intake conversation starts from different ground.

USA Today reports on veterans seeking help for moral injury, the distress that follows participating in, witnessing, or failing to prevent acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs. The story distinguishes moral injury from PTSD, covers emerging treatment and support approaches, and features veterans describing wounds of conscience that standard trauma treatment did not reach.

PTSD Is Not Weakness: A Modern Look at Post Traumatic Stress Disorder

SOFREP writing this for a special operations readership is the stigma conversation happening inside the culture rather than being preached at it from outside. The outlet's credibility with its audience is the entire mechanism; the same words from a wellness consultant would bounce off. It is the operational-applied frame, not the structural critique, and for once that is the right tool: permission to seek help, written in the dialect of the people who need it.

A SOFREP piece written for the military and special operations community reframes PTSD as a physiological injury rather than a character failure, walking through what the condition actually does to the brain and body and pushing back on the weakness narrative that keeps operators from seeking treatment. The argument is aimed at the culture's own stigma, delivered by an outlet that culture trusts.

Finding Connection, Identity and Resilience in Traditional Embroidery

A close about slow handwork carrying memory and identity across rupture. The mechanism is old and the Fathers knew it: repetitive, embodied practice as a vessel for what cannot be said, the hands holding what the mouth cannot. For people whose losses are structural and ongoing, making something beautiful and continuous is not distraction from grief but a way of remaining a self inside it. The prayer rope teaches the same thing by a different thread.

The piece profiles members of the Palestinian diaspora who practice tatreez, traditional embroidery, as a way of holding connection, identity, and resilience across displacement. Stitching patterns passed down through generations becomes both a link to place and a steadying, meditative practice in its own right.
