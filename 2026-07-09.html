Compass Closes Out Phase 3 With 6-Month COMP006 Data, Eyes H1'27 Launch

This is the closest thing the psilocybin field has to a pivotal readout, and it matters because durability is where earlier trials wobbled. Read it as a company managing a narrative toward a 2027 launch, not as settled science. The relevant question for coaching-adjacent work is not whether the drug performs in a trial, but what the standard of care looks like on the other side of approval.

Compass Pathways reported 26-week data from Part B of COMP006, its larger Phase 3 trial (n=581) of COMP360 synthetic psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression. The company frames the effect as rapid and durable out to six months and says it is eyeing a first-half-2027 commercial launch. This is the second of two Phase 3 studies, positioning the program for a regulatory filing. The report is a company milestone, so the framing leans favorable; independent read of the full dataset is still owed.

Chicago Mental Health Response Teams See 911 Calls Plummet

This is Mark's role, scaled to a city. CSF Galanin and Noradrenaline Downregulation by Psilocybin Therapy in Major Depressive Disorder

This is the kind of primary literature the rubric favors over advocacy press: a mechanistic study reaching for what psilocybin actually does to stress-system chemistry. Galanin and noradrenaline sit close to the autonomic machinery Mark works with in polyvagal terms. It is a small, biomarker-level finding, not a clinical claim, and should be read as a thread, not a rope.

Researchers report that psilocybin therapy was associated with downregulation of cerebrospinal fluid galanin and noradrenaline in patients with major depressive disorder. The finding points at a stress-and-arousal-system mechanism rather than a purely serotonergic story. It is early biomarker work with the usual limits of small samples and CSF measurement, but it begins to connect the subjective shift patients report to measurable neurochemistry.

Source: Neuropsychopharmacology (Nature)

Eight Weeks of Guided Slow Breathing Alters Stress Responses in Veterans

This is the whole polyvagal wager in a single intervention: change the breath, change the autonomic state, change the response. Eight weeks of guided slow breathing shifting stress physiology in veterans is exactly the mechanism Deb Dana's work describes, tested in the population Mark serves. Cheap, portable, no prescription. The kind of finding that survives the hype cycle.

A study found that eight weeks of guided slow breathing altered stress responses in military veterans, shifting autonomic markers toward greater regulation. The intervention is low-cost and self-administered, targeting the nervous system directly rather than the cognitive story. It is consistent with polyvagal accounts of breath as a vagal lever, and its practical value is that anyone can begin tonight.

Try this: Give a first responder client one anchor: extend the exhale longer than the inhale for a few minutes before sleep. Name it as autonomic downshift, not relaxation, so it lands with someone fluent in physiology rather than sentiment.

New VA Trial of MDMA for PTSD: What Veterans Need to Know

The VA has opened a new trial of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD and published guidance on what veterans should understand about eligibility and expectations. The move signals institutional willingness to keep testing MDMA despite the FDA's earlier rejection of the commercial application. The reporting is oriented to veterans as prospective participants, so it emphasizes access over the unresolved questions about blinding and durability that sank the prior filing.

The standard of care for responder PTSD is being rebuilt inside a more skeptical room than the one that first welcomed it.

The VA running its own MDMA-for-PTSD trial matters because the VA sets the de facto standard of care for the veteran and, by extension, first responder PTSD population. This is the space under the most credibility stress after the 2024 FDA rejection of the MAPS application. Read it as the field trying to rebuild an evidentiary case inside a more skeptical regulatory climate.

Motivational interviewing shares DNA with non-directive inquiry: meet ambivalence without pushing, let the person voice their own reasons for change.
