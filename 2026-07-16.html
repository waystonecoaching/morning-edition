PTSD Among Paramedics: A Systematic Review of Prevalence and Protective Factors

This is Mark's population, read at the level of the whole literature. Paramedics carry a PTSD burden well above the general public, and a systematic review is the cleanest map of what raises the risk and what buffers it. For a coach working the first responder line, the protective factors are the material worth building a practice around.

The review pools prevalence estimates for PTSD across paramedic populations and finds rates substantially elevated relative to the general public. It sorts the drivers into risk factors, including cumulative critical-incident exposure, organizational strain, and prior trauma, and protective factors such as social support, perceived control, and meaning in the work. The authors note wide methodological variation across studies, which widens the prevalence range and complicates direct comparison. The takeaway for practitioners is that protective factors are modifiable, which is where non-clinical support has room to work.

Try this: Pull the protective-factors list into your intake framing. Social connection, perceived control, and meaning are the levers a coach can actually touch without crossing into treatment.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Differentiating Moral Injury From PTSD: The DSM-5-TR Moral Problems Z-Code

This is the distinction Mark works inside every week. Moral injury and PTSD share a surface, but the DSM-5-TR now carries a Z-code for moral problems, which gives clinicians and coaches a shared vocabulary for the difference between a fear-conditioned nervous system and a violated conscience. The sharper the boundary, the cleaner the referral decision.

The paper argues that moral injury is not a subtype of PTSD but a distinct construct rooted in the transgression of moral belief, whether by one's own action, another's, or betrayal by leadership. The authors examine how the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code lets clinicians name moral suffering without medicalizing it as a disorder. They map where the two conditions overlap in symptom presentation and where they diverge in mechanism and repair. The practical upshot is that fear-extinction protocols aimed at PTSD may miss what moral injury actually needs.

Try this: When a client's pain reads as guilt, shame, or betrayal rather than threat, note the moral-injury frame in your session record and check whether the work belongs with a chaplain or clinician.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

FDA Rejects MDMA as a Treatment for PTSD, Calling for Additional Study

The federal door on MDMA-assisted therapy stays shut for now, and the reason given is evidence, not ideology. For clients who arrive already convinced the medicine will fix them, this is the reality check that keeps expectation honest. Scope edge: MDMA work is clinical territory, not coaching. A coach holds integration and the meaning that follows, never the dosing.

The report covers the FDA's decision to decline approval of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, citing the need for additional study before the standard of care can shift. Regulators pointed to unresolved questions about trial design and the strength of the efficacy data. The decision leaves the compound in research and expanded-access channels rather than routine clinical use. It lands as the field absorbs sustained scrutiny of its pivotal trials.

From Therapeutic Promise to Evidentiary Discipline: Reassessing MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy

Read this one slowly. It is the sober peer-reviewed counterweight to advocacy press, written from inside a trauma journal rather than a press release. The field is under real credibility stress, and this commentary names why. For a coach whose clients treat the medicine as certain, this is the discipline that keeps the conversation grounded.

The commentary calls for a shift from advocacy-driven enthusiasm to rigorous evidentiary standards in evaluating MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD. It examines methodological weaknesses in the pivotal trials, including functional unblinding and expectancy effects, that complicate claims of efficacy. The authors do not dismiss the therapy's potential but insist the burden of proof has not yet been met. Their argument is that premature adoption risks both patients and the standing of the field.

Psilocybin Reduces Fear Memory and Restores Neuroplasticity in the Hippocampus and mPFC

This is the mechanism layer under the policy noise. A preclinical study showing psilocybin dampening fear memory and reopening plasticity in the fear circuit is the kind of primary literature worth reading before the advocacy summaries built on top of it. Preclinical means animal work, so hold the human translation loosely.

The study reports that psilocybin reduced conditioned fear responses and promoted neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex, two regions central to fear regulation. The authors frame the finding against the frequent comorbidity of PTSD and depression, proposing a shared plasticity-restoring mechanism. Effects were measured at the neural and behavioral level in a controlled model. The work is mechanistic and preclinical, so it informs theory rather than clinical practice.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

Someone Who Gets It: A Critical Incident Team Offers Peer Support After the Worst Calls

Peer support done at the department level, from the people who ran the same call. This is the layer Mark's work sits beside, not inside. The value of the piece is field-level signal on what makes a critical incident team land: proximity, shared language, and someone who does not need the story explained.

The report profiles a county critical incident team that deploys trained peers to support first responders after traumatic calls. The model rests on shared experience, the sense that the person offering support has stood in the same place. Team members describe checking in after hard runs and normalizing the reactions that follow. The piece frames peer support as an early, low-barrier touch that can catch strain before it hardens.

Try this: If you coach responders inside a department with a peer team, map where your integration work hands off to and from theirs. Clear seams prevent both duplication and gaps.

Burnout, Moral Injury, and the System: Clinical Reflections for the Fourth of July

The reflection reframes clinician burnout as moral injury produced by systemic conditions rather than personal weakness. It argues that when institutions force people to act against their conscience, the resulting harm is structural and cannot be resolved by wellness programs aimed at the individual. The author draws on the healthcare setting but the logic carries to any high-duty profession. The prescription points upstream, toward the conditions of the work.

The injury is in the system, not the person.

This piece plants a flag in the structural camp of the moral injury debate. It reads burnout not as individual depletion but as the predictable result of a system that asks people to violate their own standards of care. That framing sits in active tension with the resilience-and-performance lineage, which locates the fix in the person. Both are in the field; this one names the institution.

Source: Psychiatric Times

The Most Important Part of Therapy Happens After You Leave the Room

This is the integration thesis in a mainstream outlet, and it maps directly onto Mark's lane. The change is not made in the session; it is made in the days that carry it, the small practices that turn insight into a different life. That gap between the room and the rest of the week is exactly where a coach works.

The article argues that the decisive work of therapy occurs between sessions, in how a person carries and applies what surfaced in the room. It points to the daily practices, reflection, and small behavioral shifts that consolidate change. Insight alone is described as necessary but not sufficient without integration into ordinary life. The frame is a useful corrective to the belief that the appointment itself is where healing lives.

Try this: Name the between-sessions work explicitly with each client. Assign one small, repeatable practice that carries the session into the week rather than leaving it in the room.
