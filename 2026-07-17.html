Differentiating moral injury from PTSD: the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

This is the distinction your whole practice rests on. Moral injury is not PTSD, and the field is now arguing about it inside the diagnostic manual itself. The DSM-5-TR added a Z-code for moral problems, and this paper works the seam between a fear-based disorder and a conscience-based wound. For a coach working the line, the language matters: naming a moral wound is not diagnosing a disorder.

The authors examine how moral injury and PTSD overlap and diverge, and what the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code does to that boundary. The argument is that conflating the two obscures the distinct mechanism of moral injury, which turns on transgression and betrayal rather than threat and fear. They press the implications for assessment and for what counts as treatment versus accompaniment.

Try this: Reread how you describe moral injury to first responder clients. If your framing borrows PTSD's threat-and-fear vocabulary, it may be pointing them toward the wrong door.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD

This is the field policing itself, in the trauma literature rather than the trade press. After the FDA's rejection, the question is no longer whether MDMA helps but whether the trials proved it to the standard the claim requires. Read this as the sober counterweight to the funding enthusiasm above. Scope note: this sits directly on the substance-use and high-acuity edge, so hold it as evidence review, not endorsement.

The commentary reassesses MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD through the lens of evidentiary rigor rather than therapeutic hope. It catalogs the methodological problems that dogged the pivotal trials, from functional unblinding to expectancy effects, and argues the field owes itself stricter discipline before scaling. The tone is corrective from within, not dismissive.

Compass closes out Phase 3 with 6-month COMP006 psilocybin data, eyes 2027 launch

The largest psilocybin depression program in the world just posted its durability data, and the read matters for the whole field's credibility. COMP006 ran 581 patients across three dose arms. Whether the 25mg effect holds at 26 weeks tells you how much of the psychedelic promise is acute versus lasting. Industry press, so read the framing carefully and go to the primary data before repeating any number to a client.

Compass Pathways released 26-week data from Part B of its COMP006 Phase 3 trial of COMP360 psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, the larger of its two pivotal studies at 581 participants. The company frames the results as supporting a first-half 2027 launch push. The trial compared 1mg, 10mg, and 25mg doses given three weeks apart.

Psilocybin reduces fear memory and restores neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex

This is the mechanism underneath the headlines, in a preclinical model. If psilocybin dampens fear memory and regrows connectivity in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, that is a plausible substrate for why integration windows open the way clients describe them. Preclinical work is a long way from the coaching chair, so hold it as mechanism, not proof of practice.

Researchers report that psilocybin reduced conditioned fear memory and restored markers of neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex in a model of comorbid PTSD and depression. The finding links the acute drug effect to structural recovery in circuits governing fear and mood regulation. The authors position neuroplasticity as a candidate mechanism for the therapeutic effect.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

A systematic review of PTSD prevalence, risk, and protective factors among paramedics

This is your population, measured. A systematic review of PTSD among paramedics is exactly the evidence base a first responder coach should keep current, both for the prevalence figures and for the protective factors, which is where non-clinical work like yours can actually move the needle. The protective-factor column is your lane; the prevalence column is the referral case.

The review synthesizes studies on how common PTSD is among paramedics and what raises or lowers the risk. It separates the drivers of vulnerability from the factors that appear to protect, including social support and organizational conditions. The aim is a clearer evidence base for prevention and support in a population with sustained trauma exposure.

Try this: Pull the protective-factors list and check it against what your intake actually screens for. Those are the levers a coach can pull without crossing into treatment.

The closest thing to polyvagal thinking in today's feed, and it points the same direction: below the diagnosis is the nervous system's way of processing signal. This is the transdiagnostic move Dana's work has been making from the polyvagal side.

The paper argues that sensory processing differences are a transdiagnostic mechanism linking autism, ADHD, and PTSD, examined through a neuropsychoanalytic lens. Rather than treating the three as separate categories, it looks at what they share at the level of developmental organization and nervous-system regulation. The framing is conceptual, aimed at reorienting how the conditions are understood rather than reporting new trial data.
