Differentiating moral injury from PTSD: implications of the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

This is the distinction you work with every week, now being pressed into diagnostic language. The DSM-5-TR added a Z-code for moral problems, and this paper argues moral injury is not a subtype of PTSD but its own wound of conscience. For a coach working the moral-injury line with firefighters and medics, the nosology matters: it shapes what you name as autonomic pattern versus what belongs to a clinician's assessment. The clinical-adjacent edge is real here, so treat the Z-code as their instrument, not yours.

The authors examine the newly added DSM-5-TR Z-code for moral problems and use it to separate moral injury from posttraumatic stress disorder. They argue the two are distinct: PTSD centers on fear and threat, while moral injury centers on guilt, shame, and betrayal of what a person holds sacred. The piece works through what the coding change means for assessment and for research that has long folded moral injury into PTSD measures.

Try this: Re-read how you describe moral injury on intake. Keep your language autonomic and integrative; leave diagnosis to the Z-code and the clinicians who hold it.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD

The corrective the field needed, written from inside the trauma-research house rather than the advocacy wing. After the FDA declined MDMA in 2024, the question became whether the science can meet the promise. This commentary calls for evidentiary discipline: functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and therapist influence all need honest accounting before the standard of care shifts. If you sit with responders considering this path, this is the sober read to hold alongside the hope.

The commentary steps back from the enthusiasm around MDMA-assisted psychotherapy and asks the field to hold it to ordinary evidentiary standards. It names the methodological problems that dogged the Phase 3 program: participants and therapists usually knew who received the drug, expectancy shaped outcomes, and the therapy component was hard to standardize. The authors argue that promise is not evidence, and that rigor now protects both patients and the eventual case for the treatment.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

VA, HHS to increase psychedelic therapy research for PTSD and military mental health

A federal signal, not a settled science. VA and HHS moving to expand psychedelic research for veteran and military PTSD is the policy weather that will reach responder populations next. Read it as funding and momentum, not as a change in the standard of care. The gap between what agencies are willing to study and what a coach can responsibly integrate is exactly the ground you stand on.

The Department of Veterans Affairs and Health and Human Services announced expanded support for psychedelic therapy research aimed at PTSD and other military-related mental health conditions. The move directs federal attention and funding toward trials of compounds like psilocybin and MDMA in veteran populations. It marks a shift in official posture after years of caution, while leaving the underlying evidence questions open.

A systematic review of PTSD prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors among paramedics

Your old population, measured. A systematic review of PTSD across paramedics gives you the base rates and the protective factors worth naming with clients who came up through EMS. The protective side is the coaching-relevant half: social support, meaning, and perceived control tend to buffer. That maps cleanly onto felt safety and connection without crossing into treatment.

This review pools studies on posttraumatic stress disorder among paramedics to estimate how common it is and what raises or lowers the risk. It identifies repeated trauma exposure, organizational stress, and low support as risk factors, and points to social support, meaning-making, and coping resources as protective. The authors note wide variation in measurement across studies and call for more consistent assessment.

Try this: Pull the protective-factor list into your intake framing for EMS-background clients; it gives felt-safety work an evidence spine.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 05 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-06-28

Psilocybin reduces fear memory and restores neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex

Primary literature, animal-model, mechanism-level. This is the kind of study the advocacy press flattens into a promise. It suggests psilocybin acts on fear memory and neuroplasticity in regions tied to PTSD and depression, which is mechanistically interesting and clinically premature. Keep the distance between a mouse hippocampus and a firefighter's integration session in view.

The study reports that psilocybin reduced conditioned fear and restored markers of neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex in a preclinical model of comorbid PTSD and depression. The findings point to a possible biological pathway by which the compound could support fear extinction. The work is mechanistic and preclinical, a step in understanding rather than evidence for practice.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

New study reveals urgent need for expanded mental health services for first responders

The demand-side picture behind your whole practice. A new study documents the gap between responder mental health needs and the services actually reachable. For a solo coach, the read is market and mission at once: the unmet need is large, and much of it sits upstream of clinical care, which is where coaching lives. Note the acuity in the gap; some of what goes unserved is high-risk and belongs to clinicians.

The study finds that first responders face a wide and largely unmet need for mental health support, with existing services falling short of demand. It points to barriers of access, stigma, and capacity that leave many responders without reachable care. The authors call for expanded and better-tailored services across fire, EMS, and law enforcement.

When the shift comes home: what firefighter families are telling us

Drawing on research into firefighter families, the piece describes how the stress and trauma of the job reach spouses and children, often unintentionally. Families report emotional withdrawal, hypervigilance carried home, and strain that goes unspoken. The author argues for raising awareness and building support that includes the household, not only the responder.

The trauma of the job does not stay on the truck. It comes home, and the family carries what no one names.

The part of the job that follows the truck into the driveway. Research on secondary and household stress names what shift families already carry: the trauma of the job spreads through the home, often without anyone naming it. This is squarely coaching ground, connection and communication rather than treatment, and it opens a door with clients who will talk about their family before they talk about themselves.

Try this: Add one family-facing question to your session template: what does your shift bring home that no one names?

MAPS and Colorado partner to deliver free harm reduction training for first responders

A concrete program at the intersection you actually stand in: first responders and regulated psychedelics. Colorado's natural-medicine rollout means responders will encounter people in psychedelic states in the field, and MAPS is training for that reality. Whatever you make of MAPS as an advocacy body, the harm-reduction frame here is operational, not evangelical. Worth knowing as Arizona watches its neighbors.

MAPS announced a partnership with Colorado's Department of Revenue to provide free harm reduction training for first responders under the state's regulated natural medicine program. The training prepares responders to safely support people experiencing psychedelic effects, focusing on de-escalation and care rather than enforcement. It reflects the practical questions that arise once a state legalizes supervised psychedelic use.

Someone who gets it: Scott County's Critical Incident Team offers peer support after traumatic calls

Peer support working the way it should, at the level of the call. A county critical incident team shows the mechanism you trust: the person across from you has stood where you stand. This is the ventral-vagal truth in institutional form, felt safety delivered by someone who gets it. Field-level signal, not a press release about a program that exists only on paper.

The piece profiles Scott County's Critical Incident Team, a peer support group that responds after traumatic calls to check on the responders involved. Team members are fellow responders trained to listen and to normalize the aftermath, offering contact from someone who has lived the same work. The model rests on the simple power of being met by a person who understands.

The most important part of therapy happens after you leave the therapist's office

A quiet close on the theme you live: integration is the work, and it happens between sessions. The piece argues that the hour matters less than what a person carries out of it and practices in ordinary life. That is the whole premise of transformation as path rather than event, and it is the same wisdom the Fathers kept: the cell teaches, but only to the one who returns to it daily.

The article makes the case that lasting change from therapy comes not from the session itself but from what a person does with it afterward. It points to between-session practice, reflection, and small acts of application as where insight becomes durable. The therapist's office is a starting point; the ordinary week is where the work is finished.

Source: Time Magazine
