VA and HHS move to expand psychedelic therapy research for PTSD and military mental health

The federal posture on psychedelics for PTSD keeps shifting toward the veteran and military population, which is the population adjacent to your first responder work. Policy and funding moving first, with the standard of care still catching up, is the exact gap where integration coaching gets pulled toward clinical territory. Watch the framing in these announcements: government backing is not the same as settled evidence.

The Department of Veterans Affairs and Health and Human Services signaled an expansion of federally supported research into psychedelic-assisted therapy for PTSD and other military-related conditions. The move continues a year of warming federal interest after a long stretch of caution. It funds studies rather than approving treatments, so the practical effect for practitioners is upstream of the clinic for now.

Try this: If a first responder client raises VA psychedelic access, keep the line clean: you coach integration, you do not administer or recommend a substance. Know your local referral path before the question comes.

Differentiating moral injury from PTSD and the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

This is the structural-critique lineage speaking in a diagnostic register. The field is trying to name moral injury as its own thing rather than a subtype of PTSD, and the DSM adding a Z-code for moral problems is the paperwork catching up to what the fire service has known in its body for years. The distinction matters for you because moral injury and PTSD ask for different responses, and conflating them sends a client down the wrong road.

The paper argues moral injury is conceptually distinct from PTSD and examines how the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code could support that separation in assessment and documentation. Where PTSD centers on fear and threat, moral injury centers on transgression, betrayal, and the collapse of a moral framework. The authors weigh the risk of over-medicalizing a moral and spiritual wound against the practical need for a shared clinical label.

Try this: Revisit how you screen for moral injury versus trauma at intake. A betrayal-and-leadership-failure story is not a fear-conditioning story, and the coaching arc differs.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD

Read this next to today's federal funding story and hold the tension. Inside the trauma-research journals, the mood on MDMA has cooled from advocacy to scrutiny after the FDA's rejection and the questions about trial conduct. This is the credibility-stress signal the field keeps sending, and it deserves more weight than a press release. The standard of care for responder PTSD is being litigated in these pages, not on the news wire.

This commentary calls for a shift from enthusiasm to rigor in evaluating MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD. It reflects the post-2024 reckoning after regulators declined approval and raised concerns about functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and therapist conduct in the trials. The argument is not that the approach fails, but that the evidence has been asked to carry more than it can currently bear.

MAPS and Colorado partner on free psychedelic harm-reduction training for first responders

This sits squarely on the line you walk as a behavioral crisis specialist. A state and MAPS are training first responders to assess and intervene in psychedelic crises, which is the operational edge of a movement that mostly talks about clinics. It is worth knowing the curriculum exists, both as a professional reference and as a sign of where responder culture and psychedelics are converging in the field.

MAPS and Colorado's Natural Medicine Division launched a program delivering the Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide, free of charge. The curriculum uses video and assessment modules to teach evidence-based de-escalation and harm reduction for people in acute psychedelic distress. It is aimed at the responder scene, not the therapy room.

Try this: Skim the MAPS crisis-intervention framing for your Phoenix Fire role. Even outside a legal-access state, the de-escalation content maps onto behavioral crisis calls.

How polyvagal theory explains the freeze when fight and flight are both off the table

A general-audience explainer of Porges's dorsal shutdown, the state your clients reach when neither fighting nor fleeing is available. Useful less for new science than for language: how a lay outlet renders the freeze response tells you how a client might already understand it before they walk in. Note where the popular telling flattens neuroception into a tidy story, because that is where you will need to add nuance.

The piece walks through the polyvagal account of immobilization, the dorsal vagal state that drops the body into stillness when active defense is impossible. It traces the idea to Porges's 1990s work and frames the shutdown as an ancient, involuntary survival strategy rather than a failure of will. It is a primer, so it favors clarity over the ongoing scientific debate about the theory's mechanisms.

Compass closes Phase 3 with six-month psilocybin data, eyes 2027 launch

The most-watched psilocybin program in the treatment-resistant-depression race just posted its durability data, and durability is the whole question with single-dose psychedelics. Read the numbers with the same discipline the trauma journals are now demanding of MDMA. A commercial timeline for 2027 is a business signal, not a clinical verdict.

Compass Pathways reported 26-week data from Part B of COMP006, its larger Phase 3 trial of COMP360 psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, which enrolled 581 participants across 1 mg, 10 mg, and 25 mg doses. The company framed the results as supporting durability and pointed toward a possible launch in the first half of 2027. Independent readers will want the full effect sizes and dropout figures before drawing conclusions.

When the shift comes home: what firefighter families are telling us

Matthew Thorpe reviews emerging research on how firefighters transmit job stress and trauma to their families, often without intending to. The article raises awareness of secondary traumatic stress in spouses and children and the ways shift culture and emotional armoring follow a responder through the front door. It calls for departments and families to name the pattern rather than absorb it silently.

The trauma does not clock out when the shift ends. It rides home in the truck.

The trauma does not stay at the station. This piece turns toward the household, the secondary exposure that spouses and children absorb from the person who carries the calls home. For a solo practice working the responder population, the family system is often the unspoken third party in the room, and naming it can open the real work.

Someone who gets it: a county critical-incident team offers peer support after traumatic calls

The line that names the whole model is in the headline: someone who gets it. Peer support works on the neuroception of safety, one nervous system co-regulating another, which is polyvagal theory doing its quiet work in a firehouse without the vocabulary. This is the field-level signal worth tracking, not another awareness campaign.

Scott County stood up a Critical Incident Team that deploys trained peers to support first responders after traumatic calls. The report emphasizes that the value lies in being met by someone who has stood in the same boots, which lowers the threshold to reach out. It frames peer contact as a first line of defense that sits before, not instead of, clinical care.

No. 09 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-07-04

Burnout, moral injury, and the healthcare system: clinical reflections

This is the structural-critique frame at its sharpest: burnout is not a personal failure of resilience but often a moral injury inflicted by the system a person serves. For responders, that reframe lands hard, because it moves the wound from what is wrong with me to what was asked of me and by whom. Hold this in tension with the operational resilience literature; both are true at different altitudes.

Written as a July 4th reflection, the piece distinguishes burnout from moral injury within healthcare and argues that much of what gets labeled burnout is a moral wound caused by systemic betrayal and institutional demand. It draws on the Litz and Shay lineage of moral injury to relocate the problem from individual coping to organizational failure. The clinical takeaway is that resilience training alone cannot heal a structurally inflicted wound.

Source: Psychiatric Times

No. 10 · Coaching Practice · 2026-07-16

The most important part of the work happens after you leave the room

A fitting close, because it names the ground you actually work: integration. The change does not happen in the fifty minutes, it happens in the seven days after, in the ordinary hours where insight either takes root or evaporates. This is the Being True to You claim in secular dress, and the Desert Fathers said it plainly long before the therapists did. The cell teaches what the conversation only points toward.

The article argues that the durable effects of therapy come not from the session itself but from what a person practices between sessions, in daily life. It frames integration and repeated small acts as the real mechanism of change, with the clinician's role being to seed practices that continue outside the room. The insight generalizes cleanly to coaching, where the space between meetings is where transformation is tested.

Try this: For each active client, name one small between-session practice this week. Integration is the work; the session only opens the door.
Source: Time
