From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD

This is the counterweight to the federal enthusiasm above, and it comes from inside the trauma field rather than from the sidelines. For a coach whose clients may arrive already sold on MDMA as a cure, this is the sober voice worth internalizing. Hold the promise and the skepticism in the same hand.

It reflects the credibility stress the field absorbed after regulatory rejection, functional-unblinding concerns, and questions about trial conduct. The authors press for cleaner designs and more honest reckoning with expectancy effects before clinical claims harden. This is a discipline policing itself, which is the healthiest kind of critique.

Always On: the hidden cost of firefighter readiness

Sleep is the substrate under the substrate. Before any autonomic map holds, the body has to have rested, and shift work quietly guarantees it has not. Palumbo names the chronic health tax of interrupted sleep in a culture that treats readiness as a virtue and rest as weakness. This is the felt-safety precondition failing at the level of physiology, not psychology.

Nicholas Palumbo details how irregular shifts and frequent night calls drive high rates of sleep disturbance among firefighters, with downstream chronic disease and mental health risk. He connects the always-on posture of the job to measurable physiological cost. The piece treats sleep disruption as an occupational hazard rather than a personal failing. It reads as an operational-wellness argument, squarely in the applied camp.

Try this: When a client's dysregulation resists every nervous-system tool, ask about sleep architecture first. No ventral vagal practice competes with three years of broken REM.

A systematic review of the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors of PTSD among paramedics

This is your population, measured. Systematic reviews are the closest thing the field offers to a clear-eyed base rate, and paramedic PTSD is the exact terrain Waystone works. The protective-factors half of the review matters more to a coach than the prevalence half, since protection is where non-clinical work has real purchase. Read the protective factors as a map of where coaching can honestly help.

The review pools studies on PTSD prevalence among paramedics and sorts the risk and protective factors that emerged across them. It confirms elevated rates relative to the general population and identifies exposure load, organizational stress, and support access as recurring variables. Protective factors cluster around social support, meaning, and perceived organizational backing. It is a base-rate document, useful for grounding claims about how common this actually is.

Differentiating moral injury from PTSD: implications of the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

The distinction you draw between moral injury and PTSD now has a diagnostic hook to hang on. A Z-code is not a disorder; it is the manual admitting that a moral wound is not the same object as a fear-conditioned one. This matters for scope: moral injury lives partly in the territory of meaning, conscience, and repair, which is closer to the contemplative and coaching work than to symptom treatment. Litz and Shay have argued this line for years, and the nosology is catching up.

The article examines the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code and what it means for separating moral injury from PTSD as distinct constructs. It argues the two overlap but are not interchangeable, and that conflating them muddies both assessment and care. The Z-code gives clinicians a way to name moral suffering that is not itself a mental disorder. The distinction has direct consequences for who treats what, and how.

Burnout, moral injury, and the healthcare system: clinical reflections

The reflection distinguishes burnout from moral injury in healthcare, locating the latter in systemic and institutional failure rather than personal weakness. It resists the wellness-industry tendency to treat exhaustion as an individual coping problem. The author writes from clinical experience, using it to argue that repair has to reach organizational conditions, not just the depleted worker. It sits firmly in the betrayal-and-leadership-failure lineage.

Burnout is framed as the worker's deficit. Moral injury names the betrayal that produced it.

This one plants a flag in the structural-critique camp and does not apologize for it. Burnout is often sold as a resilience deficit in the worker; moral injury names the betrayal in the system that produced it. That reframe moves the locus of repair off the individual and onto the institution, which is uncomfortable and often correct. When a client blames themselves for burning out, this is the counter-narrative to have ready.

The work is preclinical and mechanistic, examining biological pathways rather than clinical endpoints. It strengthens the plausibility case without settling the efficacy question.

The ancient nerve: revisiting Porges and the stillness response

The dorsal shutdown is the state your clients have the least language for and the most shame about. Fight and flight at least look like doing something; the freeze looks like failure from the outside and feels like death from the inside. Porges gave that collapse a name and a nervous system, which is the beginning of self-compassion for the responder who went still when they wanted to act. This is the frame that most changes how a client narrates their worst call. The stillness the Desert Fathers sought and the stillness the body imposes are not the same thing, and the difference is worth sitting with.

The piece revisits Stephen Porges and polyvagal theory, focusing on the dorsal vagal stillness response that engages when fight and flight are both foreclosed. It presents the immobilization state as an ancient, involuntary survival strategy rather than a choice or weakness. The framing is accessible science journalism aimed at the general reader. It is a useful reminder of the construct even as the theory itself remains contested in parts of the research community.

Source: Space Daily
