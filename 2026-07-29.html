Differentiating Moral Injury from PTSD: The DSM-5-TR Moral Problems Z-Code

This sits on your scope line. The DSM-5-TR now offers a Z-code for moral problems, a way to name moral injury without coding it as a disorder. That distinction is the ground you coach on: a wounded conscience is not a diagnosis. This is clinical-classification territory, so the value to you is the language, not a new intervention.

The paper argues that moral injury and PTSD overlap but are not the same construct. PTSD is fear-based and diagnostic; moral injury centers on guilt, shame, and betrayal, and the DSM-5-TR routes it through a Z-code for moral problems rather than a disorder label. The authors map where the two diverge in symptom profile and treatment implication, and caution against collapsing moral injury into PTSD by default.

Try this: When a responder describes a call that violated what they hold sacred, listen for the moral register (betrayal, shame, the words 'I should have') rather than the fear register. Naming the difference out loud often lands as relief.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

PTSD Among Paramedics: A Systematic Review of Prevalence, Risk, and Protective Factors

This is the population you came from and the one you serve. A pooled read on how common PTSD is among paramedics, what raises the risk, and what buffers it. The protective factors are where your work lives.

The review synthesizes prevalence estimates for PTSD in paramedics across multiple studies and finds rates well above the general population. Risk climbs with cumulative critical-incident exposure, organizational stress, and low perceived support. Protective factors cluster around social support, meaning-making, and a workplace culture that permits help-seeking. The authors note wide methodological variance, so point estimates should be held loosely.

Try this: When you build a support case for a department, protective-factor data carries further than prevalence numbers. Lead with what buffers, not only with what wounds.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

A New Assessment Protocol Aims to Identify Moral Injury Risk Earlier

Bath researchers describe a screening approach meant to flag moral injury before it hardens. Worth knowing it exists, with one caution: this is a clinician's assessment instrument, not a coaching tool. It marks the line you refer across, not one you administer.

The piece reports a new protocol designed to identify people at elevated risk of moral injury, aimed at earlier recognition in high-exposure occupations. It frames moral injury as distinct from ordinary stress and potentially devastating when unaddressed. The tool is positioned for clinical and organizational screening rather than self-application.

Try this: Know your referral path. If a screening like this surfaces in a partner clinic or department, you want the clinician's name ready before you need it.
Source: University of Bath

From Therapeutic Promise to Evidentiary Discipline: Reassessing MDMA-Assisted Psychotherapy for PTSD

This is the critical read the field needs. With MDMA therapy under regulatory pressure, the literature is doing the sober work of separating what the data actually support from what advocacy claimed. High-acuity, standard-of-care territory. Read it as a corrective, not a retreat.

The commentary calls for evidentiary discipline in evaluating MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD. It revisits methodological weaknesses in the trial record, including functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and therapist influence, and argues the reported effect sizes ran ahead of the rigor. The authors do not dismiss the modality; they insist the standard of proof match the stakes before it enters routine care for populations like veterans and first responders.

Try this: When a client asks whether MDMA therapy is proven for PTSD, you can now cite a peer-reviewed source that holds both the promise and the doubt. That is more honest than the marketing on either side.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Psilocybin Reduces Fear Memory and Restores Neuroplasticity in the Hippocampus and Medial Prefrontal Cortex

Mechanism, not marketing. A preclinical look at how psilocybin may act on fear memory and plasticity in regions tied to PTSD and depression. Useful for understanding the integration window you work in, with the standing caveat that cellular and animal findings do not translate cleanly to a person in your chair.

The study reports that psilocybin reduced fear-memory expression and promoted neuroplastic changes in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex, regions implicated in PTSD and depression. The authors frame plasticity as a candidate mechanism for the therapeutic effects observed clinically. The findings are preclinical, so causal claims about human outcomes remain premature.

Source: Journal of Psychopharmacology

Eli Lilly Moves Into Psychedelics With a $3.8 Billion Acquisition

Mainstream pharma has entered the room. A deal this size signals that psilocybin and related compounds are moving from the fringe into the pipeline of the largest drug makers. Read it as a market and access signal, not a clinical endorsement. Capital moving does not mean evidence maturing.

Eli Lilly is acquiring AtaiBeckley, a psychedelic medicines developer, in a deal reported at 3.8 billion dollars. Commentators frame it as a milestone in psychedelics crossing into mainstream pharmaceutical development. The move raises questions about pricing, protocol standardization, and who controls access as these compounds scale.

Try this: Watch what happens to protocol design when big pharma sets the terms. The low-touch, high-throughput model that suits a balance sheet is not the integration-rich model your clients need.

No. 07 · Polyvagal · 2026-07-27

Stephen Porges on Polyvagal Theory and the Body's Older Circuits

In the interview Porges walks through the core of polyvagal theory: the autonomic nervous system reads cues of safety and threat below conscious awareness, and the body organizes into ventral-vagal connection, sympathetic mobilization, or dorsal shutdown accordingly. He emphasizes that chronic anxiety is often the nervous system stuck in defensive readiness. Felt safety, rather than willpower, is what allows a shift back toward regulation.

When fight and flight are both off the table, an older circuit pulls the body down toward stillness.

A plain-language sit-down with the theory's originator. Little new in the model itself, but the phrasing is worth borrowing for how you explain neuroception and the ventral-sympathetic-dorsal hierarchy to a responder who has never heard the words.

Try this: Borrow his framing next time you explain why a client cannot think their way out of a shutdown. The circuit does not answer to argument; it answers to cues of safety.
Source: The Singju Post

When the Shift Comes Home: What Firefighter Families Are Telling Us

The wound rarely stays at the station. This piece surfaces what spouses and families carry: the hypervigilance that follows the responder through the door, the flatness, the second shift the household works. Your client's regulation is a system, not a solo act.

Drawing on family accounts, the article describes how the stresses of firefighting migrate into home life through withdrawal, irritability, sleep disruption, and emotional distance. Families report feeling like secondary casualties with little support of their own. The piece argues that responder wellness programs which ignore the household miss where much of the damage settles.

Try this: Ask the responder in front of you how the shift shows up at home. The answer often opens the real work faster than any question about the calls themselves.
Source: Firefighter Nation

NAU Researchers Test Whether an AI Chatbot Can Do Health Coaching

Close to home, and worth watching with a clear eye. Arizona researchers are testing whether a chatbot can deliver health coaching to students. The scope-of-practice question underneath is the one your whole trade will face: which part of coaching is presence, and which part is protocol a machine can run?

Researchers at Northern Arizona University are studying whether an AI chatbot can serve as a health coach for students, measuring whether automated conversation can support behavior change. The work sits inside a broader push to scale coaching through technology. It leaves open the question of what is lost when the coach is not a person who can be affected by what they hear.

The Delay Is Not the Same as Being Fine

A quiet closing note. A person can take terrible news with a steady face and find their hands shaking an hour later. The composure was real, and so is the tremor. The body keeps its own time. For those who pride themselves on holding it together on scene, this is worth sitting with.

The piece explains delayed stress responses through the lens of the autonomic nervous system: composure in the moment can give way to physical activation once the immediate demand passes. The delay reflects how the body sequences survival before it processes the aftermath. Outward calm is not evidence that a person was unaffected.

Source: Space Daily
