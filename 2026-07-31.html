Psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD: the open-label pilot, primary paper

The trial the whole wire is quoting now has its primary paper, and the primary paper is where you read it. This is the ground your BTTY integration work sits beside, which is exactly why the sample size, the open-label design, and the absence of a control arm matter more to you than the remission figure. When the headlines say the word resolves, the study says feasibility and preliminary. Hold the gap.

Communications Medicine publishes the safety, feasibility, and preliminary outcomes of psilocybin-assisted therapy in a small cohort of veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD. It reports strong symptom reduction and a favorable safety profile in an open-label pilot with no placebo group. The authors frame it as a signal worth a controlled trial, not as settled evidence. The reporting layered on top of it has been running ahead of what an early study can support.

Try this: If a client or peer raises the trial, point them to what it is: an open-label pilot with no control arm, promising and not standard of care.

Differentiating moral injury from PTSD: the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

Moral injury and PTSD travel together, yet they are not the same wound. The field now has a DSM-5-TR Z-code for moral problems to mark the seam. For the firefighter carrying a call that violated his own code, that seam is where integration actually begins, and naming it correctly is the first act of respect.

The paper argues that moral injury and PTSD overlap but remain distinct, and examines how the DSM-5-TR Z-code for moral problems lets clinicians name the moral dimension without folding it into a fear-based diagnosis. It positions moral injury as a disturbance of conscience and meaning rather than threat. The authors weigh what the Z-code can and cannot carry. It is a nosological argument with direct bearing on how you frame the work with responders.

Try this: Carry one clean distinction into peer conversation: fear-based suffering and conscience-based suffering ask different things of a person.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD

This is the counterweight to the psilocybin headline above, and it belongs on the same page. The MDMA field went from therapeutic promise to a regulatory reckoning, and this commentary reads that arc as a discipline the whole psychedelic space now has to accept. For a practitioner working the integration side, the lesson is method, not mood.

The commentary revisits MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD after the regulatory setbacks, arguing the field must trade advocacy for evidentiary rigor. It names functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and reporting problems as the reasons enthusiasm outran the data. The authors call for stricter trial design rather than a retreat from the work. It is a sober read from inside the tent, not a dismissal from outside it.

Try this: Keep the standard consistent across compounds: whatever caution you apply to MDMA claims, apply to psilocybin claims too.

The body keeps its own time: calm now can mean shaking later

The right note to close on. A person can take terrible news with a steady face and find their hands shaking an hour later, and the delay is not proof they were fine. This is the autonomic truth the whole issue keeps circling, and it is one the desert tradition already knew: the body confesses on its own clock, and the work is to wait with it rather than rush it.

The piece describes the lag between a threat and the body's response, the calm face that gives way to trembling once the moment has passed. It explains the delay as autonomic rather than as weakness or denial. It reframes the shaking as the nervous system completing what it could not finish in the moment. It is a small, human-scale account of how safety returns after it is lost.
