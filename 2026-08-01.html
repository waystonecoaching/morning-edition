States Move to Let First Responders Use Marijuana and Psychedelics to Treat PTSD

This is the access side of the psychedelic question moving faster than the evidence side, and it lands squarely on the population Mark serves. When a state opens a legal lane for responders before the standard of care is settled, the practical weight shifts onto the people around the responder, coaches and peer teams included. Name the scope edge plainly: legal access is not clinical readiness, and integration support is not the same as sanctioned treatment.

Several states are advancing measures that would let first responders and veterans use cannabis and psychedelics for PTSD, some through medical carve-outs and some through broader access bills. The pattern is legislative momentum outpacing federal scheduling and outpacing the closed-out trial data that would normally precede it. Coverage frames this as compassionate access for a high-burden group, with less attention to who provides the wraparound care.

Try this: If a client raises legal psychedelic access under a new state provision, have a clear line ready on where your role ends and a clinician's begins.

Moral Injury Can Be Devastating: New Assessment Protocol Makes It Easier to Identify Those at Risk

This sits in the structural-critique lineage of moral injury, the Litz and Shay camp, where the wound is not fear but violated conscience. A validated screening protocol is useful, and screening is a clinical act. The value for a coach is conceptual: sharper language for the difference between fear-based trauma and the slow corrosion of having done, or failed to prevent, what one believed was wrong.

The framing keeps moral injury distinct from PTSD, locating it in transgression and betrayal rather than threat. Could COMPASS Pathways' Low-Touch Psilocybin Protocol Quietly Redefine Its Competitive Position

Read this one as market analysis, not clinical news. A shift toward a lower-touch protocol, less therapist time per dose, is partly a science claim and partly a margin claim. That distinction matters for a field already accused of overselling, because reducing the psychotherapy scaffold around a dose is exactly the variable most likely to move outcomes. When you see efficiency framed as innovation, ask what got subtracted.

The article examines COMPASS Pathways narrowing the amount of clinician support wrapped around its psilocybin protocol and what that would mean for cost and scale. The investor angle is explicit: a leaner protocol improves unit economics and competitive standing. Left underexamined is whether stripping therapeutic contact hours preserves the outcomes seen in more intensive trial designs.

Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy for Veterans With Severe, Treatment-Resistant PTSD: Open-Label Pilot Results

This is the primary literature under the headlines, an open-label pilot in Communications Medicine, and it deserves to be read at the source rather than through the wire copy that turned it into a remission slogan. Open-label with a small sample and no blinding is a signal, not a verdict. The 75 percent figure is real and worth respect, and it is also exactly the kind of number the field has been warned about inflating.

The pilot reports safety, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy of psilocybin-assisted therapy in a small group of veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD. Investigators describe a high proportion of participants no longer meeting PTSD criteria after treatment, alongside acceptable tolerability. The authors themselves flag the limits: open-label design, no control arm, and a sample too small to generalize, framing the result as a basis for controlled trials rather than a finished case.

The Body Keeps a Slower Clock: Why the Hands Shake an Hour After the Calm

A quiet close on the same nerve as story five, told at the scale of one body in a parked car. The delayed shake is not weakness or malingering, it is the autonomic system discharging on its own clock after the threat has passed. For a responder culture that reads composure as proof of being fine, this is the correction worth carrying: stillness at the scene says nothing about the cost, which arrives later and asks to be met.

The piece describes the common experience of staying outwardly calm through a crisis and then trembling or breaking down well afterward, and explains it through delayed autonomic discharge. Composure in the moment reflects mobilization holding the system online, while the later shaking is the body completing a stress response it had suspended. The takeaway is that a delayed reaction is not evidence that a person was unaffected.

Try this: When a client reports feeling nothing at the scene and coming apart hours later, name it as normal autonomic timing before anything else.
