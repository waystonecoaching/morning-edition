Psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD: an open-label pilot trial

This is the primary literature behind the week's wave of veterans-and-psilocybin headlines, so read it before the coverage. The population is the one closest to the people you sit with: severe, treatment-resistant PTSD in a service cohort. The signal is genuinely large, and the design is an open-label pilot with no control arm, which is exactly where the field's credibility stress lives right now.

An Ohio State-led team ran psilocybin-assisted therapy in a small group of veterans whose PTSD had not responded to standard care. Symptom reductions on standard measures were substantial, with news coverage citing remission in roughly three of four participants. Because the trial was open-label and uncontrolled, expectancy and selection effects cannot be ruled out, and the sample is too small to generalize. The authors frame it as safety and feasibility groundwork, not proof of efficacy, which is the honest reading.

Try this: When a client raises the veteran psilocybin news, you can affirm the hope and model sober reading in the same breath: strong early signal, small uncontrolled trial, standard of care unchanged for now.
Source: Communications Medicine (Nature)

New assessment protocol makes moral injury easier to identify in those at risk

Moral injury has long suffered from being felt everywhere and measured almost nowhere. A validated way to flag who is carrying it, before it hardens into despair or substance use, is a practical gift to anyone working the first responder line. Note the frame: this is the structural-injury lineage, closer to Litz and Shay than to a resilience-training pitch.

Researchers at Bath describe an assessment approach designed to identify people at elevated risk of moral injury earlier and more reliably. The aim is to move moral injury from a felt but slippery experience toward something clinicians and programs can screen for. Earlier identification opens the door to support before the wound compounds. The work sits in the research-and-measurement camp rather than the applied-wellness camp.

Try this: Watch for the underlying instrument to surface; a validated moral-injury screen could sharpen how you frame intake conversations without drifting into diagnosis.
Source: University of Bath

No. 05 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-07-25

Recovering from moral injury by breaking the silence

The applied counterpart to the Bath measurement piece: less instrument, more the lived path out. Its core claim, that silence is the wound's preservative and speech begins to loosen it, maps cleanly onto Campbell's direct-speech-as-contact and onto the confession tradition you already carry. Read the two moral injury pieces together and the tension between measuring and tending becomes the real content.

The essay argues that moral injury festers in isolation and that recovery starts when the carrier can speak the act or failure aloud to someone who will not flinch. It frames disclosure not as catharsis but as the first repair of moral connection. The path it describes is relational and slow rather than technical. It stays in the meaning-and-identity register where moral injury actually lives.

Try this: The frame is a clean fit for the sensation-feeling-story-want move; naming the story aloud, in felt safety, is where integration starts.
Source: Psychology Today

Porges and the polyvagal account of shutdown: when fight and flight are both off the table

A clean lay explanation of the dorsal branch, the freeze-and-collapse state that the stoic shift culture most often misreads as weakness or laziness. This is the vocabulary you use to describe why a responder goes flat rather than reactive. Worth keeping in view as you refine how you present the autonomic hierarchy to clients and on the Waystone site.

The piece revisits Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory and its central image: an ancient vagal circuit that pulls the body into stillness and shutdown when neither fighting nor fleeing is possible. It explains dorsal collapse as an evolved protective response, not a failure of nerve. The framing helps normalize the flat, checked-out states that follow overwhelming exposure. It keeps the autonomic hierarchy accessible without flattening it into generic calm-down advice.

No. 10 · Contemplative · 2026-07-31

The relationship between trust and humility: a systematic review

A fitting close. Modern psychology, working by PRISMA and grey literature, arrives where the Desert Fathers began: humility is the ground trust grows in. For the coach and the responder alike, the finding is quietly practical, since the felt safety your whole method depends on is built more on humility than on competence displayed.

This systematic review synthesizes research on how humility and trust relate, following PRISMA 2020 and including grey literature to widen the evidence base. From 76 records identified, 56 met inclusion, and the review organizes their findings around four research questions. The pattern that emerges is that humility functions as a condition for trust rather than a byproduct of it. It gives empirical shape to something the contemplative tradition has long held.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
