Psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD: an open-label pilot trial

This is the primary source under the wave of headlines you saw all week (Reuters, Military.com, Ohio State, PsyPost all cover the same trial). Read it before the coverage. It touches your work directly: severe, treatment-resistant PTSD is the population that walks into first responder coaching after everything else has failed, and psilocybin is the intervention most likely to reshape what your clients arrive already having tried.

An open-label pilot at Ohio State reports large reductions in PTSD symptoms among veterans with severe, treatment-resistant presentations following psilocybin-assisted therapy. The design is the signal and the caution in one: open-label means everyone knew what they received, the sample is small, and there is no blinded comparator. The effect sizes are notable and the safety profile is described as manageable within a supported setting. It is a feasibility and preliminary-outcomes study, not a confirmatory trial.

Try this: When a client cites psilocybin results, you can now point to the actual trial rather than the press. Note out loud that open-label pilots measure feasibility, not proof of efficacy.

From therapeutic promise to evidentiary discipline: reassessing MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD

This is the corrective the field needs and the piece to hold next to every 80-percent headline. After the FDA rejection of MDMA-assisted therapy, the question is no longer whether the molecule helps but whether the evidence was ever disciplined enough to bear the weight placed on it. For a coach working near the clinical line, this is the standard of reading to model: promising is not proven, and enthusiasm is not data.

A commentary in the Journal of Traumatic Stress argues the MDMA-PTSD literature outran its own evidentiary base, with functional unblinding, expectancy effects, and therapist-allegiance problems the trials never fully resolved. It reframes the FDA decision not as an obstacle to healing but as a demand for methodological rigor the field skipped. The authors call for blinded designs and independent replication before MDMA re-enters the standard of care. This is the skeptical lineage on psychedelics, and it is worth as much of your attention as the optimistic one.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

New Jersey's Cop2Cop, a peer-staffed crisis and support line for law enforcement, is seeking expanded funding amid rising concern over police suicide rates. The reporting frames peer support as effective but chronically underfunded, dependent on grants and political will rather than stable budget lines. Trained peers, not clinicians, staff the front end, with clinical referral behind them. The tension named here is structural: the intervention responders trust most is the one least reliably paid for.

Try this: If you map referral paths for Phoenix clients, note how this program layers peer contact ahead of clinical handoff. That two-tier structure is a template.

Moral injury can be devastating: new assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

Moral injury is finally getting measurement instruments built for it rather than borrowed from PTSD scales, which matters because the two are not the same wound. This is the operational-applied lineage: build a screen, catch people earlier. Useful to you as language, not as a tool you would administer; the assessment belongs to clinicians, the recognition belongs to everyone who works with responders.

Researchers at Bath describe a new protocol for identifying people at elevated risk of moral injury, distinct from screening for PTSD or depression. The framing is early identification: catch the injury of conscience before it calcifies into avoidance, shame, and withdrawal. The tool targets the specific signature of moral injury, transgression against deeply held values, rather than fear-based trauma. It reflects a broader shift toward treating moral injury as its own category with its own instruments.

Source: University of Bath

A systematic review of the prevalence, risk factors, and protective factors of PTSD among paramedics

This is your former population and your current one, read at the level of the whole literature. A systematic review is worth more than any single study because it tells you what holds across many. For describing risk and protective factors to a paramedic client, this is the evidence base you want under your feet rather than anecdote or a single alarming headline.

A systematic review synthesizes prevalence estimates and risk and protective factors for PTSD among paramedics across the published literature. Prevalence runs well above general-population baselines, with cumulative exposure, organizational stressors, and low perceived support among the recurring risk factors. Protective factors cluster around social support, meaning-making, and workplace culture rather than individual toughness. It is the kind of source that lets you speak about paramedic risk with a whole field behind you, not one study.

Try this: Pull the protective-factors list into your intake language for EMS clients. Naming what buffers risk is more useful than cataloging what causes it.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Route this straight into the discipline the JTS reassessment demands (story 02). No. 07 · First Responder MH · 2026-06-17

Differentiating moral injury from PTSD: implications of the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code

The paper examines the DSM-5-TR moral problems Z-code as a formal hook for distinguishing moral injury from posttraumatic stress disorder. It argues the two conditions share surface features but differ at the root: PTSD is organized around threat and fear, moral injury around guilt, shame, and betrayal. The Z-code gives clinicians a way to name moral suffering without forcing it into a PTSD frame that mistreats it. The stakes are practical, because interventions built for fear extinction do not reach an injury of conscience.

PTSD is a fear circuit. Moral injury is a rupture of conscience. The paths back are not the same road.

The DSM-5-TR added a Z-code for moral problems, and this paper works out what that means for telling moral injury apart from PTSD at the point of diagnosis. This is the structural lineage on moral injury, the one that insists the difference is not academic. For your work it draws the clean line: PTSD is a fear circuit, moral injury is a rupture of conscience, and the integration paths are not the same.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-04

The long way home: moral injury and Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey

A fitting place to end. The oldest story we have about a soldier coming home is a story about how long the coming home takes and how changed the man is when he arrives. Moral injury is not a modern invention; it is the ancient wound the Odyssey was written to hold. This is the human-scale close: the journey back from what the work costs is measured in years, not sessions.

The essay reads Homer's Odyssey, occasioned by Nolan's film adaptation, as one of the earliest and most durable accounts of moral injury and the difficulty of homecoming. Odysseus returns not triumphant but altered, carrying what he did and saw, unable to simply resume his old life. The piece argues the ancient text still describes the veteran's return more honestly than most clinical language. It is a reminder that the wound is old and the way home is long, which is its own kind of consolation.

Source: Modern War Institute, West Point
