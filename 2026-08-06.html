Directly applies

Psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD: an open-label pilot

This is the primary literature the week's headlines are all circling. It sits closest to your line of work: veterans and, by extension, first responders carrying treatment-resistant PTSD. Read the design before the number. Open-label, single-arm, small n, no placebo and no blinding, which means expectancy effects cannot be separated from drug effect. Promising signal, not settled standard of care.

An open-label pilot gave psilocybin with psychological support to a small cohort of veterans whose PTSD had not responded to prior treatment. The authors report large symptom reductions and a favorable safety and feasibility profile, with a sizable share no longer meeting PTSD criteria at follow-up. Because there was no control arm, the effect size should be held loosely until randomized, blinded trials replicate it.

Try this: When a client raises psilocybin, you can point to this as real peer-reviewed signal while naming the open-label caveat. Stay on your side of the line: integration support, not treatment recommendation.
Source: Communications Medicine (Nature)

New assessment protocol makes moral injury easier to identify in those at risk

Moral injury is the fracture underneath much of what walks into your practice, and the field has lacked clean tools to name it. A validated screening protocol matters because it separates moral injury from PTSD proper, which changes how you would frame the work and where the referral line sits. This is the structural Litz-and-Shay lineage rather than the performance-psych frame.

Researchers describe an assessment approach meant to identify people carrying moral injury before the wound consolidates into worse outcomes. The stated aim is earlier recognition and better-targeted support, distinguishing moral injury from adjacent trauma presentations. It is a measurement contribution, not a treatment claim.

Try this: Read moral injury as its own category in intake language, distinct from PTSD, and keep a referral path ready when the material moves past coaching scope.
Source: University of Bath

Written exposure therapy for reducing depressive symptoms in PTSD: a systematic review

Written exposure therapy is short, structured, and low-dropout, which is why it keeps drawing interest for populations that will not sit still for twelve sessions. This review asks whether it moves the depression that rides alongside PTSD, not just the PTSD itself. Worth knowing where the evidence actually stands rather than where the enthusiasm sits.

The review synthesizes trials of written exposure therapy to gauge its effect on depressive symptoms in people with PTSD. It weighs a briefer protocol against standard trauma-focused treatments and reports where the effect is supported and where the evidence thins.

Try this: Know the modality by name so you recognize it when a client's clinician is using it, and coordinate your integration work around it rather than across it.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress
No. 06 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-08-04
80% of soldiers, per headline

MDMA-assisted psychotherapy reduces PTSD in 80% of soldiers, Israeli study finds

Hold this number at arm's length. It comes through advocacy-leaning press, not the primary paper, and the MDMA-for-PTSD field is under real credibility stress after regulatory setbacks and questions about blinding and trial conduct. A striking response rate in soldiers is a reason to read the methods, not to celebrate. The population is soldiers, not first responders, so the transfer is suggestive at best.

An Israeli study is reported to show large PTSD symptom reductions in a majority of treated soldiers using MDMA-assisted psychotherapy. The coverage leads with the response rate; the underlying design, controls, and follow-up window are what would determine whether the figure holds.

Try this: If this comes up with a client, model the skeptical read out loud. Big numbers from small, unblinded trials are a starting question, not an answer.
Source: The Jerusalem Post

How to navigate tough conversations without losing trust as a leader

Ordinary business-press packaging, but the underlying skill is the one Campbell's Getting Real is built on: staying in contact through a hard conversation instead of managing around it. For a solo coach this is also client-facing craft, the difference between challenging a client's reasoning and losing the relationship in the process.

The article offers guidance on holding difficult conversations while keeping trust intact: candor delivered without contempt, staying present to the other person, and separating the hard message from the relationship. Standard leadership fare with a relational core.

Try this: Pressure-test your own hard-conversation habits against this. Where do you soften the message to protect comfort, and where does the comfort cost the client the truth they came for?
