Psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD: an open-label pilot

This is the primary literature under all the week's psilocybin headlines, so read it here rather than in the aggregators. An open-label pilot in veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD, published in Nature's Communications Medicine. Open-label and small means promising, not proven: no control arm, expectancy effects uncontrolled, and the population is veterans, not first responders. It sits on the scope-of-practice edge for you, since integration coaching lives downstream of trials like this one.

Researchers ran psilocybin-assisted therapy with a cohort of veterans carrying severe PTSD that had not responded to standard treatment. They report the protocol was safe and feasible, with meaningful symptom reduction and a large share of participants no longer meeting PTSD criteria at follow-up. The authors are careful: the design is a pilot, uncontrolled, and built to justify a larger randomized trial, not to change practice yet.

Try this: Read the methods section, not the press. Note the absence of a control arm before you cite any remission figure to a client or partner clinic.

Recovering From Moral Injury by Breaking the Silence

A fitting close. The claim that moral injury heals through breaking silence is old news to the Desert Fathers, who called the naming of one's thoughts to an elder the beginning of freedom. Read it as confirmation from the clinical side of something the tradition already knew: the wound kept secret grows, the wound spoken begins to lose its grip. This is not therapy. It is the first turn toward return.

This Psychology Today piece argues that recovery from moral injury begins with breaking the silence around it. Isolation and shame are named as the forces that keep the wound alive, and disclosure to a trusted other as the counter-move. The frame is relational: healing starts in being heard, not in being fixed.
