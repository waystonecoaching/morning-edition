Major VA study to examine effectiveness of psilocybin on depression, PTSD

The VA is standing up a large multi-site trial of psilocybin for depression and PTSD in veterans. This is the standard of care moving under Mark's feet. Whatever the evidence base becomes for veterans will shape what first responders, their departments, and their unions ask for next. It also sits on a scope-of-practice edge: this is medical treatment, not coaching integration, and the line matters.

The Department of Veterans Affairs has authorized a major study evaluating psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with depression and PTSD, its most significant institutional step toward psychedelic research to date. The trial is positioned as rigorous and multi-site, a shift from the smaller open-label work that has carried the field so far. Read it as a signal of legitimacy and funding, not yet as settled outcome data. Federal sponsorship raises the credibility bar and the scrutiny at the same time.

Try this: Note the trial design when it publishes. A properly controlled VA result is the number to cite with skeptical department leadership, not the open-label pilots.

Moral injury can be devastating: new assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

This is the structural-critique lineage of moral injury, not the performance-psych frame. A validated screening protocol means moral injury gets named earlier, before it hardens into the shame-and-silence pattern Mark sees in the firehouse. Naming is the first act of contact. A tool that lets a responder be identified before collapse is worth watching.

Researchers at the University of Bath describe a new assessment protocol designed to identify people at elevated risk of moral injury earlier and more reliably. Moral injury, the wound from acting against or being betrayed on one's own moral code, has lacked clean measurement, which has slowed both recognition and care. The protocol aims to close that gap for high-exposure workforces. Earlier identification is the point, not a treatment claim.

Try this: Watch for the published instrument. A short, validated moral-injury screen would be a cleaner intake signal than the PTSD checklists that miss the moral dimension entirely.

Free Motivational Interviewing Training

Motivational interviewing is one of the few behavior-change modalities with a real evidence base for substance use and ambivalence, and it overlaps cleanly with non-directive inquiry. Free, credentialed training is a concrete asset for a solo practice. Worth flagging where the coaching field is otherwise thick with SEO noise and certification mills.

The University of Maryland, Baltimore is offering free motivational interviewing training. MI is a client-centered, directional method for working with ambivalence, built on reflective listening and evoking the person's own reasons for change rather than supplying them. It maps well onto the sensation-feeling-story-want distinction and onto BTTY's non-directive stance. The offer is a training resource, not new research.

Try this: If the format fits, take the free MI training this month. It sharpens the same muscle non-directive inquiry uses, and it is defensible on an intake form.

Psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD: open-label pilot

Primary literature, which is where Mark should live when the topic is psychedelics under credibility stress. This is an open-label pilot: no control arm, small sample, expectancy effects uncontrolled. Read it as a safety-and-feasibility signal, not efficacy. The honest version of enthusiasm names those limits out loud.

An open-label pilot trial published in Communications Medicine reports on psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD, finding it feasible and generally safe with preliminary reductions in symptoms. Open-label design means everyone knew they received the drug, so expectancy and placebo response cannot be separated from any real effect. The sample is small and the follow-up limited. It is a green light for larger controlled trials, not evidence of efficacy.

Source: Communications Medicine (Nature)

Minnesota spent $100M to slow first responder PTSD retirements. It isn't working.

A hard number against the easy story that money alone fixes responder PTSD. Only 17 of 337 participants returned to work, and the fund is projected to run dry by 2027. This is the operational-program frame meeting its own outcome data. Treatment access without the deeper work of integration and identity does not automatically return people to the job, and Mark's practice lives exactly in that gap.

Minnesota's roughly $100 million program to treat PTSD among police and firefighters has produced limited return-to-work results, with only 17 of 337 participants going back on the job, and the fund is expected to be exhausted by 2027. The reporting frames a well-funded intervention that is not moving its stated outcome. It raises the question of whether disability-and-treatment models miss the reintegration and meaning work that recovery actually requires. The dollar figure is the headline; the design problem is the story.

Source: Firefighter Nation
