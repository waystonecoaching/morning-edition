VA launches a large psilocybin study for veterans with depression and PTSD

The VA moving its own dollars into a psilocybin trial is the signal that matters, not another advocacy press release. When the largest care system for combat trauma runs the study in-house, the standard of care for PTSD starts to shift, and first responder programs tend to follow the veteran track a few years behind. This is veteran population, not fire/EMS, so read it as adjacent evidence rather than direct proof for your people.

The Department of Veterans Affairs announced a funded, multi-site trial testing psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans carrying depression and PTSD together. It marks a reversal from years of the agency keeping its distance from Schedule I research. The design pairs dosing sessions with structured psychotherapy rather than treating the molecule as a standalone drug. Enrollment and endpoints are still early, so treat the framing as intent, not outcome.

Try this: If a client asks whether psychedelics are 'real medicine now,' point to who is running the trial (the VA) rather than the hype. Ownership of the study is the credibility marker.

Texas maps moral injury across its first responder workforce and names it a policy problem

This sits squarely in the structural-critique lineage: moral injury framed as a workforce and system failure, not a private weakness to be resilience-trained away. That is the Litz and Shay reading, and it argues that betrayal by leadership and broken systems does measurable damage. Worth holding against the operational-wellness camp that would locate the fix inside the individual responder.

A state-level report documents moral injury across Texas first responders and issues policy recommendations rather than individual coping tips. It treats the problem as structural: staffing, leadership, and institutional betrayal as drivers of harm. The recommendations aim at departments and legislators, not at the responder's own regulation habits. It is a policy foundation product, so read the framing with its advocacy posture in view.

Psilocybin PTSD sessions turn out to be mostly silent

A process finding that should interest anyone trained in non-directive inquiry: the therapeutic hours were largely wordless. That maps onto the hesychast intuition that stillness, not commentary, does the deeper work, and onto the BTTY posture of trusting the client's inner movement over the practitioner's talking. Useful counterweight to the assumption that integration means more processing out loud.

Researchers coded what actually happens during psilocybin therapy sessions and found long stretches of silence, with practitioners speaking sparingly. The result challenges the picture of active, talk-heavy intervention during dosing. It suggests the therapist's job may be closer to holding presence than directing content. As a single descriptive study, it maps behavior rather than proving what drives outcomes.

Source: PsyPost

An open-label pilot reports psilocybin gains in severe, treatment-resistant veteran PTSD

This is the primary literature behind the week's psilocybin headlines, so read it before the news write-ups. Open-label with no control arm and a small sample means the effect is promising and easy to overstate; expectancy alone can move these numbers. Keep it in the evidence file as a signal, and keep the credibility caveats attached when a client raises it.

A peer-reviewed pilot tested psilocybin-assisted therapy in veterans with severe, treatment-resistant PTSD and reported it feasible, tolerable, and associated with symptom reduction. The design is open-label without a placebo control, so the findings establish safety signals more than efficacy. The authors frame it as grounds for larger controlled trials, not as evidence of a proven treatment. Small n and unblinded design are the load-bearing limits.

Source: Communications Medicine (Nature)
No. 06 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-08-04
80% of soldiers, per the study

Israeli study reports 80% of soldiers saw PTSD symptoms ease with MDMA therapy

The number is the story and also the reason to slow down. The MDMA-PTSD field is under real credibility stress after regulatory rejection over unblinding and functional-unblinding problems, and an 80% figure from a smaller wartime cohort in national press should be read as preliminary, not settled. Hold it as morale-context for the soldiers in question, not as a benchmark you can quote to clients.

Israeli researchers reported that roughly 80% of soldiers in an MDMA-assisted psychotherapy study experienced eased PTSD symptoms. The result is being carried widely in national and regional outlets. Details on control conditions, blinding, and follow-up duration are thin in the coverage. Given the field's ongoing scrutiny over unblinding, the headline figure deserves the primary paper before any weight is placed on it.

Source: The Jerusalem Post

A new protocol makes moral injury easier to screen for

Researchers at Bath developed an assessment protocol designed to identify people at risk of moral injury more reliably. The aim is earlier recognition in populations like military and emergency services. It positions moral injury as distinct from PTSD, with its own markers around guilt, shame, and betrayal. As a screening instrument it flags risk; it does not itself treat the wound.

A validated screening protocol is the kind of tool that changes practice at the edges of your lane. It helps name and route moral injury without pretending assessment is treatment; identifying risk is a coaching-adjacent act, intervening on it is not. Worth reading for how it defines the construct, since that definition shapes how you would describe the territory to a client.

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-07-25

Recovering from moral injury by breaking the silence

This closes the edition where the Desert Fathers would: the wound spoken aloud loses its grip. What the piece calls breaking the silence, the tradition calls confession and the manifestation of thoughts to an elder, the practice of not letting a logismos fester in the dark. The mechanism is old and the coaching application is simple; the naming is the beginning of the healing.

The article argues that moral injury festers in secrecy and that speaking it, to someone trusted, is central to recovery. It frames disclosure not as catharsis for its own sake but as a way to break shame's isolating hold. The counsel is relational: healing happens in the presence of another who can receive the account without flinching. It is a general-audience piece, not a clinical protocol.

Source: Psychology Today
