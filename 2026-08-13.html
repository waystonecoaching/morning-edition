Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

This is the lineage Mark trained near. The company once called MAPS PBC, then Lykos, now Resilient, has put MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD back in front of the FDA two years after the 2024 rejection. For a coach on the Being True to You integration path, the regulatory status of MDMA is not abstract. It shapes what clients arrive asking for and what the standard of care may become. Read it as a status marker, not a victory lap. A refiling is a door reopened, not walked through.

Psychedelic Alpha reports that Resilient Pharmaceuticals has resubmitted its new drug application for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD. The move follows the 2024 FDA rejection that sent the program back to address concerns about trial design, functional unblinding, and data integrity. The refiling is described as quiet, without the public campaign that surrounded the earlier bid. What has changed in the underlying evidence base since the rejection remains the open question.

Try this: If a client raises MDMA therapy this week, name where it actually stands: refiled, not approved, still contested. Keep the framing honest and scope-aware.

Moral Injury Doesn't Always Look Like Moral Injury: What Military Leaders Need to Recognize

This sits in the structural-critique lineage, closer to Shay and Litz than to the performance-psych wellness frame. The argument is that moral injury hides in plain sight, misread as burnout, cynicism, or discipline problems. For Mark's population that mistranslation is the whole problem. The wound gets managed as a behavior instead of met as a rupture of conscience.

The piece argues that moral injury in military and first responder settings rarely presents as obvious guilt or grief. It shows up as withdrawal, irritability, moral numbing, or contempt for leadership, and gets misfiled as a performance or attitude issue. The author presses leaders to recognize the signs and to look at their own role, since betrayal by leadership is itself a documented source of moral injury.

Psilocybin therapy sessions for PTSD are mostly silent, study finds

Read this one against the Desert Fathers. A study looking at what actually happens inside psilocybin sessions for PTSD found that most of the therapeutic hour is silence, not talk therapy. The contemplative tradition would not be surprised. Stillness is not absence; it is where the work happens under the words. For a coach trained to value non-directive presence, this is a quiet confirmation from an unexpected direction. Hold it lightly. One study describing session structure is not evidence of mechanism.

PsyPost reports on a study analyzing the content of psilocybin-assisted therapy sessions for PTSD. The finding is that the sessions are largely silent, with therapists speaking far less than a talk-therapy model would predict. The implication is that the therapeutic action may lean more on presence and internal processing than on verbal intervention. The work describes what happens in the room; it does not settle the question of what drives outcomes.

Source: PsyPost

80% of Soldiers in Israeli Study See PTSD Symptoms Ease With MDMA Therapy

A striking number from a combat-trauma population close to Mark's responder work. Treat the 80% with discipline. Israeli military PTSD studies of MDMA have tended to be small and open-label, and this is exactly the kind of headline figure that carried the earlier program before the FDA pushed back on unblinding and expectancy effects. The number is a reason to read the study, not to trust the study.

The Media Line reports that an Israeli study found roughly 80 percent of soldiers with PTSD experienced symptom relief after MDMA-assisted therapy. The figure is presented as strong support for the treatment in a combat-trauma population. As with prior MDMA trials, the methodological questions of blinding, expectancy, and sample size determine how much the number can bear. The coverage does not resolve those questions.

For First Responders, New Nevada Program Offers Path to Resilience With Horses

Equine work is easy to dismiss and worth a second look. The mechanism a polyvagal reading would point to is co-regulation: a large prey animal reads your autonomic state faster than you do, and meeting it requires the ventral-vagal calm you cannot fake. Whether the program measures outcomes or trades on novelty is the question to ask. The frame is promising; the evidence bar for these programs is usually low.

KUNR reports on a new Nevada program offering equine-assisted work as a resilience path for first responders. Participants work with horses in a structured setting aimed at stress regulation and connection. The piece is a program profile rather than an outcome study, so it describes the approach and participant response without measured results. The appeal rests on embodied, non-verbal regulation more than on talk-based intervention.

Recovering From Moral Injury by Breaking the Silence

A fitting close. The claim is old and true: what is carried alone festers, and what is spoken into presence begins to heal. The Desert Fathers called it manifestation of thoughts to an elder. The clinical world calls it breaking the silence. The scope edge is worth naming, since moral injury runs near shame and self-harm, and the spoken hearing has to be held by someone equipped to hold it.

The Psychology Today piece argues that recovery from moral injury depends on breaking the silence that surrounds it. Isolation and secrecy deepen the wound; disclosure to a trusted other begins to loosen it. The article frames voicing the injury, to a peer, a clinician, or a community, as a central mechanism of repair. It is a general-audience treatment rather than new research, but it names the pattern cleanly.

Source: Psychology Today
