Major VA study to examine effectiveness of psilocybin on depression and PTSD

This is the federal government putting institutional weight behind psilocybin for the exact injuries first responders carry. When the VA runs a large trial, it changes what counts as a defensible standard of care for PTSD, which is the ground your integration work stands on. Watch it as a signal of where reimbursement and access are heading, not as a result. Enrollment is not evidence.

The VA is launching a large multi-site study of psilocybin-assisted therapy for veterans with treatment-resistant depression and PTSD, one of the more significant federal commitments to the modality to date. Reporting places it among a wave of VA psychedelic research after years of the agency keeping its distance. The design and endpoints will matter more than the announcement. A federal trial of this size lends legitimacy the field has been seeking, and it also sets a bar that results will have to clear in public view.

Try this: If a current or former client raises psilocybin as an option, you can now point to a VA-run study as the honest state of play: promising, under formal investigation, not yet settled. That framing keeps you inside scope.

Coverage frames the refiling as low-key, which fits a strategy of addressing prior criticism rather than relaunching with fanfare. Psilocybin therapy sessions for PTSD are mostly silent, study finds

A content analysis found that most of the therapist's work during a psilocybin session is silence, not intervention. That is your lane described in a journal: non-directive presence, the client's own inner knowing carrying the process, the practitioner holding safety rather than steering. It lands close to the hesychast intuition that stillness is not absence but attention. Read it as validation of restraint and as a caution against the therapist who cannot stop talking.

Researchers coded what actually happens during psilocybin-assisted therapy sessions for PTSD and found that clinicians spend the large majority of dosing time silent, intervening only briefly and mostly to support rather than direct. The finding pushes back on the assumption that the medicine works through active psychotherapy in the moment. It suggests the therapeutic frame is more about holding a safe container than delivering content. The work is descriptive and small, but it sharpens what the practitioner's role during dosing really is.

Try this: Sit with how much of your own integration presence is silence versus talk. The study is a mirror for the temptation to fill space when the client's process is already doing the work.
Source: PsyPost

80% of Soldiers in Israeli Study See PTSD Symptoms Ease With MDMA Therapy

The 80 percent figure is the kind of number that travels fast and deserves friction. It is a single-country study in soldiers, reported through general press, without the design detail you would need to weigh it. Hold it next to the FDA's unresolved concerns from the same drug class. A strong headline number is where credibility stress usually enters, not where it resolves.

An Israeli study of soldiers with PTSD reports that roughly 80 percent saw meaningful symptom reduction after MDMA-assisted therapy. The result adds to a growing international body of MDMA-for-PTSD work, much of it in military populations. As reported, the coverage lacks the sample size, control condition, and follow-up detail needed to judge durability or generalizability. Read it as one data point in a contested field, not as a settled effect size.

Moral Injury Doesn't Always Look Like Moral Injury: What Leaders Need to Recognize

The piece argues that moral injury rarely presents as visible anguish and more often shows up as detachment, moral numbing, irritability, or a brittle over-functioning that passes for resilience. It warns leaders that the people least likely to raise a hand are often the ones carrying the most, and that standard check-ins miss them. The corrective is to watch for the subtle behavioral shifts rather than waiting for disclosure. It sits in the structural lineage that treats the injury as a wound to conscience, not a performance deficit.

The ones least likely to raise a hand are often carrying the most.

This one earns its place because it refuses the tidy picture. Moral injury often wears the mask of cynicism, withdrawal, or a hard competence that reads as fine, which is exactly why it gets missed on the floor and in intake. Written for leaders, it is really about neuroception: the guarded state that looks like strength and is actually a shutdown. Good language for naming what a stoic responder will not name for himself.

MI is a well-validated, client-centered approach for helping people resolve ambivalence about change, widely used in substance use and health behavior work. Free, university-hosted training is a credible entry point compared with the paid coaching-certification market. Format and eligibility details sit behind the listing.

Try this: Check the eligibility and dates this week. If it is open and remote, it is a cheap add to the eliciting and change-talk side of your practice.
