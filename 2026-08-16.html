Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

If this clears, the standard of care for first responder PTSD changes and every referral conversation you have changes with it. Read the filing strategy carefully. Resilient, formerly Lykos and before that MAPS PBC, has refiled without running a new Phase 3. The 2024 Complete Response Letter asked for durability data, better safety characterization, and steps to reduce bias after functional unblinding. A resubmission that answers those questions with reanalysis rather than new data is a bet on the agency's posture, not on new evidence. Hold your enthusiasm at arm's length until the review class and the acceptance decision are public.

Psychedelic Alpha reported on August 9 that Resilient Pharmaceuticals resubmitted its new drug application for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, two years after the FDA declined it. The company did not conduct an additional Phase 3 trial before refiling. Proponents argue that core efficacy findings and adverse event reporting held up despite partial functional unblinding in the original trials. MAPS, which initiated and funded the underlying clinical research completed in 2022, issued its own statement on August 10 acknowledging the filing. Nothing about the resubmission resolves the methodological criticisms that drove the original rejection.

Try this: When a client asks whether MDMA therapy is coming, say what is true: an application is filed, the FDA has not accepted or approved it, and nothing is available outside a trial today.

Moral Injury in the Texas First Responder Workforce: Findings and Policy Recommendations

This is the structural-critique lineage doing policy work, and it lands close to your lane. The report puts moral injury at nearly two thirds of the Texas responder workforce and locates the cause in operational conditions the state controls, not in individual fragility. Note the argument it makes against clinical-entry-only systems: if the only door is a clinician's door, you meet people after the crisis. That is precisely the gap a coach standing outside the clinical line can occupy, and precisely where scope discipline has to be tightest.

The report finds moral injury widespread and largely undiagnosed among Texas first responders, concentrated in the group most likely to leave the profession or die by suicide. It argues that the drivers are substantially operational and organizational, which puts them within the state's authority to change. The recommended approach is early detection of responders who are not yet in crisis, using a ten-minute screen, followed by intervention that already exists. The authors point to infrastructure Texas has already built: the Texas Law Enforcement Peer Network offering 24-hour anonymous peer support to every peace officer, HB 35 from 2025 extending a parallel network to fire and EMS, and the First Responder Mental Health Program moving grant money to departments annually.

Try this: Read their screening argument, then check what Arizona has that is comparable. If nothing does the pre-crisis detection job here, that is a gap worth naming in your partner conversations.

Moral injury can be devastating: New assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

A validated measure with a proposed cutoff is a scope-of-practice object, not a coaching tool. Screening, scoring, and interpreting a cutoff is clinical work. What it gives you is a shared vocabulary with the clinicians you refer to and a clearer sense of what they are looking at when they use the word. Know the instrument. Do not administer it.

Psychologists at the University of Bath developed and validated the Moral Injury Scale, MORIS, described in the European Journal of Trauma and Dissociation. It is the first such measure developed and validated specifically for UK populations. The scale assesses exposure to potentially morally injurious events, the distress associated with them, and relevant risk and protective factors. The team identified a candidate cutoff score for moral injury related distress, addressing a long-standing problem in deciding when someone needs additional support. MORIS is now moving into clinical trials evaluating moral injury treatments, with a parallel validation underway in Turkey.

Try this: Add MORIS to your referral vocabulary so that when a clinician mentions it, you know what they measured and what the cutoff means.
Source: Medical Xpress / University of Bath

Virtual intervention reduces substance use in American Indians and Alaska Natives

The design lesson travels even though the population does not. A remote, group-delivered, culturally grounded program beat a single-session workshop on alcohol and cannabis quantity, on time spent around using peers, and on anxiety. Facilitators came from inside the culture. That last variable is the one your practice already runs on: fluency in the room buys you a hearing that content alone will not. Substance use sits on the clinical edge, so hold the coaching frame carefully here.

An NIH-funded randomized trial tested Traditions and Connections for Urban Native Americans, TACUNA, a virtual program blending evidence-based prevention with traditional cultural practice. Researchers randomized 541 American Indian and Alaska Native adults aged 18 to 25 to TACUNA or to standard care, a single opioid and pain cultural health and wellness workshop. Both arms were delivered by American Indian and Alaska Native facilitators. Both groups reduced consequences and cannabis use. Only the TACUNA group reduced the quantity of alcohol and cannabis consumed, reduced time spent around peers using cannabis and heroin, and showed greater decreases in anxiety. Results appeared in JAMA Network Open on August 11.

Try this: If you build any group offering, staff it with people fluent in the culture before you optimize the curriculum. The trial suggests that is the load-bearing variable.
Source: National Institutes of Health

Mobile Integrated Health Care and Community Paramedicine: A Position Statement and Resource Document of NAEMSP

Your behavioral crisis role at Phoenix Fire lives inside the model this document is trying to standardize. NAEMSP is arguing for needs-based community care decoupled from transport reimbursement, plus common data definitions so programs can actually be compared. Both fights determine whether behavioral crisis response stays a pilot or becomes funded infrastructure. Know the position statement your program will eventually be measured against.

NAEMSP defines community paramedicine and mobile integrated health care as expanded EMS models delivering needs-based, patient-centered care in the community. Successful implementation, the statement argues, requires a community health needs assessment, an engaged EMS medical director, multidisciplinary collaboration, and sustainable reimbursement that pays for prehospital care rather than for transport. It calls on state and federal agencies to build Medicare and Medicaid reimbursement pathways decoupled from transportation, and on EMS agencies to negotiate the same with private payors. It also presses for standardized data collection, a common taxonomy for describing programs, and shared outcome measures, without which the field cannot evaluate itself or grow.

Try this: Pull the data and taxonomy section and compare it against what your unit currently records. Gaps there are the gaps that will show up when funding is reviewed.

Minnesota put $100 million into first responder PTSD treatment. Seventeen have returned to work.

This is the most important number in first responder mental health this year, and it is not a treatment-efficacy story. Of 337 responders who entered the program since 2023, 17 returned to work, 158 finished treatment and retired anyway, and about 100 are still in it. Treatment was delivered. Return to duty did not follow. The gap sits in what happens after, in the culture, the workers compensation incentives, the pension math, and whether a department has any usable path back for someone who has been away for eight months. Coaching lives in exactly that gap. So does the risk of overpromising what coaching can do about a structural problem.

Minnesota created a program in 2023 requiring police officers and firefighters to complete mental health treatment before retiring with a PTSD-related disability, funded at roughly $100 million, reimbursing cities and counties for treatment plus salary, benefits, and health insurance for up to 32 weeks. Since launch, 337 emergency responders have entered treatment. Seventeen returned to work, a five percent rate. One hundred fifty-eight completed treatment and retired anyway. Roughly a hundred remain in the program. Lawmakers, retirement officials, and responder advocates disagree about the causes but broadly agree the system is not keeping experienced officers and firefighters on the job, and that funding could be exhausted by the end of 2027.

Try this: Track this one. If Arizona proposes similar legislation, the Minnesota data is the argument for funding the return-to-duty pathway alongside the treatment, not after it.

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

MAPS partnered with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to deliver its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training free to first responders statewide, making Colorado the first state to do so. The program uses videos and assessments covering the common effects of natural medicines, how to interact with someone in an altered state, and best practices for assessing, de-escalating, and managing a psychedelic crisis. It also addresses legal considerations and the shifting role of first responders under a changing policy regime. The audience includes law enforcement, mental health providers, EMS personnel, and firefighters. The initiative supports implementation of Colorado's Natural Medicine Code.

The first state to train its responders for the calls its own policy created.

This is your job description meeting the psychedelic policy wave, and Colorado is doing it first. As natural medicine access expands, behavioral crisis responders start meeting people in altered states on calls that used to be coded differently. The training covers assessment, de-escalation, and the legal ambiguity in between. Arizona will face the same call volume eventually. Knowing what Colorado built is worth more to your Phoenix Fire work than most research that crosses your desk.

Try this: Watch the Colorado modules yourself. If the material holds up, it is a concrete thing to bring to your Phoenix Fire leadership before Arizona policy moves.

Psilocybin therapy sessions for PTSD are mostly silent, study finds

Seventy-eight percent silence. This corrects a misconception your clients carry and it validates the discipline the sitter role actually asks for. The support is quiet, unobtrusive presence, not trauma processing under the influence. That is not a lesser skill. Holding a room without filling it is the hardest thing to learn in this work, and it is the same restraint hesychastic practice asks for. The integration conversation is where language belongs. The session is not.

A study in the Journal of Psychopharmacology documented speech production during high-dose psilocybin administration sessions for PTSD in the COMP360 program. Investigators found that 78 percent of the session time was spent in silence between participants and support providers. The support offered was largely nondirective and unobtrusive, distinct in kind from the therapeutic dialogue used in conventional psychotherapy or trauma-focused treatment. The authors note a common misconception that patients undergo talk therapy while actively under the drug's effects, a belief that partly comes from MDMA trial protocols, which do feature active verbal psychotherapy during the dosing session.

Try this: Use the 78 percent figure the next time a client imagines the session as guided processing. It resets expectations before the appointment, not after.

Volunteers mark 30 years of supporting York Region first responders after traumatic incidents

Thirty years of continuous volunteer peer support is the rarer accomplishment in this field. Most teams flare up after a bad call and quietly die within five years. What kept this one alive is worth reading for, and the current lead's move is instructive: he is prioritizing stigma reduction and moving from reactive debrief toward registered workshops for responders and the community. That is a team recognizing that after-the-fact response has a ceiling. Same insight the Texas report makes with different evidence.

The York Region Critical Incident Stress Management Team marked 30 years of service. Between 30 and 50 volunteers provide confidential peer support to first responders after traumatic incidents. Team lead Wing Tse said the priority now is reducing stigma around mental illness and extending resources through community engagement. The team is rebranding and introducing registered informative workshops aimed at both first responders and the wider community, shifting from a purely post-incident response model toward ongoing education.

Recovering From Moral Injury by Breaking the Silence

The piece is pop-press and the mechanism is old. What it names correctly is that silence is not neutral. In an organization it compounds the injury. In the Desert tradition the same claim appears as the disclosure of thoughts to an elder: the logismos held in secret gains strength, and the one brought into speech loses it. The Fathers were not describing job satisfaction. But they described the mechanism first, and they knew what the listener owes: presence, not correction. Close the day with that.

The article defines moral injury as social, psychological, and spiritual harm arising from betrayal of core values such as justice, fairness, and loyalty. It identifies three routes to that harm: harming others, failing to protect others through error or inaction, and failing to be protected by leaders. The resulting anger, guilt, and shame can reshape a person's worldview and damage their capacity to trust. Drawing on research with police officers, it reports that employee silence amplifies the negative effect of moral injury on job satisfaction and engagement, and amplifies the positive effect on intention to leave the agency. Recovery, the author argues, starts with listening and acknowledgment, and many find that having suffering heard and validated is itself a decisive step.

Source: Psychology Today
