Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

This is the single biggest movement in the psychedelic field since the 2024 Complete Response Letter. Resilient, the company formerly known as MAPS PBC and then Lykos, has resubmitted without running the new Phase 3 the FDA asked for. Read that as a wager, not a vindication. If it clears, the standard of care for responder PTSD shifts under Mark's feet inside a year; if it does not, the credibility drag on the whole field gets heavier.

Resilient Pharmaceuticals resubmitted its new drug application for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD in early August 2026, two years after the FDA rejected the first filing. The 2024 Complete Response Letter cited durability of response, safety characterization, and steps to reduce bias in the trial data. The resubmission rests on the original MAPS-funded research completed in 2022 rather than a new Phase 3 study. MAPS confirmed it has no active role in Resilient's drug development program and only responded to the report. Nothing has been approved; this is a filing, and the review clock is the story to watch.

Try this: If a client asks whether MDMA therapy is legal yet, the honest answer stays no. A filing is not an approval. Hold that line clearly.

VA Launches New Study Using Psilocybin To Treat Veterans With Depression And PTSD

Federal money now moves through the VA on psychedelics, and the VA is where responder-adjacent care standards often get set first. Veterans and first responders share overlapping exposure profiles, so what the VA validates tends to reach fire and EMS wellness programs a few years later. Worth tracking as infrastructure, not as hype.

The Department of Veterans Affairs opened a trial called PIVOT, for Psilocybin Intervention for Veterans Overcoming Treatment-Resistant Depression. The study enrolls veterans with and without PTSD and compares two psilocybin doses under controlled safety protocols. Five VA medical centers across Alabama, Oregon, Pennsylvania, and Washington State will run it. The VA reports roughly twenty active psychedelic-related clinical trials, and PIVOT sits inside a recent executive order pushing faster psychedelic research and approval pathways. This is high-acuity treatment-resistant work, well inside the clinical line and not coaching territory.

Moral injury can be devastating: New assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

A validated screen changes what a conversation can carry. The MORIS is a self-report measure with a distress cutoff, which means moral injury moves from something a coach names by ear to something a clinician can document and track. That is a scope boundary worth watching. A screen with a cutoff is a clinical instrument, and running one crosses out of coaching.

Psychologists at the University of Bath released the Moral Injury Scale, MORIS, a self-report tool for health care providers. It assesses exposure to potentially morally injurious events, the distress attached to them, and relevant risk and protective factors. The scale offers a cutoff intended to support clinical decision-making and treatment monitoring in at-risk groups. Preliminary reliability and validity come from UK adult samples, so generalization to US fire and EMS populations is untested.

Try this: Know the instrument exists so you can recognize it when a partner clinic uses it. Do not administer it yourself.

Advances in time-sensitive assessment and intervention for acute stress reaction

This is the window Mark actually stands in on a CAP shift. Acute stress reaction is not acute stress disorder and not PTSD, and the review insists on keeping those distinct. The clinical argument here is that the first seventy-two hours are a different intervention problem than anything downstream, which is exactly the ground a behavioral crisis specialist works.

The review separates acute stress reaction from acute stress disorder, PTSD, and PTSD-risk constructs, arguing the conflation costs precision in early care. It maps evidence across three windows: the immediate aftermath, the acute phase of twenty-four to seventy-two hours, and the subacute phase from three days to one month. Instruments validated specifically for ASR remain scarce, so the authors borrow from broader acute traumatic stress literature. The practical claim is that the window for early psychological care is narrow and that assessment tools have not caught up to it.

Try this: Read the three-window frame against your own post-incident contact pattern. Where does your touch actually land, and is it in the phase you think it is?
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Optimizing trauma-focused treatments to address guilt and shame: Clinical considerations and strategies

Guilt and shame are the affects that show up in integration work long after the incident is closed. This is clinical treatment literature, so it sits across the line from coaching. Read it for how the field thinks about the terrain, not as technique to import. When a client's shame is load-bearing and structural, that is a referral conversation.

The paper reviews how standard trauma-focused protocols handle guilt and shame, and where they fall short. It offers clinical considerations and strategies for adapting existing treatments when those affects drive the presentation rather than fear. Guilt and shame frequently attach to moral injury rather than to threat, which is why fear-extinction logic underperforms with them. The piece is written for clinicians delivering trauma-focused treatment.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Moral Injury in the Texas First Responder Workforce: Findings and Policy Recommendations

State-level data on the exact population Mark serves, framed as policy rather than pathology. The report's argument is structural: moral injury clusters in the responders most likely to leave the job or die by suicide, and the drivers are operational conditions a state can change. Arizona is not Texas, but the survey design and the legislative hook are portable. This piece touches suicidality directly; handle it as policy reading, not as clinical guidance.

A survey of Texas first responders found 62.6 percent had experienced a morally injurious event, and half of those screened positive for probable PTSD. More than half reported numbness or detachment from people and surroundings. The report argues moral injury concentrates among responders at highest risk of attrition and suicide, and that the drivers are operational conditions the state has authority to influence. Its central policy claim is that the gap is choice, not capability: a ten-minute screen identifies who needs help and the interventions already exist. Texas has moved adjacent legislation requiring a licensed mental health assessment within ten days of a critical incident, including evaluation for suicidal ideation.

Try this: Check whether Arizona has a comparable post-incident assessment mandate. If not, this report is a usable template for the conversation.

Psilocybin therapy sessions for PTSD are mostly silent, study finds

A study in the Journal of Psychopharmacology tracked what actually happens during high-dose psilocybin sessions for PTSD. Participants and support staff spent the large majority of session time in silence. Interview data indicated that the quiet presence of staff was not passive from the participant's side; it was the thing that produced a sense of safety. The authors argue high-dose sessions function on a different mechanism than conventional talk therapy, with monitoring and presence rather than dialogue doing the work.

Silence was not absence. The quiet presence of staff was what produced the sense of safety.

This is the closest thing in the week's research to a direct description of what a trip sitter does. The finding maps onto the non-directive stance Mark already holds through Being True to You: presence carries the safety, not intervention. Useful language for describing the sitter role to a partner clinic that assumes the session is talk therapy with a drug attached.

Try this: Use this when a clinic partner asks what the sitter contributes. The answer now has a citation behind it.

Moral Injury Doesn't Always Look Like Moral Injury: What Military Leaders Need to Recognize

The structural-critique lineage, aimed at command rather than at the injured. This sits in tension with the operational resilience camp that treats the problem as individual capacity, and the tension is the content. Fire service leadership reads the same way military leadership does: what a leader cannot recognize, a leader will discipline instead.

The piece argues moral injury rarely presents as visible distress. It shows up as cynicism, disengagement, rule-lawyering, sudden rigidity, or attrition that reads on paper as a performance problem. The author's target is leadership recognition, not individual treatment: the failure mode is a command structure that reads moral injury as an attitude issue and responds accordingly. The frame is Litz and Shay's lineage, where betrayal by legitimate authority is the injuring mechanism rather than the event itself.

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

Older item, surfacing because it is the only existing model of a state health authority training responders on psychedelic crisis. Colorado's Natural Medicine Division is doing what Arizona will eventually need to do. This is the intersection of Mark's two roles, behavioral crisis specialist and integration coach, and the training is free.

MAPS partnered with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to deliver its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide. The program is video and assessment based, covering evidence-based education, de-escalation practice, and field-tested response approaches for psychedelic crisis. It was announced in May 2026 as part of Colorado's regulated natural medicine rollout. The material is aimed at responders encountering acute psychedelic states in the field, not at therapists.

Try this: Look at whether the Colorado curriculum is accessible outside the state. If it is, it is the most direct professional development available for the CAP role right now.

Feeling Lost at Sea: Storytelling as a Tool for Resilience

A quieter close. The claim is old, older than psychology: a person who can narrate what happened to them is standing on different ground than a person who cannot. The Desert tradition knew this as the practice of disclosure of thoughts to an elder, exagoreusis, where the naming itself does the work before any counsel is given.

The piece argues that narrative structure is a resilience mechanism rather than a byproduct of recovery. Disorientation, the feeling of being lost at sea, is partly a story problem: the events have no shape, so the person has no position within them. Constructing a coherent account restores sequence, agency, and a sense of location. The author positions storytelling as accessible practice rather than clinical technique.

Source: Psychology Today
