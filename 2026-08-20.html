No. 01 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-08-19

Study finds sustained benefit for people using Oregon psilocybin services

This is the first multisite look at what actually happens inside Oregon's regulated, non-clinical psilocybin market, published in JAMA Network Open. It matters because it describes the setting most people will encounter first, which is a licensed facilitator in a service center, not a research protocol. If you sit with clients on the integration side, this is the population you will meet. Note the scope-of-practice edge clearly: facilitators are not therapists, and four first-time users needed medical attention.

Researchers followed 346 people who completed a single psilocybin session with a licensed facilitator at 24 Oregon service centers between November 2024 and June 2026. Participants reported at baseline, one week, one month, and three months. At one month, 91.5 percent said they benefited and 64.9 percent ranked the session among the ten most meaningful experiences of their lives. At three months, moderate to severe depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms had dropped, with improved wellbeing and life satisfaction. Harm reports were rare, though four first-time users had adverse behavioral reactions requiring medical attention.

Try this: Read the JAMA Network Open paper itself before quoting the headline numbers. Self-selected, self-reported, single-arm, no control group. The effect is real enough to take seriously and soft enough that you should say so out loud to a client.

Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

The 2024 Complete Response Letter asked for durability data, better safety characterization, and steps to reduce bias. Resilient, formerly Lykos and MAPS PBC, has resubmitted without running a new Phase 3. That is the whole story. Read it as a test of whether FDA meant what it said, and be careful about telling a first responder client that MDMA is close.

Resilient Pharmaceuticals resubmitted its new drug application for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, per an August 9 report from Psychedelic Alpha, later acknowledged by MAPS. The filing rests on the trial data completed in 2022 rather than new Phase 3 work. MAPS funded the original research but states it has no active role in Resilient's development program. The original rejection cited durability of response, safety characterization, and potential for bias, all of which came out of the functional-unblinding critique.

Try this: If a client asks when MDMA will be available, the honest answer today is that the same dataset FDA rejected is back on FDA's desk. No date. Say that plainly rather than borrowing optimism from advocacy press.

Congress Considers Mental Health Grant Program for First Responders

The Lifeline for First Responders Act would put federal money behind counseling, peer support, suicide prevention, and family services through NHTSA's Office of EMS. It is bipartisan in both chambers. The money is modest and the timeline is long, which is the part most coverage skips.

The bill would create an EMS and First Responder Wellness Grant Program housed in the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's Office of Emergency Medical Services. It authorizes 7.5 million dollars per year from fiscal 2028 through 2032, roughly 37.5 million total, covering firefighters, paramedics, EMS staff, and 911 dispatchers. The House version was introduced by Rep. Andrea Salinas with three cosponsors, co-led by Reps. Gabe Evans, Sharice Davids, and Thomas Kean Jr. The Senate companion, from Sens. Whitehouse and Hawley, has nine cosponsors. Authorization is not appropriation, and the first dollar would not move until fiscal 2028.

Stellate ganglion block emerges as a 'reset' for the overactive nervous system

SGB is the clearest example in medicine of treating trauma as an autonomic problem rather than a cognitive one. That is the same claim polyvagal work makes, arrived at from the anesthesiology side. Worth knowing well, because responders talk about it and the marketing around it runs far ahead of the evidence. This is squarely outside coaching scope: it is an interventional procedure, and the success rates quoted below come largely from clinic marketing rather than blinded trials.

A stellate ganglion block injects local anesthetic near the sympathetic ganglion in the neck, temporarily interrupting sympathetic outflow. The stated aim is to rebalance an autonomic system stuck in chronic activation, with symptom relief sometimes reported within hours. Advocates report success rates of 70 to 75 percent for standard SGB and higher for the two-level Dual Sympathetic Reset protocol. Those figures come mostly from clinics selling the procedure. The controlled literature is thinner, and the durability question is unsettled.

Try this: Add one line to how you describe autonomic state to clients: the nervous system is treatable at the level of the nervous system. You do not need to endorse SGB to make that point, and it lands differently than another breathing exercise.

Moral injury can be devastating: New assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

This sits in the structural-critique lineage rather than the performance-psychology one. It treats moral injury as something done to a person by events and institutions, and it builds a measure around exposure and betrayal rather than around individual hardiness. That distinction is live in the field and worth keeping sharp instead of smoothing over.

Psychologists at the University of Bath developed and validated the Moral Injury Scale, MORIS, published in the European Journal of Trauma and Dissociation. It measures exposure to potentially morally injurious events, the distress attached to them, and relevant risk and protective factors. It is the first such instrument developed specifically for UK populations, with validation underway in Turkey. The authors note that people carrying moral injury face elevated risk of depression, anxiety, PTSD, suicidality, and alcohol misuse.

PTSI, Psychedelics, and the Fire Service

Arizona is running the first clinical trial using whole psilocybin mushrooms for PTSD, and more than 280 firefighters and 50 officers have already signed up. That is your department's population, in your state, and some of them will walk into a room with you afterward. Whatever you think of the science, the integration demand is arriving locally before the evidence base is settled.

The piece argues that post-traumatic stress injury remains a first-order threat in the fire service, citing that firefighters are 72 percent more likely to die by suicide than the general working population. It positions psilocybin-assisted work as no longer fringe: studied academically, pursued by veterans, and quietly used by firefighters trying to stay functional. Observational data from veteran psilocybin and ayahuasca retreats show improvements in PTSI symptoms, depression, anxiety, and reintegration. Psilocybin remains federally illegal, but state law is shifting. Arizona is funding a whole-mushroom PTSD trial and Missouri has advanced a bill permitting psilocybin and ibogaine for veterans and first responders inside approved studies.

Try this: Find out where the Arizona trial sits in enrollment and what its aftercare looks like. If 280 firefighters are going through it, someone will need integration support that the trial protocol does not cover.
Source: Firefighter Nation

No. 07 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-08-16

Nine veterans with PTSD went to Mexico for a psychedelic retreat. This is how they felt nearly a year later.

60 Minutes returned to nine US veterans with combat-related PTSD who attended a week-long psilocybin retreat near Puerto Vallarta, organized and paid for by the Heroic Hearts Project, which has helped more than 1,000 veterans access psychedelics abroad. Nearly a year on, eight of the nine reported improvement, some describing intrusive thoughts as simply gone. One veteran said his mental health deteriorated after the retreat and he has returned to antidepressants. The retreat happened outside US regulation, with no clinical follow-up structure attached.

Eight of nine got better. One got worse. That is the honest ratio, and nobody selling this will tell you.

The follow-up is the valuable part. Most psychedelic coverage stops at the week of the retreat, when everyone is glowing. This one waited a year and reports the man who got worse alongside the eight who got better. That ratio is closer to what the room actually looks like than either the advocacy press or the backlash press will tell you.

Source: CBS News / 60 Minutes

Gilbert police focus on officer wellness as new grant expands first responder support

This is Maricopa County money moving into responder wellness right now, and Gilbert PD has run a full-time peer support unit since 2018. Two things worth watching: who administers the Parsons money across the county, and whether Struggle Well's post-traumatic growth model leaves room for outside practitioners or closes the loop internally.

Gilbert Police is publicizing its wellness resources as a new 600,000 dollar grant from The Bob and Renee Parsons Foundation expands first responder support across Maricopa County. The department has staffed a full-time peer support and wellness unit since 2018, available around the clock after critical incidents, alongside counseling and programs covering fitness, sleep, and nutrition. Gilbert partners with the Boulder Crest Foundation and its Struggle Well curriculum, which is built on post-traumatic growth rather than symptom reduction.

Try this: Ask who is administering the Parsons grant countywide and whether it funds outside referral. A 600,000 dollar pool with no named provider list is worth one phone call.

MAPS & State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

This is the closest thing in the field to a curriculum for your actual job: what to do when you arrive on a psychedelic crisis call. Colorado built it into a state program with the Natural Medicine Division. Arizona has no equivalent, which is either a gap or an opening depending on how you look at it.

MAPS partnered with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to make its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training free to first responders statewide. The program runs on video modules and assessments covering evidence-based education and de-escalation practice for people in acute psychedelic distress. It came out of MAPS' decades of festival and event harm-reduction work rather than a clinical setting. As state legal-access programs expand, these calls land on responders first.

Try this: Take the Colorado modules even though you are in Arizona. It is free, it is directly on your behavioral crisis lane, and it gives you a documented curriculum to point at if Phoenix ever asks who should write ours.

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-17

Feeling Lost at Sea: Storytelling as a Tool for Resilience

Written for the crew of a carrier on a record-length deployment, but the mechanism is the one you work with every session. A person who cannot narrate what happened to them stays inside it. Naming it moves them from inside the event to beside it, which is where change becomes possible.

The piece looks at sailors aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln through a long deployment and a rising mental health burden, and argues that structured storytelling is a usable resilience practice rather than a soft one. Telling one's own story recalibrates orientation when a person feels lost, surfaces positive emotion by putting accomplishment and contribution back in view, and cuts isolation by creating contact. The author points to art, writing, and music as vehicles, and calls for giving service members instruction and resources rather than assuming they will find the words alone.

Try this: One question, next session: ask the client to tell you what happened as a story with a beginning, not as a symptom list. Watch where the story stops. That is usually where the work is.
Source: Psychology Today
