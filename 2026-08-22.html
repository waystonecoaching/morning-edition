Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

This is the single biggest lever on the standard of care for responder and veteran PTSD, and it moved without a press release. If MDMA-assisted therapy reaches approval, every integration conversation you hold shifts from adjacent to adjunctive, and the referral map around Waystone changes shape. Read the quiet of the refiling as data in itself.

The applicant now known as Resilient Pharmaceuticals, formerly MAPS PBC and then Lykos Therapeutics, has resubmitted its new drug application for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD. The original application drew a Complete Response Letter on August 9, 2024, which called for more work on durability of response, safety characterization, and steps to reduce bias in the trial design. Reporting indicates the resubmission arrives without a new Phase 3 study behind it. The company has not discussed the filing publicly; the account rests on sources speaking anonymously. That absence of a public statement is worth holding alongside the news itself.

Nine veterans with PTSD went to Mexico for a psychedelic retreat. This is how they felt nearly a year later.

This is the unregulated lane, filmed. Your clients are not waiting for an NDA, and some of them will come back from a week in Jalisco with an experience no clinical protocol prepared them for and no aftercare attached to it. Watch how the follow-up is framed: a year out, on self-report, with no control and no blinding. That is journalism, not evidence, and the distinction is the whole job.

60 Minutes followed nine U.S. veterans with combat-related PTSD who traveled to a village near Puerto Vallarta for a week-long retreat organized and funded by the Heroic Hearts Project, a nonprofit that says it has helped more than a thousand veterans access psychedelics abroad. Traditional healers ran three ceremonies, the first two using psilocybin. The August 2026 segment returns to the group nearly a year later. Most described the experience as life-changing. What the piece cannot tell you is who did not improve, who was harmed, and who was screened out before the plane left.

Try this: Write your intake language for a client returning from an unregulated retreat: what you can hold as a coach, what belongs to a licensed clinician, and where the line sits. Have it on paper before you need it.

Congress Considers Mental Health Grant Program for First Responders

Fire and EMS have never had what law enforcement got in 2018. If this passes, departmental money appears for peer support, counseling, family services, and training, and departments will go looking for people who can deliver it. That is a procurement door, and it opens for whoever already has the credential and the curriculum on the shelf.

Rep. Andrea Salinas introduced the Lifeline for First Responders Act, a bipartisan bill co-led by Reps. Gabe Evans, Sharice Davids, and Thomas Kean Jr., with a Senate companion. It would create the first federal grant program dedicated to mental health and wellness services for firefighters, paramedics, EMS staff, and 911 dispatchers, authorizing 7.5 million dollars a year through 2032 for counseling, suicide prevention, peer support, family services, and training. Supporters cite estimates that up to a quarter of firefighters carry PTSD and that 37 percent of fire and EMS personnel have contemplated suicide. Endorsers include NAMI, the IAFC, and the IAFF. The Law Enforcement Mental Health and Wellness Act built the same structure for police in 2018; no equivalent exists for fire and EMS.

Try this: Track the bill number and set a reminder for markup. If it advances, Phoenix Fire and the surrounding departments become grant applicants, and vendors get named in applications months before money moves.

Moral injury can be devastating: New assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

A validated instrument with a distress cut-off changes what moral injury is in institutional terms. It stops being a word people use in the bay and becomes a score a department can be asked about. That cuts two ways: it opens funding and recognition, and it hands administrators a number they can use to sort people. Note also that a cut-off score sits on the clinical side of your line. Screening is not coaching.

Psychologists at the University of Bath developed and validated a measure for moral injury, reported in the European Journal of Trauma and Dissociation. The instrument, the Moral Injury Scale (MORIS), is the first developed and validated specifically for UK populations. It is built to help clinicians and researchers identify people affected by moral injury, track symptom change over time, and evaluate treatment. The team also proposed a cut-off score for moral injury-related distress, addressing a long-standing gap in the field. The framing throughout is risk: people carrying moral injury show elevated rates of depression, anxiety, PTSD, suicidality, and alcohol misuse.

The protective role of memory suppression in psychological resilience among young adults with childhood trauma

This one will irritate you, and it should. The finding runs against the integration premise that what is suppressed must eventually be met. Hold the tension rather than resolving it: there is a real difference between the capacity to set a memory down at will and the involuntary numbing of dorsal shutdown, and this study measures the first. It is a preprint, and the sample is students, not responders. Read it as a question about voluntary attentional control, which is closer to hesychast territory than the headline suggests.

An online survey of 2,914 students measured childhood trauma, resilience, and the ability to voluntarily inhibit unwanted memories. Suppression ability fully mediated the relationship between trauma exposure and resilience. A subset of 91 participants, grouped by trauma exposure and resilience level, then completed a Think/No-Think task under fMRI. Behaviorally, higher-resilience individuals showed better memory suppression. Imaging pointed to the left intraparietal sulcus as the neural substrate, with greater suppression-related activity in the high-resilience group and a mediating role between suppression performance and resilience. The work has not cleared peer review.

Source: Research Square (preprint)

95% of Oregon Psilocybin Study Participants Saw Improvements in Depression, PTSD

The number is real and the number is not a trial result. This is observational data from a legal market, self-selected, unblinded, uncontrolled, with people who chose to pay for and attend a session. That design cannot separate the molecule from the expectation, the facilitator, or the ritual. What it can do, and this is the useful part, is describe what actually happens when a state stands up a licensed service model at scale. Four adverse behavioral reactions requiring medical attention, and seven people calling the experience harmful, belong in the same sentence as the 95 percent.

OHSU led the first multisite study of Oregon's voter-approved psilocybin services. Researchers enrolled 346 people who attended a single session with a licensed facilitator at 24 service centers between November 2024 and June 2026, with self-report at baseline and at one week, one month, and three months. At one month, 91.5 percent felt they had benefited and 64.9 percent ranked the session among the ten most meaningful experiences of their lives. At three months, 95 percent reported decreases in moderate-to-severe depression, anxiety, and PTSD alongside gains in wellness and life satisfaction. On safety, four first-time participants had adverse behavioral reactions requiring medical attention, no serious physical events were reported, and seven people described the experience as harmful at three months.

No. 07 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-13

Operator Syndrome and Moral Injury

In 2020 a research team led by psychologist Chris Frueh named Operator Syndrome to describe a pattern clinicians kept seeing in special operations patients that no single diagnosis captured: traumatic brain injury tangled with endocrine dysfunction, chronic pain, sleep disruption, and existential distress marked by a foreshortened future, survivor's guilt, and difficulty finding meaning after the mission. Frueh's model did not include moral injury. In 2024 clinical ethicist Michele DeMarco proposed adding it, rebuilding the schematic with a moral and spiritual domain. The August 2026 piece reports that moral injury has now been added as a specifier. Validation work continues; a study from the VA Palo Alto Intensive Evaluation and Treatment Program went up as a preprint in November 2025 and has not been peer reviewed.

Moral injury is not an endocrine problem or a sleep problem. It is a violation of conscience, and working with it means working with guilt, meaning, and trust.

Here is the applied camp and the structural camp arguing inside one construct. Frueh's original model treats the operator as a body under load: brain, endocrine, sleep, pain. DeMarco's amendment says the conscience is also an organ that can be wounded, and that no amount of sleep hygiene reaches it. Both are right about different things, and the fire service inherits the same split. Your clients have both problems and usually only get offered the first.

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

This is your job description meeting the psychedelic file. Colorado is licensing a crisis curriculum statewide for the exact role you hold, and Arizona shares the demographic pressure without the regulated market. Whatever you think of MAPS as an advocacy organization, the training content is the operational question: what does a behavioral crisis responder do when the person in front of him is in an altered state and the standard de-escalation script does not fit.

MAPS announced a partnership with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to distribute its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide, at no cost. The curriculum uses video modules and assessments covering education, de-escalation, and field tools for law enforcement, mental health providers, and EMS and fire personnel responding to people in altered states. More than twenty MAPS and subcontracted professionals built it, drawing on law, medicine, psychiatry, mental health, neuropsychopharmacology, law enforcement, crisis response, quality improvement, and education. The work traces back to 2019, when Denver decriminalized psilocybin and the city partnered with MAPS on responder training. Colorado becomes the first state to license the material for distribution across agencies.

Medical marijuana, firefighter/paramedics, and the difference between legal and right

The quiet fact under this piece: a firefighter/paramedic sought treatment for PTSD, anxiety, and sleep, did it through a physician, and lost his career for it. Every crew member reading that story learns something about the cost of disclosure. That is the substance-use and scope edge you work on daily, and it is also why the culture of silence in the moral injury literature is not an abstraction. Employment law is doing autonomic work here, teaching the nervous system that honesty is unsafe.

EMS defense attorney David Givot examines the gap between what is legal and what departments punish in off-duty cannabis use by firefighter/paramedics. His argument: where someone receives genuine medical treatment, uses responsibly off duty, and reports to work unimpaired and able to perform every essential function, automatic career-ending discipline protects an outdated bright-line rule rather than public safety. He presses the distinction among therapeutic use, recreational use, and impairment, and notes the law has not caught up to it. The piece centers on Hillsborough County firefighter/paramedic Angelo Giambrone, fired after a positive test for marijuana he said he used off duty on a physician's recommendation for PTSD, anxiety, and sleep problems.

Recovering From Moral Injury by Breaking the Silence

The Fathers knew this before anyone measured it. The logismos kept unspoken grows; the one brought into the light loses its grip. What the paramilitary agency calls professionalism, the desert calls concealment, and it produces the same fruit either way. This is the closing piece because it names the mechanism your whole practice runs on: not insight, but speech, offered to someone who will receive it without flinching. Note also that the finding is structural. Silence is not a personal failing when the culture is built to reward it.

Wendy L. Patrick reviews research by Pariona-Cabrera and colleagues on moral injury among police, working from the definition of moral injury as the psychological distress that follows perpetrating, failing to prevent, or witnessing acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs. Studying 371 Australian police officers, the researchers found that employee silence amplifies the negative indirect effect of moral injury on job satisfaction and engagement, and strengthens the indirect effect on intention to leave the agency. Authoritarian management combined with paramilitary structure fosters the culture of silence, and those same conditions drive dissatisfaction and turnover. Moral injury research began with service members and has since moved into social work, healthcare, and policing.
