VA launches psilocybin trial for veterans with treatment-resistant depression

This is the federal government running the study itself rather than reviewing someone else's. When the VA generates its own data on psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, the resulting standard of care reaches first responders through the same clinical channels that carry every other VA finding. Note the scope edge: treatment-resistant depression in a supervised trial is a clinical population, not a coaching one. If a client raises this, the honest move is to name the difference between an enrolled trial and a retreat.

The VA opened PIVOT, the Psilocybin Intervention for Veterans Overcoming Treatment-Resistant Depression trial, across five sites: Birmingham and Tuscaloosa in Alabama, Portland, Philadelphia, and Puget Sound. The design enrolls veterans with and without co-occurring PTSD and compares two psilocybin doses under supervised safety protocols. It follows an executive order on accelerating medical treatments for serious mental illness, which pushes clinical trial participation and faster approval pathways for psychedelic compounds. This is the first VA-run psilocybin trial at this scale.

Try this: If a first responder client asks about access, the accurate answer is that VA trial enrollment is veteran-only and site-specific. Know the five locations before the question comes up.

Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

This is the credibility-stress story of the year, and it deserves a slow read. The 2024 complete response letter asked for durability data, better safety characterization, and steps to reduce bias from functional unblinding. The resubmission reportedly arrives without a new phase 3. Whatever one thinks of the compound, refiling against the same objections without new trial data is a governance question before it is a clinical one, and Mark will be asked about it by clients who read only the headline.

Psychedelic Alpha reported on August 9 that Resilient Pharmaceuticals resubmitted its MDMA-assisted therapy NDA to the FDA, two years after the August 2024 complete response letter. Sources described the filing on condition of anonymity, and the company has not discussed it publicly. MAPS confirmed the news the following day. The applicant has changed names twice: MAPS Public Benefit Corporation became Lykos Therapeutics in January 2024 and Resilient Pharmaceuticals in August 2025. Reporting indicates the resubmission proceeds without a new phase 3 trial, on the argument that core efficacy and adverse event data held up despite partial unblinding.

Police officer suicide: How northern Illinois is breaking the silence

A department-level account of what actually changes after a line-of-duty suicide, written without the usual varnish. The mechanism here is legislative and budgetary rather than inspirational: a state law removing cost as a barrier, plus funded annual wellness visits with responder-specialized clinicians. That is the difference between a culture campaign and a structural fix. This piece touches suicidality directly and is high-acuity reading; it names Officer Christian Pascente by name.

The article traces changes in northern Illinois law enforcement following the March 2026 funeral of Wauconda officer Christian Pascente. Illinois Senate Bill 3538, signed in August 2024 and effective June 1, 2025, gives first responders no-cost access to mental health services. Departments report officers now calling chaplains and requesting support after traumatic calls at rates they did not before. Next year's budgets fund annual wellness visits with clinicians who specialize in first responder populations. The piece is candid that repeated exposure to violence, death, child abuse, and fatal crashes accumulates in ways most civilians never face.

Try this: Compare the Illinois annual-wellness-visit model against what Phoenix currently funds. It is a concrete, fundable ask, not a vague call for culture change.

Congress considers a mental health grant program for first responders

Federal money for peer support, counseling, and suicide prevention, with a named administrator and a sunset date. Bipartisan co-leads and a Senate companion mean this has more life than the usual message bill. If it passes, departments will be looking for people who can build and staff the programs the grant funds, which is directly adjacent to the work Mark already does.

Rep. Andrea Salinas of Oregon's 6th district introduced the Lifeline for First Responders Act, co-led by Reps. Gabe Evans, Sharice Davids, and Thomas Kean Jr., with companion legislation in the Senate. The bill authorizes $7.5 million a year through 2032 for counseling, suicide prevention, peer support, family services, and training for firefighters and EMS personnel. It would be administered through the Office of Emergency Medical Services under the Department of Transportation. The structure mirrors a 2017 law that directed the Justice Department to fund mental health programs for law enforcement.

Try this: Track the bill number now. If it moves, grant-funded peer support programs will need curriculum and facilitators on short notice.

Moral injury can be devastating: new assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

A validated instrument with a distress cutoff is a different object than a construct everyone agrees is real but nobody can measure the same way twice. This sits squarely in the structural-critique lineage: it treats moral injury as its own injury, separable from PTSD, with its own exposures and its own protective factors. The cutoff is the load-bearing piece and also the risk, because a screening threshold in non-clinical hands turns quickly into a label.

Psychologists at the University of Bath published the Moral Injury Scale (MORIS) in the European Journal of Trauma and Dissociation on July 28. The instrument is the first developed and validated specifically for UK populations. It assesses exposure to potentially morally injurious events, the distress attached to those events, and associated risk and protective factors. The researchers identified a preliminary cutoff score for moral-injury-related distress intended to support clinical decision making and treatment monitoring. Reliability and validity data come from UK adult samples, so cross-population generalization is not yet established.

Source: University of Bath

95% of Oregon psilocybin study participants saw improvements in depression and PTSD

The first multisite look at a real, legal, non-trial psilocybin market. That is genuinely new data, and it is also observational: no control group, self-selected participants, self-reported outcomes. A 95% improvement rate in an uncontrolled sample tells you about the population that showed up as much as about the drug. Read the harms line alongside the benefit line, because both are in the paper.

Published in JAMA Network Open, the study followed 346 people who completed a single session with a licensed Oregon psilocybin facilitator between November 2024 and June 2026. At three months, 95% reported decreases in moderate-to-severe depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms alongside gains in wellbeing and life satisfaction. Just over 91% said they benefited, and roughly 65% ranked the session among the ten most meaningful experiences of their life. Four first-time users had adverse behavioral reactions requiring medical attention, no serious physical events were reported, and seven participants described the experience as harmful at three months.

Source: Behavioral Health Business

The case for extending this work to firefighters rests on shared features: repeated life-threat exposure, moral injury, identity fused to the role, chronic sleep debt, and a culture that pays out for stoicism.

No. 08 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-12

Operator Syndrome and moral injury

Watch what happens when the operational lineage and the structural-critique lineage collide inside one diagnosis. Operator Syndrome was built as a medical cluster: TBI, endocrine dysfunction, chronic pain, sleep disruption. Adding moral injury as a specifier puts a violation of conscience next to a thyroid panel, and those two things do not respond to the same intervention. That tension is the content here, not a flaw to smooth over.

Chris Frueh's team coined Operator Syndrome in 2020 for the pattern clinicians kept seeing in special operations patients that no single diagnosis covered: traumatic brain injury entangled with endocrine dysfunction, chronic pain, sleep disruption, and existential distress including foreshortened future, survivor guilt, and loss of meaning after the mission ends. The original model excluded moral injury. Clinical ethicist Michele DeMarco proposed adding it in 2024, and the article reports it now carries as a specifier. A November 2025 validation study in active-duty SOF is in peer review. The piece argues moral injury needs work on guilt, meaning, and trust rather than a medical protocol.

Source: SOF News

Medical marijuana, firefighter/paramedics, and the difference between legal and right

Substance use policy for firefighter/paramedics is a live wire, and Arizona is not settled. Any client working a career-transition or substance-use question can hit this bright line and lose the job without an impairment finding. Mark's lane is the person in front of him, not the policy, but knowing that the legal terrain now varies by state changes what an honest conversation about disclosure sounds like.

The article centers on Hillsborough County firefighter/paramedic Anthony Giambrone, fired after a 2019 random test returned a marijuana metabolite. There was no allegation of on-duty use, impairment, or unsafe performance; he held a Florida medical marijuana card and used it off duty. The piece argues that automatic career-ending discipline for an unimpaired provider preserves an outdated bright-line rule rather than protecting the public. It lays out how state legalization, medical marijuana protections, federal law, collective bargaining agreements, and agency policy point in different directions. Maryland moved the other way when Governor Wes Moore signed Senate Bill 439 on April 28, 2026, barring employers from punishing firefighters, EMTs, cardiac rescue technicians, and paramedics for off-duty state-authorized medical cannabis use.

What The Odyssey teaches us about moral injury

The oldest description of the wound in the Western canon, and it is not a symptom list. Odysseus does not come home because he cannot; the sea is the shape of what he did. The three clusters named here map onto what any experienced responder recognizes in a colleague who is still functioning and no longer present. Worth holding next to the Desert Fathers on compunction, which describes the same territory without pathologizing it.

The piece reads Homer as one of the earliest portraits of moral injury and post-traumatic stress. It uses the Moral Injury Project definition: damage to conscience when a person perpetrates, witnesses, or fails to prevent acts that transgress their own moral code. Three clusters mark the injury: spiritual or existential collapse, guilt and shame, and alienation from others. Odysseus violates xenia, the sacred code of hospitality, and wins the war by deception; the long sea wandering follows as shame, guilt, and spiritual vacancy. The article also notes Impact of Killing, a 2022 CBT protocol for veterans that works on distorted self-appraisal with self-forgiveness at the center.

Source: Psychology Today
