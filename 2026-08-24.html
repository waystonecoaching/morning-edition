No. 01 · Peer Support · 2026-08-23

Bipartisan bill would fund firefighter, EMS mental health programs

Federal grant money decides which programs live. It also decides what counts as a legitimate service, because a grant line item is a definition with a budget attached. A solo coach sits outside the departmental pipeline but downstream of what it funds, since departments that get money build in-house and departments that do not keep referring out.

A bipartisan bill in Congress would establish grant funding for mental health services aimed at fire and EMS personnel. Coverage frames it as filling a gap that existing federal responder wellness money has not closed. The reporting does not yet specify whether awards would fund peer support infrastructure only or extend to contracted clinical and non-clinical services. Companion coverage appeared the same week on EMS1 and Firefighter Nation, which suggests the trade press is treating it as live rather than symbolic.

Try this: Find the bill number and read the eligible-use language. The distinction between funding peer teams and funding outside providers is the whole question for a practice like Waystone.

Two Years After Rejection, Resilient Quietly Refiles MDMA for PTSD Application

This is the file that shapes the standard of care for responder PTSD, so it deserves the closest reading of anything on today's list. The word doing the work in the headline is quietly. A sponsor confident in its data does not refile without a press cycle. Read the silence as information about what changed in the submission, which may be a narrower indication, a revised trial set, or a different framing of the functional-unblinding problem that sank the first application.

Resilient, the corporate successor to the MAPS drug development arm, has resubmitted its New Drug Application for MDMA-assisted therapy in PTSD roughly two years after the FDA issued a complete response letter. The 2024 rejection turned on trial design questions rather than a safety signal, including functional unblinding, therapist conduct, and the difficulty of separating drug effect from psychotherapy effect. Psychedelic Alpha notes the refiling surfaced without a coordinated announcement. What is not yet public is whether the company ran new trials or repackaged the existing dataset with revised analyses.

Try this: Do not update any client-facing language on MDMA until the acceptance-for-review decision lands. A refiling is not an approval and the gap between them is where most of the field's credibility damage has happened.
Source: Psychedelic Alpha

No. 03 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-07-29

Moral injury can be devastating: New assessment protocol makes it easier to identify those at risk

Assessment instruments quietly decide what gets treated, because what a scale measures is what a program funds. This one sits on a scope-of-practice edge that matters: a screening protocol invites a non-clinician to score a person, and scoring a person is diagnosis wearing work clothes. The useful move is to take the item content as a listening frame and leave the instrument to the clinicians.

Researchers report a new protocol intended to identify individuals at elevated risk of moral injury earlier than existing tools allow. Coverage positions it against the field's long-standing problem, which is that moral injury overlaps with PTSD on symptom surface while differing on mechanism, so PTSD instruments miss it. The reporting is press-release adjacent and does not give validation sample size, population, or discriminant performance against established measures such as the MIES or the Expressions of Moral Injury Scale. Whether this improves on what already exists is not answerable from the coverage.

Try this: Pull the underlying paper before treating the protocol as usable. If the validation sample is student or general-population rather than occupational, it says little about a fire crew.
Source: Medical Xpress

No. 04 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-13

Operator Syndrome and Moral Injury

This one sits squarely in the operational and applied camp rather than the structural-critique camp, and it is worth naming that rather than smoothing it. Operator Syndrome treats the damage as an accumulated physiological and neurological load in the individual. Litz and Shay would ask what the institution did and who broke faith. Both readings are true of a twenty-year fire career and they point at different interventions, which is exactly why the tension is content and not a problem to fix.

The piece works Frueh's Operator Syndrome construct against moral injury as a separate lens on the same population. Operator Syndrome bundles sleep disruption, endocrine dysfunction, repetitive blast and head trauma, chronic pain, substance use, and relational collapse into a single clinical picture built from special operations veterans. Moral injury names the wound differently, locating it in transgression, betrayal, and the failure of leadership rather than in tissue and hormone. The argument is that the two overlap heavily in presentation and that treating one while ignoring the other produces partial recovery.

Try this: The fire service has an unnamed analog to Operator Syndrome. Sleep debt across decades of shift work, repeated exposure, orthopedic damage, and the drinking culture form one picture. Naming it in intake language may open a conversation that the PTSD frame closes.
Source: SOF News

The Swara-Informed model of autonomic synchrony in psychotherapy: theoretical foundations and hypotheses

If you describe the polyvagal frame to clients as autonomic state being the substrate of behavior, this paper proposes a mechanism for the part you already do without naming: that the practitioner's own state is an active ingredient, not a background condition. Hold it loosely. It is a hypotheses paper, which means it is a well-formed argument with no outcome data behind it, and the field is already crowded with polyvagal-adjacent theory that outran its evidence.

The authors propose that autonomic dysregulation is a shared mechanism across anxiety, trauma-related, and mood presentations, with persistent sympathetic hyperarousal at one pole and dorsal shutdown or dissociation at the other. They build a model in which therapeutic change depends on autonomic synchrony between clinician and client rather than on content processing alone, drawing on the Swara tradition of breath and nasal-cycle regulation as an organizing frame. The paper states testable hypotheses and proposes measurement approaches. It reports no trial results.

Try this: Read it for the hypotheses section, not the conclusions. If the synchrony claim holds, your own regulation before a session is a clinical variable and belongs in your pre-session practice rather than in your notes about the client.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology
No. 06 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-08-20
95%

95% of Oregon Psilocybin Study Participants Saw Improvements in Depression, PTSD

The number is doing a lot of persuading and very little proving. This is observational data from people who chose to pay for and attend psilocybin services in a legal-access state, measured by self-report, with no control arm and no blinding. That design cannot separate drug effect from selection, expectancy, and the fact that people who book a supervised eight-hour session have already decided something needs to change. Useful as a safety and feasibility signal. Not useful as efficacy.

An OHSU-led study of participants in Oregon's regulated psilocybin services program reports that roughly 95 percent showed improvement on measures of anxiety, depression, or PTSD symptoms following sessions. The study draws on the state's supervised non-clinical access model rather than a research protocol. Coverage across KGW, KOIN, and KTVZ ran the same headline figure without much attention to design. The strongest legitimate finding here is that a state-regulated, non-medical delivery model operated at scale without notable adverse-event signal.

Try this: If a client cites this study, the honest response is that it tells us the Oregon model is workable and tells us almost nothing about whether psilocybin caused the improvement.
Source: Behavioral Health Business

No. 07 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-19

The protective role of memory suppression in psychological resilience among young adults with childhood trauma

The study examines memory suppression ability in young adults with childhood trauma histories and finds it associated with better psychological resilience rather than worse outcomes. This runs against the clinical folk model in which suppression is avoidance and avoidance maintains symptoms. The sample is young adults, not an occupational trauma population, and the design is cross-sectional, so it establishes association rather than direction. The mechanism proposed is inhibitory control over retrieval rather than conscious effortful avoidance, which is a meaningful distinction the coverage mostly loses.

Suppression is not the opposite of integration. It is what a system does when integration is not yet safe.

This cuts against integration-as-path and should be read all the way through rather than around. If suppression capacity predicts better functioning in trauma-exposed adults, then pushing a client toward material before their system can hold it is not neutral. Polyvagal reading resolves some of the tension: suppression may be what a nervous system does competently when ventral safety is not yet established. Non-directive inquiry already respects this, since the client sets the pace. The paper is a reminder of why that constraint is not merely courteous.

Source: Nature (via Scientific Reports)

MAPS and State of Colorado Partner to Deliver Free Harm Reduction Training for First Responders

This is the piece on today's list closest to your actual chair. Colorado is training first responders to manage psychedelic crisis in the field, which is the behavioral crisis role meeting the psychedelic access wave head on. Arizona has no comparable framework and its responders will meet the same calls anyway, because access does not wait for policy. Advocacy-organization training carries an obvious conflict of interest, so evaluate the curriculum on its de-escalation content rather than on its source.

MAPS partnered with the Colorado Department of Revenue's Natural Medicine Division to deliver its Psychedelic Crisis Assessment and Intervention training to first responders statewide at no cost. The training addresses field assessment and de-escalation for people in acute psychedelic distress, an encounter type that regulated access states now generate at volume. Colorado's healing center model creates both supervised sessions and unsupervised personal-use situations, and responders meet the second category more often. The program is state-sanctioned rather than department-specific.

Try this: Ask whether MAPS licenses this curriculum outside Colorado. A behavioral crisis specialist who can teach psychedelic-specific de-escalation to a Phoenix crew is occupying ground nobody else in Arizona is standing on.
Source: MAPS

PTSI, Psychedelics, and the Fire Service

The trade press is now saying out loud what crews have been doing quietly for years. That matters for you twice over: it changes what a client will admit in a first session, and it puts anyone doing integration work near a controlled-substance conversation with employment and licensure consequences attached. Scope-of-practice edge, stated plainly. Integration coaching after a legal or out-of-state experience is one thing. Anything that reads as facilitating access is another, and the line has to be visible in your intake paperwork, not just in your head.

Firefighter Nation covers psychedelic use among firefighters carrying post-traumatic stress injury, using PTSI rather than PTSD as the framing, which is the language the fire service has been moving toward for a decade. The piece treats underground and out-of-country use as an existing reality rather than a hypothetical. It raises the gap between what departments prohibit, what employee assistance programs can address, and where members actually go. Trade coverage of this kind tends to precede policy conversation by a year or two.

Try this: Review your intake language on prior and planned substance use. If it does not distinguish integration support from facilitation, it needs to before this becomes a common presenting topic.
Source: Firefighter Nation

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-17

What The Odyssey Teaches Us About Moral Injury

Shay walked this road first and the ground still holds. The point is not that Homer anticipated a diagnosis. The point is that the return is the hard part, that it takes ten years while the war took ten, and that a man can survive everything and still not be able to walk into his own house. The Desert Fathers knew the same thing under a different name. Coming back is its own labor and nobody does it alone.

The essay reads the Odyssey as a text about nostos, the long and failed return, rather than as a war story. Odysseus loses every man he brought home, arrives unrecognized and in disguise, and cannot simply resume the life he left. The argument connects that structure to moral injury as distinct from fear-based trauma, since what obstructs the return is what he did and what was done in his name rather than what frightened him. Shay's Odysseus in America made this case in 2002 and the piece stands in that lineage.

Source: Psychology Today
