Research finds moral injury affects nearly two-thirds of Texas first responders

This is the structural-critique lineage, not the operational one. Moral injury at this prevalence is not a story about individual resilience deficits. It is a story about what the job asks people to do and what the institution does with them afterward. Read it alongside Litz and Shay rather than alongside a wellness program brochure.

A new Right On Crime study reports moral injury symptoms in close to two-thirds of the Texas first responders surveyed. The framing places the finding inside criminal justice reform work rather than clinical psychiatry, which is itself worth noting: the organization is arguing that responder harm is a policy problem. Prevalence at this level puts moral injury in the same tier as the exposure statistics departments already track. Treat the specific instrument and sampling method as unverified until the full report is in hand.

Try this: Pull the methodology section before quoting the two-thirds figure to anyone. Prevalence numbers in this space vary widely by instrument.
Source: Right On Crime

Bipartisan bill would fund firefighter and EMS mental health programs

Federal money changes what a department can build. If this moves, behavioral health roles inside fire agencies stop being pilot projects funded out of the overtime line. That is your role, and it is the funding path for the peer and clinician structures around it.

A bipartisan bill in Congress would direct funding toward firefighter and EMS mental health programs. Coverage frames it as grant support for departmental behavioral health infrastructure rather than individual treatment benefits. Bills of this shape have stalled before at appropriations, so the introduction matters less than whether it survives markup. Watch which committee it lands in.

Try this: Find the bill number and set a tracker on it. If it advances, Phoenix Fire's grant office should know before the trade press writes it up.

Police officer suicide: how northern Illinois is breaking the silence

Regional reporting on responder suicide, written from inside the culture rather than about it. This sits on a scope-of-practice edge. Suicidality is clinical territory, and the coaching frame does not cover it. What a coach can hold is the silence around it, which is where the reporting is strongest.

The piece covers how agencies and survivors in northern Illinois are working to make officer suicide speakable. It centers the culture of silence rather than intervention protocols. Local reporting like this is useful mostly as a read on how shift culture is changing, not as evidence about what works. The named programs are worth checking against outcome data before recommending them.

Source: Shaw Local

Oregon psilocybin services linked to major drops in anxiety, depression and PTSD

This is real-world services data out of Oregon's regulated non-clinical model, not a randomized trial. That distinction is the whole story. Self-selected clients, no control arm, self-reported outcomes. It tells you something about who walks into these services and how they describe themselves afterward. It does not establish efficacy, and the field is under enough credibility stress that the difference matters.

An OHSU-led analysis of Oregon's psilocybin services program reports substantial reductions in self-reported anxiety, depression, and PTSD symptoms among participants. The Oregon model operates outside the medical system, with facilitators rather than clinicians. Effect sizes reported from open-label, self-selected samples routinely shrink or vanish under blinding. Read the pre-post design as a description of a population, not a treatment result.

Try this: When a client cites Oregon numbers, name the design limitation without dismissing their experience. Both things are true at once.
Source: KTVZ (OHSU study)

Findings about resilience after trauma have been robust, yet mostly ignored

The Bonanno line: most people exposed to trauma do not develop chronic pathology, and the field keeps building programs as though they do. This cuts against the assumption baked into a lot of responder wellness work. It also cuts against the sales pitch of every intervention, including some you might otherwise favor.

The article argues that decades of trajectory research showing resilience as the modal outcome after trauma exposure have been largely absorbed but not acted on. The practical implication is that universal prescriptive interventions may do little for the majority and may pathologize normal recovery. The counterweight is that responder populations carry cumulative rather than single-incident exposure, which is where the resilience finding is least established. Hold both.

Try this: Audit your intake language for assumptions that a hard call must have caused damage. Ask rather than assume.
Source: Psychology Today

The constraint is almost never knowledge. It is sustained attention.

The practical end of the operational lineage. Most departments are not going to fund a behavioral health division. No. 09 · Coaching Practice · 2026-08-24

How motivational interviewing can help with common mental health conditions

Written for general practitioners, which makes it a useful mirror. Motivational interviewing shares ancestry with non-directive inquiry: both refuse to push, both treat ambivalence as information rather than obstruction. Where it differs is that MI has a destination in mind. Worth watching that difference in your own work.

A clinical viewpoint on applying motivational interviewing to common presentations in primary care. The core moves are familiar: reflective listening, rolling with resistance, eliciting change talk rather than supplying it. The evidence base for MI in brief encounters is reasonably solid, which is more than most conversational methods can claim. The primary care framing compresses it into a shorter container than coaching typically allows.

Try this: Notice where you are steering. MI is honest about having a target. Non-directive inquiry is not supposed to have one.
Source: GPonline

No. 10 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-26

Nolan's The Odyssey is an emotive meditation on modern moral injury

Shay got here first, and got here through the same door. The Iliad and the Odyssey have been the working texts for combat trauma and homecoming since the early nineties, because the ancients described the wound before anyone had a diagnostic code for it. A film reading is a lighter instrument, but it points at the same thing: the harder part is not the war, it is the return.

Academic commentary reading Christopher Nolan's Odyssey adaptation through the lens of moral injury and the difficulty of homecoming. The argument follows Jonathan Shay's line that Homer preserves a clinical picture of the returning warrior that modern categories flatten. Odysseus takes ten years to get home and arrives unrecognizable, which is the part that lands in a firehouse. The piece is criticism, not research, and should be read that way.

Source: Durham University
