New Right On Crime Research Finds Moral Injury Affects Nearly Two-Thirds of Texas First Responders

This is the prevalence number your whole practice rests on. If moral injury reaches nearly two thirds of a state's first responder workforce, then what walks into your sessions is not an outlier population. It is the baseline. Note the source: Right On Crime is a criminal justice reform outfit, not a trauma research center, so read the methodology before you quote the figure to a chief.

A new study from Right On Crime reports that close to two thirds of surveyed Texas first responders show markers of moral injury. The framing is structural rather than clinical. It points at organizational betrayal, impossible mandates, and the gap between what the job demands and what the system permits. The policy ask is for state level investment in responder mental health infrastructure.

Try this: Pull the methodology section before citing the two thirds figure. If it holds, it is a stronger opening line for Waystone's first responder page than anything currently there.

OPINION: System failing first responders who suffer with trauma

The structural critique lineage, stated plainly and from the field rather than from a journal. The argument is that responders are not failing at resilience. The system around them is failing at duty of care. Litz and Shay would recognize the shape of this.

An opinion piece arguing that first responders carrying occupational trauma are routinely failed by the systems meant to catch them: slow claims processes, thin coverage, and treatment pathways that assume a person can advocate for themselves while in the worst state of their life. The writer contrasts the ceremonial language departments use about wellness with what members actually encounter when they ask for help.

Police officer suicide: How northern Illinois is breaking the silence

Law enforcement suicide handled as a culture problem rather than an individual one. Worth reading for how the departments describe breaking silence, since that is the same move you are making inside Phoenix Fire. This one sits squarely on the scope of practice edge. Suicidality is clinical territory, and a coaching frame does not cover it. Read it for the culture change mechanics, not for intervention technique.

Northern Illinois departments are going public about officer suicide after years of treating it as a private matter. The piece covers peer support build out, chaplaincy, and the slow work of making it survivable to say you are struggling. Leadership visibility is the recurring variable: when a command officer names it first, use of services goes up.

Federal money moving toward fire and EMS behavioral health. If this passes, grant funded peer support and clinician networks expand, and departments that had no line item suddenly have one. That changes the market a solo practitioner works inside.

Mystical experiences don’t explain the success of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD

This cuts against the mystical experience story that a good part of the integration field leans on. If the peak experience is not what carries the therapeutic effect in MDMA work for PTSD, then the mechanism sits somewhere else, likely in the felt safety and fear extinction window. That is a polyvagal argument, and it changes how you would describe integration to a client.

Analysis of MDMA-assisted therapy trial data found that mystical type experience scores did not account for symptom improvement in PTSD outcomes. Participants who reported little in the way of transcendent experience improved comparably to those who reported a great deal. The authors suggest the working ingredient is closer to reduced fear response during trauma processing than to insight delivered by a peak state.

Try this: If your integration language leans on the peak experience as the healing event, revise it. The safer claim is that the medicine opens a window in which approach becomes possible.
Source: PsyPost

No. 07 · Polyvagal · 2026-08-29

We have been convinced that traumatic experiences are life sentences. Why?

The piece argues that cultural messaging around trauma has drifted toward permanence, and that this framing itself becomes a barrier to recovery. It reviews evidence that most people who experience serious trauma do not develop chronic disorder, and that expectation shapes outcome. The critique is aimed less at survivors than at the professional and media language surrounding them.

The expectation of permanence is itself part of what makes it permanent.

A direct challenge to the life sentence framing of trauma. This is the same ground you work in every intake, where a member has been told, sometimes by a well meaning clinician, that this is now permanent. The nervous system is more plastic than that narrative allows.

Source: Psychology Today

No. 08 · Resilience & Moral Injury · 2026-08-25

Findings about resilience after trauma have been robust, yet mostly ignored.

The other half of the same coin, and the more uncomfortable half. Post traumatic resilience findings have been robust for decades and get very little airtime, partly because the field's funding and attention structure rewards pathology. Hold this alongside the moral injury data in story one rather than letting either one win.

A review of resilience research showing that recovery without lasting disorder is the statistically ordinary outcome after trauma, not the exception. The author argues these findings have been sidelined because they sit awkwardly against both clinical incentive structures and advocacy narratives. The point is not that trauma is harmless, but that the default trajectory bends toward recovery when conditions allow.

Source: Psychology Today

Viewpoint: How motivational interviewing can help with common mental health conditions

Motivational interviewing as a practitioner tool, written for general practice clinicians. The overlap with non directive inquiry is substantial: evocation over installation, rolling with resistance, change talk arising from the client rather than the practitioner. Useful for sharpening where your BTTY trained inquiry differs from MI, which matters when you are explaining scope to a referring clinician.

A clinical viewpoint on applying motivational interviewing to common mental health presentations in primary care. It covers the core stance of partnership and evocation, the practical constraint of short appointments, and the evidence base for ambivalence focused conversation in depression, anxiety, and substance use. The emphasis is on the practitioner resisting the pull to fix.

Try this: Write one paragraph on how non directive inquiry and MI differ. You will need it the next time a clinician asks what exactly you do.

No. 10 · Contemplative · 2026-08-31

Why balance matters more than totals: a candidate structural model of non-compensable wellbeing, duratva, and Buddhist contemplative practice

A structural model arguing that wellbeing does not sum. You cannot offset a deficit in one domain with a surplus in another, which is why the wealthy report emptiness and the accomplished report paralysis. The Buddhist contemplative frame is not the hesychast one, but the diagnosis is the same one the Desert Fathers made: a life can be full and still be starved at the center.

The paper proposes a model of non compensable wellbeing, where domains of a life cannot substitute for one another and a shortfall in one cannot be paid off with abundance in another. It draws on Buddhist contemplative categories to describe balance as structural rather than additive. The argument is aimed at positive psychology's tendency to total up life satisfaction as if it were a ledger.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
