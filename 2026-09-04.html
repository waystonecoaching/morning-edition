New Zealand drugs regulator approves use of MDMA to treat severe PTSD

New Zealand is now the second country after Australia to let psychiatrists prescribe MDMA for severe PTSD. Regulatory approval is not the same as an evidence verdict, and this one arrives while the field is still carrying the FDA's 2024 rejection of Lykos and the unresolved questions about functional unblinding and therapist conduct that came with it. Two countries approving under tight authorised-prescriber schemes is a real signal about access policy, and a weak signal about efficacy. Read it as the former.

New Zealand's medicines regulator has cleared MDMA-assisted therapy for severe post-traumatic stress disorder. Coverage across outlets indicates a narrow authorised-prescriber pathway rather than open market approval, with only two psychiatrists initially cleared to prescribe. This places New Zealand alongside Australia, which opened a similar restricted route in 2023. Reporting so far is regulatory rather than clinical; no new trial data accompanies the decision.

Try this: If a client raises this, hold the distinction out loud: two small countries opened a restricted prescribing lane. That is not the standard of care in Arizona and it is not a route available to them here. Scope note: prescribing and clinical treatment sit outside a coach's lane. Integration support after the fact does not.

A cognitive model of moral and nonmoral self-criticism: the role of judgment criteria

Self-criticism is the mechanism most first responders run on and almost none of them name. This paper does something useful by splitting moral self-criticism from the nonmoral kind and locating the difference in the judgment criteria a person applies to themselves. That split maps cleanly onto the line between a responder who thinks they performed badly and one who thinks they became someone bad. Different wound, different work.

The authors review existing theoretical models of self-criticism and argue each captures a different slice of the phenomenon. They propose a cognitive framework organised around the judgment criteria people use to evaluate themselves, distinguishing moral self-criticism from nonmoral self-criticism. The claim is that the criterion applied, not just the severity of the judgment, determines what kind of self-attack follows. This is conceptual work, not an empirical test.

Try this: In your next session where self-blame shows up, ask which standard the client is measuring against: a performance standard or a moral one. The answer changes the whole shape of the inquiry.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Ohio city to route some 911 medical calls to nurses instead of EMS

Nurse triage of low-acuity 911 traffic is the same structural move that put you in a behavioral crisis seat: pull the calls that never needed an engine off the engine. Every one of these programs generates the same two arguments, about liability and about who owns the risk of a wrong sort. Watch how this city answers them, because Phoenix will be asked the same questions about behavioral routing.

An Ohio city is diverting a subset of low-acuity 911 medical calls to nurse triage rather than dispatching an EMS unit. The stated aim is to preserve ambulance availability for higher-acuity work. Reporting is at the announcement stage; no outcome or safety data yet exists for this deployment. Similar programs elsewhere have reported reduced transport volume alongside ongoing debate over triage accuracy.

Try this: Note it as precedent for the alternative-response argument. If it holds, it is one more data point that the sort is the intervention.

Generative AI and Moral Injury

Moral injury is a betrayal wound: someone with authority made a call that violated what you hold, or a system put you in a position where every option was wrong. The interesting question here is whether decisions offloaded to a machine produce the same wound with nobody left to be angry at. Betrayal with no betrayer is a hard thing to metabolise. Keep an eye on this category as automated triage and dispatch tools spread into the field.

The piece considers whether generative AI systems can produce or worsen moral injury when they are placed inside consequential decisions. The framing extends Litz and Shay's territory into decisions where accountability is diffused across a tool. Available at headline and abstract level only; the full argument is behind the source.

Source: Psychology Today

Operator Syndrome and Moral Injury

Operator Syndrome is the special-operations construct for the whole stack at once: endocrine disruption, sleep collapse, repeated blast exposure, chronic pain, and moral injury sitting on top. It is the closest thing in the literature to what a twenty-year engine company career actually produces, and it is more honest than treating PTSD as the single diagnosis. This sits in the applied camp rather than the structural-critique camp; it names the physiology without much interest in who set the conditions. Worth holding both.

The article connects the Operator Syndrome construct, which bundles physiological, neurological, and psychological sequelae of sustained high-tempo operations, with moral injury as one of its components. The argument is that treating any single element in isolation misses the interaction. Source is a special-operations trade outlet rather than a peer-reviewed journal; treat the synthesis as field-informed opinion.

Try this: The multi-system framing is a useful client handout concept. When someone lists sleep, gut, pain, and shame as separate problems, the syndrome frame lets you say they are one thing without pathologising them.
Source: SOF News

State-level dedicated funding streams for responder mental health are the mechanism that turns peer support from a volunteer favour into a staffed program. The pattern to watch is not the dollar amount but the structure: who administers it, whether it survives an administration change, and whether clinician contracts come with it or only training. Those three answers determine whether a program is still running in three years.

The Northern Kentucky Police Chiefs Association is among the first recipients of a state mental health support fund for law enforcement. Reporting is at the award-announcement stage. No program design or outcome detail is available beyond the funding award itself.

Try this: Check whether Arizona has an equivalent named fund and who administers it. A solo practice that knows the funding channel is easier to route referrals into.

No. 07 · Moral Injury · 2026-09-01

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in Healthcare

The article addresses moral injury among healthcare workers and what individual clinicians can do about it. Framed as self-management within a system the writer treats as the source of the injury. Headline-level access only; the tension between individual coping and structural cause is the thing to read for.

The wound is not that they failed. The wound is that the system asked them to.

Healthcare moral injury and responder moral injury are the same structure with different uniforms: trained to a standard, then placed where the standard cannot be met, then held responsible for the gap. This piece sits in the structural-critique lineage rather than the resilience-training one, which matters, because those two camps prescribe opposite things. One says build the person's tolerance. The other says the tolerance is not the problem.

Port Coquitlam Local 1941 welcomes Phil, a new partner in fire fighter behavioral health

Easy to file this under feel-good and move on. Do not. An occupational stress intervention dog is a co-regulation device with a union budget line, and the polyvagal read is exact: a body that will not come down off sympathetic activation for a peer debrief will often come down for an animal that wants nothing from it. No performance demand, no story to manage, just another nervous system in the room offering cues of safety.

IAFF Local 1941 in Port Coquitlam, British Columbia has added an occupational stress intervention dog named Phil to its behavioral health supports. The dog is deployed as an additional source of support for members after high-stress incidents and traumatic calls. It supplements rather than replaces existing peer support and clinical resources.

Try this: Worth raising with Phoenix behavioral health as a low-cost adjunct on post-incident deployments, positioned as autonomic support rather than morale.

We have been convinced that traumatic experiences are life sentences. Why?

This is the argument that matters most for how you describe your framework. If autonomic state is a substrate rather than a sentence, then the whole point of the work is that state is reachable. The risk on the other side is real too: overcorrect and you land in a facile you-can-just-heal position that insults people whose bodies have not moved in twenty years. The honest line is that the pattern is plastic and the timeline is not yours to promise.

The piece challenges the cultural assumption that trauma produces permanent, irreversible damage. It argues the life-sentence framing is itself part of what keeps people stuck, and that recovery language in the field has drifted toward chronicity. It is opinion writing in a general-audience outlet, not new data.

Try this: Audit your own site language for anything that implies permanence. Substrate is not sentence, and the words a client reads before the first session set what they think is possible.
Source: Psychology Today

Nolan's The Odyssey is an emotive meditation on modern moral injury

Homer got there first. The Odyssey is a homecoming story in which the coming home is the hard part, and Jonathan Shay built an entire clinical literature on exactly that reading. The reason this belongs at the end of the issue is that it says something the research papers cannot: the wound is old, it has been described before, and men have found their way through it in every century. That is not sentiment. That is the only evidence base that runs longer than a funding cycle.

Durham University classicists read Christopher Nolan's Odyssey adaptation as a study of moral injury and the difficulty of return. The piece connects the epic's account of a warrior's homecoming to contemporary moral injury scholarship, following the interpretive line Jonathan Shay opened in Odysseus in America. It is scholarly commentary on a film, not clinical research.

Source: Durham University
