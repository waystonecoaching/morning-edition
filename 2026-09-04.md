### 02. A cognitive model of moral and nonmoral self-criticism: the role of judgment criteria

**Source:** Frontiers in Psychology  \
**Category:** research  \
**Published:** 2026-09-04  \
**Link:** [https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1824090](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1824090)

**Why this matters to you:** Self-criticism is the mechanism most first responders run on and almost none of them name. This paper does something useful by splitting moral self-criticism from the nonmoral kind and locating the difference in the judgment criteria a person applies to themselves. That split maps cleanly onto the line between a responder who thinks they performed badly and one who thinks they became someone bad. Different wound, different work.

**The story:** The authors review existing theoretical models of self-criticism and argue each captures a different slice of the phenomenon. They propose a cognitive framework organised around the judgment criteria people use to evaluate themselves, distinguishing moral self-criticism from nonmoral self-criticism. The claim is that the criterion applied, not just the severity of the judgment, determines what kind of self-attack follows. This is conceptual work, not an empirical test.

**Actionable:** In your next session where self-blame shows up, ask which standard the client is measuring against: a performance standard or a moral one. The answer changes the whole shape of the inquiry.
