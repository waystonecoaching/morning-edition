New Zealand drugs regulator approves use of MDMA to treat severe PTSD

New Zealand becomes the second country after Australia to let psychiatrists prescribe MDMA for severe PTSD. That is a regulatory fact, not a clinical verdict. What it changes for you is the room: clients who read the headline will arrive believing the question is settled, and you will be the one holding the difference between approved access and demonstrated benefit. This sits on a scope-of-practice edge. Prescribing, screening, and medical supervision are not yours; integration is.

Medsafe, the New Zealand medicines regulator, has authorized MDMA for severe post-traumatic stress disorder under psychiatrist prescription. Australia opened the same door in 2023 and the real-world outcome data from that decision remains thin. Coverage across roughly a dozen outlets carries the same regulatory facts and very little on eligibility criteria, session structure, or who pays. The interesting question is not whether the drug works but whether a national health system can build the delivery container the trials assumed.

Try this: Draft two paragraphs of plain language for the Waystone site on what integration coaching is and is not, so you have an answer ready when a client leads with the headline.

Operator Syndrome and Moral Injury

This is the operational lineage speaking, not the structural-critique lineage. Operator Syndrome frames the damage as a cluster of physiological and cognitive sequelae from sustained high-tempo work: sleep, endocrine, TBI, pain, relational erosion. Shay and Litz would say that framing risks turning betrayal into a medical chart. Both readings are true at once, and the tension is the useful part. Fire has its own version of this argument and mostly has not had it out loud.

The piece places moral injury inside the Operator Syndrome model developed from special operations populations, where physical, neurological, and moral wounds are treated as a single interacting picture rather than separate diagnoses. It argues the moral component is routinely missed because it does not present as a symptom you can measure. The operational frame gives you leverage on sleep, pain, and performance. It gives you less on the question of what was asked of a person and by whom.

Try this: When a responder client presents with a sleep and pain cluster, run one question about the moral ledger before you route to the physiological explanation.

Ohio city to route some 911 medical calls to nurses instead of EMS

This is the alternate-response argument arriving on the medical side of the house, and it is the same argument you live inside on the behavioral side. Nurse triage for low-acuity medical calls is the sibling of a behavioral crisis unit for low-acuity psych calls. Watch the failure modes: undertriage, liability, and the slow drift where the alternate unit becomes the dumping ground for everything the system finds inconvenient.

An Ohio city will divert a subset of low-acuity 911 medical calls to nurse triage rather than dispatching an ambulance. The stated aim is unit availability and reducing non-emergency transports. Programs of this shape live or die on the triage protocol and on who carries the risk when a call is miscategorized. The reporting is brief on both points.

Try this: Pull the Phoenix behavioral dispatch criteria and read them next to this. Note where the medical model has language your side lacks.

Pa+ Library: Psychedelic REMS

REMS is the boring machinery that will decide whether integration practitioners have a lane at all. Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies determine who may prescribe, in what setting, with what monitoring, and with what documentation. If MDMA or psilocybin clears FDA, the REMS is the document that says whether a non-clinical integration coach sits inside or outside the certified pathway. This is the BTTY question in regulatory form.

Psychedelic Alpha has assembled a reference library on REMS as it would apply to psychedelic therapies. REMS are FDA-imposed safeguards layered on top of approval, used when a drug's risk profile requires controls beyond labeling. Applied here they would likely govern prescriber certification, treatment setting, and monitored session requirements. Most of the specifics remain undecided, which is exactly why practitioners should be reading now rather than after.

Try this: Ask BTTY directly whether they are tracking REMS design and whether practitioner pathways are being represented in comment periods.

A cognitive model of moral and nonmoral self-criticism: the role of judgment criteria

Self-criticism is the water your clients swim in, and this paper does something useful with it: it separates the criteria a person judges themselves by from the act of judging. That distinction is operationally live. A firefighter flogging himself over a moral criterion needs a different conversation than one flogging himself over a competence criterion, and the field usually treats both as one undifferentiated symptom.

The authors review existing theoretical models of self-criticism and propose a cognitive framework centered on judgment criteria, meaning the standards against which a person evaluates the self. They argue this component explains why self-criticism looks so different across people despite scoring similarly on measures. Moral and nonmoral criteria are distinguished, with different downstream affective consequences. It is conceptual work, not trial data, so treat it as a lens rather than evidence.

Try this: In your next session with a self-critical client, ask what standard they are measuring against before you touch the criticism itself.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

First responders lean on peer support to heal one year after the Canfield Mountain ambush

One year is the interval where organizational attention has left and the nervous system has not. Anniversary coverage tends to record the ceremony and miss the arc. What matters here is whether peer support persisted past the acute phase or whether it surged and receded with the news cycle, which is the usual pattern.

Idaho first responders who worked the Canfield Mountain ambush, in which firefighters were shot after being lured to a brush fire, describe relying on peer support in the year since. The report centers on continuing use of peer teams rather than a single debrief. It does not say what the program looked like structurally or whether it was resourced beyond the first weeks. The gap between acute mobilization and sustained availability is where most department programs fail.

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in Healthcare

The piece addresses moral injury among clinicians, distinguishing it from burnout by locating the wound in transgressed moral expectation rather than depleted capacity. It surveys management strategies at the individual level: meaning-making, peer disclosure, and reconnection to purpose. What it largely leaves alone is the institutional side, where staffing, throughput pressure, and administrative decisions generate the transgressions in the first place. That omission is itself the story.

The wound is not that they could not cope. The wound is that coping was the only thing on offer.

Healthcare is running the same experiment fire ran twenty years ago: name the injury, then treat it with individual resilience training while leaving the conditions untouched. Read this as a case study in how the structural critique gets absorbed and defanged. The language of moral injury travels fast. The implication that leadership caused it travels slowly.

Try this: Watch your own language this week for the moment you offer an individual coping frame where a structural one is true.

Attorney General Coleman launches peer support program for law enforcement

A statewide, top-down peer support build is a different animal than a department program. It brings funding and standardization, and it brings the risk that peer support becomes a compliance box with a state logo on it. Kentucky is also standing up 'Stand by the Badge' funding in the same window, so this is a coordinated policy push worth tracking as a model rather than a headline.

Kentucky's attorney general has launched a statewide peer support program for law enforcement, coordinated at the state level rather than department by department. Companion funding is going to regional police chief associations under a separate mental health support initiative. The reporting does not describe training standards, confidentiality protections, or how peers are selected. Those three details determine whether officers use it.

Try this: Track whether Kentucky publishes its peer selection and confidentiality standards. Statewide models are where usable templates come from.

No. 09 · Polyvagal · 2026-08-31

We have been convinced that traumatic experiences are life sentences. Why?

This is the argument you make in the room, made in public. If autonomic state is substrate rather than sentence, then the story a person tells about permanence is itself a state-dependent story. The risk on the other side is real: push too hard on plasticity and you land on 'you should be over this,' which is its own injury. Hold both.

The article challenges the cultural framing of trauma as a permanent condition, arguing that popular trauma discourse has hardened into an identity narrative that outlives the physiology it describes. It points to evidence on recovery trajectories and the ordinariness of post-event adaptation. The counterweight it gives less attention to is that permanence framing often arises where conditions have not changed and the nervous system is reading the environment correctly.

Try this: Notice which of your clients hold permanence as belief and which hold it as accurate reading of an unchanged environment. The intervention differs.

What 'The Odyssey' Teaches Us About Moral Injury

Shay got here first with Odysseus in America, and the fact that the argument is circulating again says something about where the culture is. The nostos, the long return home, is not a metaphor for integration. It is the same thing described before we had clinical language for it. Ten years to cross a sea that took ten days to cross going out.

The piece reads the Odyssey as an account of the return rather than the war, locating moral injury in what Odysseus carries home and cannot set down. It draws on Jonathan Shay's framing of the veteran's homecoming as the harder campaign. The value is not new evidence but old attention: the recognition that the injury of return has been described for three thousand years and repeatedly forgotten. The recovery of that memory is itself the work.
