## Editor's Note

Two nations now permit MDMA for severe PTSD and neither has shown its work on delivery. Meanwhile the moral injury literature keeps circling the same question from four directions: the operator's body, the clinician's ledger, the officer's peer, and Odysseus on the water for ten years going home. The Fathers would have recognized that last one. What is asked of a man and what he can bear are not the same measurement, and the gap between them has a name older than our language for it. Read slowly today.

### 05. A cognitive model of moral and nonmoral self-criticism: the role of judgment criteria

**Source:** Frontiers in Psychology  \
**Category:** mental_resilience  \
**Published:** 2026-09-04  \
**Link:** [https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1824090](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1824090)

**Why this matters to you:** Self-criticism is the water your clients swim in, and this paper does something useful with it: it separates the criteria a person judges themselves by from the act of judging. That distinction is operationally live. A firefighter flogging himself over a moral criterion needs a different conversation than one flogging himself over a competence criterion, and the field usually treats both as one undifferentiated symptom.

**The story:** The authors review existing theoretical models of self-criticism and propose a cognitive framework centered on judgment criteria, meaning the standards against which a person evaluates the self. They argue this component explains why self-criticism looks so different across people despite scoring similarly on measures. Moral and nonmoral criteria are distinguished, with different downstream affective consequences. It is conceptual work, not trial data, so treat it as a lens rather than evidence.

**Actionable:** In your next session with a self-critical client, ask what standard they are measuring against before you touch the criticism itself.
