Davis, Farchione and Colleagues Present FDA's Psychedelic Drug Framework in NEJM

This is the agency writing its own terms into the record, two years after it sent Lykos home over MDMA for PTSD and three months after the final clinical guidance landed. For anyone trained through BTTY, the regulatory lane is the lane. It decides which conversations are integration and which are practicing medicine without a license. Read it as the boundary of your scope moving, not as good news or bad news.

Four FDA officials from the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, among them Tiffany Farchione and Bernard Fischer of the Division of Psychiatry, published a commentary in the September 10 New England Journal of Medicine laying out what the agency calls its new framework for psychedelic drugs. They open by naming the scale of the depression and PTSD burden in the United States, state that FDA intends to meet the moment with urgency, and name psychedelic compounds as one route to better treatments. The piece follows the final guidance on clinical investigations of psychedelic drugs released in July, three years after the draft. It arrives in a field still working through the 2024 Complete Response Letter to Lykos Therapeutics.

Try this: Read the NEJM commentary itself, not the trade coverage, before you describe the regulatory picture to a client considering a legal pathway.

Polyvagal Theory debunked in comprehensive new study

This one lands on your own floorboards. If you name polyvagal on the Waystone site and in session, you now need to know exactly which claim you are making. The critique goes after the evolutionary story, the anatomy, and the measurement. It does not touch the clinical observation that autonomic state shapes what a man can perceive and who he can stay in contact with. Know the difference cold, because a client who reads a headline will ask.

Paul Grossman and thirty eight other autonomic nervous system scholars published a paper in Clinical Neuropsychiatry titled Why the Polyvagal Theory is Untenable. They make three arguments: the evolutionary claims rest on weak evidence, the anatomical split between ventral and dorsal vagal pathways is not as clean as the theory needs, and respiratory sinus arrhythmia does not hold up as a reliable proxy for vagal tone across physiological conditions. Defenders, including a rebuttal in Psychology Today, note that the critique targets the neurophysiological and evolutionary foundation rather than the clinical practice built on top of it. The central working insight, that autonomic state shapes perception, emotion, and relational capacity, was not itself tested or disproven.

Try this: Rewrite your one-paragraph description of the polyvagal frame this week so it claims the clinical observation and not the evolutionary mechanism.
Source: InSight+ (MJA)

No. 03 · First Responder MH · 2026-09-07

Study: Toxic dust, PTSD may accelerate brain aging in 9/11 first responders

The cost does not stay in the mind. Responders with PTSD showed brains structurally older than their birthdays, and the ones without it did not. Sit with a retired engine man who cannot hold a name and you are looking at this. It also sharpens the referral line: cognitive complaints in an older responder with a trauma history belong with a physician, not with a coach.

Researchers studying World Trade Center responders used MRI and an AI brain-age model trained on more than 11,500 scans from healthy people aged 5 to 95. Responders with PTSD had brains that appeared roughly three years older than their chronological age. Responders without PTSD showed brain ages that matched their actual age. Prolonged Ground Zero exposure and PTSD both tracked with the acceleration. The team notes that about 23 percent of WTC responders carry a PTSD diagnosis, against a lifetime rate near 6 percent in the general population.

Try this: If a client over fifty reports memory or word-finding trouble alongside a trauma history, route to medical workup before doing any more process work.

Pilot Study Indicates Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy Safe, Promising for Veterans With PTSD

Twelve people, no control arm, open label. The safety signal is the real finding and the response numbers are a hypothesis, not a standard of care. This is the population closest to yours, and it is also the population most likely to arrive at your door having read only the headline. High-acuity treatment-resistant PTSD with suicidality screening sits outside coaching scope entirely. Your work is before and after the room, not in it.

An open-label pilot trial published in Communications Medicine in July tested psilocybin-assisted therapy in twelve US military veterans with severe treatment-resistant PTSD. Participants received about eight hours of preparatory therapy, two dosing sessions at 15 mg and 25 mg spaced two to three weeks apart, and roughly eight hours of integration therapy afterward. No serious adverse events occurred and there was no increase in suicidal ideation or behavior; side effects were mostly mild headache and brief anxiety. One month out, nine of twelve met the study criteria for both response and remission. Lead author Stacey Armstrong of Ohio State called the results striking for the population, while the design allows no causal claim.

Source: U.S. Medicine

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in Healthcare

This sits squarely in the structural-critique camp, Litz and Shay territory rather than the departmental wellness lane. It says plainly that breathwork and better sleep do not touch a wound whose cause is the incentive structure a man works inside. Worth holding against the applied resilience material you also use. The tension between those two lineages is live, and it is the content, not a problem to smooth over.

Wendy Patrick reviews a 2026 bibliometric study by Guixiang Yao and colleagues covering 1,180 publications on moral distress and moral injury among healthcare workers across nearly twenty years. Clinical medicine, nursing, psychology, and ethics form the disciplinary base. Moral distress dominated the literature, with moral injury gaining ground lately, particularly in high-intensity care settings. The article argues that meditation, mindfulness, resilience skills, exercise, and diet help but do not reach the root when the root is a for-profit system's incentives. It names moving out of avoidance and speaking openly about the experience as the first real step.

Try this: When a client names moral injury, ask what the system asked of them before you ask what they felt. Order matters.
Source: Psychology Today

SAMHSA Awards $600,000 Grant to Fund Rural EMS Training Program

Federal money is still flowing to rural EMS workforce and overdose response, and it flows through SAMHSA's Rural EMS Training line. Worth tracking as a funding channel for responder-facing work that is not strictly clinical, and as a marker of where the substance use conversation is heading in departments.

The RI Disaster Medical Assistance Team received a $600,000 federal award from SAMHSA to run a Rural Emergency Medical Services Training Program. The two-year grant runs September 2026 through September 2028. Starting September 30, program staff work directly with EMS agencies and fire departments across Rhode Island's eighteen rural communities. The three program areas are workforce capacity, overdose response, and connecting patients to treatment. Agencies can participate in one, two, or all three at no cost to the agency or its people.

Attorney General Coleman launches peer support program for law enforcement

Kentucky Attorney General Russell Coleman opened a first-of-its-kind Law Enforcement Peer Support grant program in May 2026, offering agencies up to $5,000 to build or strengthen peer support teams. Allowed uses included overtime for peer support specialists, training and education, and team equipment. In September the office unveiled the broader Stand by the Badge program, with eight agencies named as first recipients. The office also launched a secure website for officers, deputies, and troopers, plus a toolkit of best practices so any agency in the state can stand up its own team.

A state that publishes a peer support toolkit has just set the floor every department will be measured against.

A state attorney general standing up peer support with grant money and a published toolkit is the structural version of this work, not the inspirational version. Watch the toolkit. When a state publishes best practices for peer teams, that language tends to become the floor departments get measured against, including in Arizona.

Conn. launches first state-designated mobile integrated health program

State designation is the part that matters. It means mobile integrated health stops being a pilot a chief likes and becomes a category with standards, billing, and a defined scope. That is the same road the behavioral crisis role is on, and how other states write it will shape how Arizona writes it.

Connecticut named the first state-designated mobile integrated health program, moving community paramedicine and in-home care from local pilot status into a formal state category. Designation creates a defined program type rather than a department-by-department arrangement. EMS1 frames it as a template other states are likely to follow. Details on scope, staffing, and reimbursement sit with the state EMS office rather than in the coverage itself.

Try this: Pull the Connecticut designation criteria and compare them against how the Phoenix behavioral crisis role is currently scoped on paper.

The day before the call we'll never forget: A first responder readiness checklist

Plain shift discipline, written by somebody who still checks the bag. The frame is the useful part: the work of the worst call gets done the day before it comes. That is the same argument you make about nervous system capacity, only in gear and sleep instead of language. Nothing here is novel and that is the point.

The piece walks a single shift's readiness routine. The rig and gear check runs off an electronic checklist covering lights, mirrors, fuel, first-in bag, oxygen, monitor, AED, and gloves. Personal gear follows: food, sunglasses, hat, BDU pockets, emesis bag, reading glasses. The author holds to Gordon Graham's line that every day is a training day and reviews at least one protocol plus the related equipment and medications each shift. The day ends with the phone docked and a book, lights out by 9:30, knowing tomorrow is covered. The 9/11 anniversary is the frame, not the subject.

Try this: Steal the structure for your own pre-shift ritual and name it out loud to one client this week as a capacity practice rather than a gear practice.

No. 10 · Polyvagal · 2026-08-31

We have been convinced that traumatic experiences are life sentences. Why?

A hard word for an industry Mark works inside, this magazine included. If two thirds of people recover on their own, then the story that a man needs years of purchased help to be whole again is doing damage on its own account. The Fathers would say the same thing in fewer words: do not make a man's wound his name. Sit with whether your own language leaves room for a client to simply get well.

The author argues that popular culture, social media, and a growing trauma-treatment industry have taught people to read potentially traumatic events as permanent sentences. Decades of resilience research say otherwise: regardless of the length or intensity of the event, roughly two thirds of people regain their footing without extensive intervention. Vocabulary like triggered, fight and flight, and trauma-informed has spread far past its evidence. The piece is not a denial that some people need serious treatment. It is a claim that the default story ignores the majority who recover, and that naming recovery as the normal case frees people rather than abandoning them.

Source: Psychology Today
