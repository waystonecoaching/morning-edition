FDA Staff Present a Framework for Psychedelic Drugs in NEJM

This is the closest thing yet to the agency saying out loud how it intends to judge these drugs. Four FDA staffers, not advocates, writing in the country's most-cited medical journal. For anyone whose clients may one day sit in a legal psilocybin or MDMA room, the rules of that room start here.

In a New England Journal of Medicine commentary, four FDA staff outline what they call a new framework for evaluating psychedelic drugs. They open by naming the scale of the depression and PTSD crisis, then set out how the agency plans to weigh trial evidence, functional unblinding, and the therapy that comes wrapped around the dose. It reads as the agency signaling its own standards ahead of pending applications.

Polyvagal Theory Debunked in Comprehensive New Study

Read this one slow. A medical outlet reports a study claiming the anatomical claims under polyvagal theory do not hold. You use this framework with clients and on the Waystone site. The move is not to defend the map or burn it, but to separate what is clinically useful in how you meet a dysregulated nervous system from what may be shaky as literal neuroanatomy.

MJA InSight+ reports on a new review arguing that core anatomical and evolutionary claims of polyvagal theory are not supported by the current physiological evidence. The critique targets the theory's account of the vagus nerve and its proposed autonomic hierarchy, not the value of somatic or co-regulation practice as such. It lands in a field where the theory has become near-doctrine in trauma work.

Try this: Look at where your client language leans on polyvagal as fact versus as metaphor. The felt-safety and co-regulation work stands on its own feet. Say it that way.

Pilot Study: Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy Safe, Promising for Veterans With PTSD

Veterans and first responders share enough of the injury that trial signal in one population is worth watching for the other. Keep the reading honest: pilot means small and early, promising is not proven, and open-label designs flatter every drug. This is a green light to keep watching, not to draw conclusions.

U.S. Medicine reports on a pilot study finding psilocybin-assisted therapy safe and showing early promise for veterans with treatment-resistant PTSD. The sample is small and the design preliminary, so the result speaks to feasibility and safety signal more than efficacy. It adds to a slow-building case rather than settling anything.

Moving Through Moral Injury

This one sits in the structural camp, closer to Shay and Litz than to a wellness app. Moral injury is not a symptom cluster to be soothed. It is what happens when a person does, or witnesses, or is failed by leadership into, something that breaks their moral floor. The healing it names is meaning and repair, not regulation.

A Santa Clara University piece frames moral injury as a wound of conscience distinct from PTSD, rooted in betrayal and transgression rather than fear. It follows people working back toward wholeness through acknowledgment, community, and moral repair rather than symptom management alone. The through-line is that recovery is relational and ethical, not only clinical.

Source: Santa Clara University

New Zealand Regulator Approves MDMA for Severe PTSD

Australia opened this door in 2023. New Zealand is now the second country to let named prescribers use MDMA for PTSD. Watch the guardrails more than the headline: who can prescribe, under what oversight, with what therapy attached. Those details, not the approval itself, tell you what real-world access looks like when the U.S. gets there.

New Zealand's medicines regulator has authorized two psychiatrists to prescribe MDMA for severe, treatment-resistant PTSD under restricted, closely supervised conditions. It makes New Zealand the second country after Australia to permit clinical MDMA use outside a trial. Access remains narrow and physician-gated rather than broadly available.

Source: The Guardian

How Motivational Interviewing Helps With Common Mental Health Conditions

A GPonline viewpoint makes the case for motivational interviewing as a practical, evidence-based way for practitioners to support people with common mental health conditions. It centers the core stance of rolling with resistance, evoking the person's own motivation, and holding autonomy rather than directing. The argument is that brief, spirit-first MI improves engagement without adding clinical burden.

MI shares a spine with non-directive inquiry: you do not push, you draw out the person's own reasons to move. This is a clinician-facing viewpoint, but the mechanics travel straight into coaching. Change talk is elicited, not installed.

Kentucky AG Launches Peer Support Program for Law Enforcement

Another state stands up peer support at scale. The pattern is worth tracking because it tells you where the field's money and legitimacy are moving: toward responder-to-responder contact as first-line, ahead of clinical referral. That is the same ground your practice stands on.

Kentucky's Attorney General has announced a statewide peer support program for law enforcement, framing responder-to-responder contact as a first line of mental health support. The initiative pairs trained peers with officers and points toward clinical care when needed. It reflects the broader institutional shift to fund peer models rather than rely on referral alone.

The Day Before the Call: A First Responder Readiness Checklist

Most readiness talk aims at the gear and the tactics. This one aims at the person before the worst call finds them. Prep as prevention, not debrief as cleanup. It is the kind of concrete, do-it-now piece a coaching practice can actually hand a client.

An EMS1 piece offers a practical checklist for building personal and psychological readiness before a critical incident, rather than only processing it afterward. It covers sleep, relationships, meaning, and rehearsed responses as protective factors laid down in advance. The frame is that resilience is stocked ahead of time, not summoned in the moment.

We Were Told Trauma Is a Life Sentence. Why?

A fitting close for a day that put two frameworks under the knife. The piece pushes back on the story that damage is permanent, that a person is fixed by the worst thing that happened to them. That is not sentiment. It is the whole wager of the work: that a person can change, that the sentence can be commuted.

A Psychology Today essay questions the cultural conviction that traumatic experience condemns a person for life, tracing where that fatalism comes from and what it costs. It argues that framing trauma as a permanent sentence can itself become an obstacle to recovery. The counter-case leans on neuroplasticity and the ordinary evidence of people who heal.

Source: Psychology Today
