Davis, Farchione and Colleagues Present FDA’s Psychedelic Drug Framework in NEJM

Four FDA staffers used the New England Journal of Medicine to lay out how the agency now thinks about approving psychedelic drugs. When the regulator writes its own framework in the field's most-cited journal, that is the tide turning, not another advocacy press release. This is the document that will shape whether MDMA and psilocybin ever reach a first responder with PTSD through a real prescription.

FDA officials, including Tiffany Farchione, published a NEJM commentary describing what they call a new framework for psychedelic drugs. They open by naming the scale of the depression and PTSD crisis, then set out how the agency plans to weigh trial design, functional unblinding, and real-world delivery for a class of drugs that does not fit the usual model. The piece signals FDA engagement rather than retreat after the 2024 MDMA rejection, but it stops well short of a green light. Read it as the regulator setting terms, not opening a door.

Propagation, not aggregation: linking individual psychological flexibility to organizational functioning

This is the structural half of the argument, and it is the half departments keep dodging. You can train an individual firefighter into psychological flexibility and still return him to an organization that breaks him. The paper names the gap between fixing the person and fixing the system, which is the same tension moral-injury work keeps surfacing.

Writing in Frontiers in Psychology, the authors note that individual psychological flexibility reliably tracks with worker health, yet interventions that build it in individuals do not reliably change the organizations those workers belong to. They argue the missing mechanism is propagation, how flexible individuals spread new patterns through a system, rather than simple aggregation of fixed individuals. The frame moves the conversation from personal resilience to organizational function. For a solo coach, it is a reminder that the client's department is part of the case.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Generative AI and Moral Injury - Psychology Today

A sharp piece naming a new vector for an old wound: what happens to conscience when a machine makes or shapes the call. Moral injury has always been about acting against what you know to be right, or being made to. Automation adds a fresh way to be complicit without consent, which is worth Mark's attention as the tooling creeps into every field.

Writing in Psychology Today, the author extends moral injury into the age of generative AI, asking what happens to human conscience when automated systems make, shape, or obscure morally weighted decisions. The argument is that moral injury arises not only from what a person does but from being placed in systems that force or launder harm, and AI widens that exposure. It is a conceptual piece, not a study, and it leans on the structural lineage of moral-injury thinking rather than the operational one. A useful provocation for anyone whose work is being quietly reorganized around a machine.

NREMT launches study to track long-term health effects of fire, EMS careers - ems1.com

NREMT is starting a long-horizon study on what a fire and EMS career actually does to a body and mind over decades. This is the unglamorous, load-bearing work: follow the same people long enough to see the real cost. If it is built well, it becomes the citation Mark points to when a client says the job is fine.

The National Registry of Emergency Medical Technicians launched a study to track the long-term health effects of fire and EMS careers over time. Longitudinal design is the point: rather than a snapshot, it follows responders across years to capture cumulative physical and psychological toll. Career-length data is exactly what the field lacks, since most studies catch people once and let them go. Worth watching for enrollment scope and what outcomes they choose to measure.

Try this: Note this one for your evidence file. When a client insists the job leaves no mark, career-length data beats anecdote.

The piece sits in the structural lineage: the injury comes less from personal failing than from being made to practice against one's values. It offers reflection more than protocol, which is fitting for the wound it names. For a coach who works the same terrain, it is a mirror worth holding.
