Davis, Farchione and Colleagues Present FDA’s Psychedelic Drug Framework in NEJM

The FDA's own reviewers, writing in the New England Journal of Medicine, laid out how the agency now thinks about approving psychedelic drugs. That is the regulatory ground under everything you and BTTY work near. When the people who sign the approvals publish their reasoning in the field's most-cited journal, you read it before the advocacy press tells you what to think about it.

Four FDA staffers, including Davis and Farchione, published a commentary describing what they call the agency's new framework for evaluating psychedelic drugs. They set out how trials should be designed and read given the blinding problem and the role of accompanying therapy. It is the clearest signal yet of how the agency intends to weigh this class. The tone is cautious, not promotional.

Try this: Read the primary commentary yourself before quoting any secondary coverage of it. Note the design concerns they name; those are the questions to bring to any responder-PTSD trial you evaluate.

Treat vendor topline data as a starting point, not a finding, until the full paper lands.

A treatment provider is arguing that awareness campaigns have hit their ceiling and the work now is concrete intervention. The claim lines up with what the field has said for years: knowing about a problem is not treating it. The source has a commercial stake in that conclusion. The underlying point about intervention over awareness still stands on its own.

NREMT launches study to track long-term health effects of fire, EMS careers

NREMT is starting a long-term study tracking what a fire and EMS career does to the people who work it, over years, not one shift. This is the population you serve, and longitudinal data on career-length harm is exactly what has been missing. If it holds, it changes what you can say with evidence rather than anecdote.

The National Registry of Emergency Medical Technicians launched a study to follow the long-term physical and mental health effects of fire and EMS careers. Longitudinal tracking of responders across a career is rare and hard to fund, which is why most claims about cumulative harm rest on cross-sectional snapshots. A registry-backed cohort could give the field durable numbers. Early days, but worth watching.

Try this: Note the study for later citation. Cross-sectional stress claims are weak ground; a cohort like this is the kind of source to lean on once it reports.

Examining veteran preferences and attitudes across intervention modalities for posttraumatic stress disorder

A peer-reviewed look at what veterans actually want across the different PTSD treatments, not what works in a lab. Client preference is where a lot of care quietly succeeds or fails, and it is the part a coach sits closest to. Veterans are not first responders, but the intake question is the same.

A Journal of Traumatic Stress study examined veteran preferences and attitudes across several PTSD intervention types. Preference and buy-in shape whether someone starts and stays with a treatment, independent of the treatment's efficacy. The findings speak to matching people to modalities they will actually accept. Useful for anyone who helps a client choose a path.

Try this: When a client is weighing PTSD treatments, ask what they are drawn to and what they refuse before you talk efficacy. Fit predicts follow-through.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

MDMA-enhanced couples therapy shows promise for treating PTSD and relationship distress

MDMA-assisted work extended to couples, aimed at both the PTSD and the relationship damage it drives. This is where the psychedelic literature meets the relational frame you already work in: trauma lives between people, not only inside one nervous system. Early and small, but the direction is worth noting.

A study reported that MDMA-enhanced couples therapy showed promise for reducing both PTSD symptoms and relationship distress. The design treats the partner and the bond as part of the healing, not a bystander to it. Sample sizes in this space are small and the blinding problem persists. Read it as a signal of direction, not settled practice.

Try this: Hold this next to the Getting Real frame: the relational field is a site of repair, not just a casualty of trauma. Watch for a full write-up before citing it.

Generative AI and Moral Injury

The piece applies the concept of moral injury to situations involving generative AI, where people are pressed to act against their own moral judgment by systems and institutions. It leans on the structural understanding of moral injury as a wound of conscience and betrayal rather than a performance deficit. The AI angle is new; the underlying frame is not. It is a reminder that moral injury is about being placed in an impossible position, not about weakness.

Moral injury is a wound of conscience, not a failure of nerve.

Psychology Today running moral injury against generative AI is the structural-critique lineage doing what it does: naming the harm that comes from being made to act against conscience by a system, not a battlefield. Whether the AI framing holds or not, the underlying model is Litz and Shay's, and it is the one that fits betrayal and institutional failure better than the performance-psych frame does.

Try this: Keep the structural frame handy for clients whose injury traces to a system or a command, not their own failure. That distinction changes what repair even means.

Pα+ Library: Psychedelic REMS

As approvals get closer, the fight moves from does it work to how it gets delivered, and REMS is where that gets decided. This is the unglamorous machinery that will shape who can actually access psychedelic therapy and under what supervision. If you or BTTY ever sit near delivery, this is the terrain.

Psychedelic Alpha's library piece examines what a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy could look like for approved psychedelic therapies. REMS programs govern how a drug is dispensed, monitored, and restricted after approval. For therapies that require supervised sessions, the delivery rules may matter as much as the approval itself. Much remains undecided.

Try this: Track REMS as the real access question. Efficacy debates get the headlines; delivery rules decide who gets care.

Nolan’s The Odyssey is an emotive meditation on modern moral injury

A scholar reading Nolan's Odyssey as a meditation on modern moral injury. Odysseus does not come home whole; he comes home changed, carrying what the war put in him, and the old poem knew that before we had the clinical word. Sometimes the truest account of the wound is not in a journal but in the story we have been telling for three thousand years.

A Durham University piece reads Christopher Nolan's adaptation of the Odyssey as an exploration of moral injury, the lasting wound of having done or witnessed what violates conscience. The return home, in this reading, is not resolution but a reckoning with what cannot be undone. The ancient text carries the same knowledge the moral injury literature now formalizes. Art held the wound before medicine named it.
