FDA lays out its framework for psychedelic drugs in the NEJM

This is the regulatory ground shifting under the whole field, written by FDA staffers themselves rather than by advocates. When the agency states its own terms in a journal like the NEJM, it sets the shape of every trial and approval that follows. For anyone walking clients toward integration work, the standard of care starts here, not in the press releases below.

Four FDA staff, including Davis and Farchione, published a commentary in the New England Journal of Medicine describing what the agency calls a new framework for psychedelic drugs. The piece lays out the questions FDA weighs when reviewing these therapies, from trial design to real-world delivery. It reads as the agency signaling how it intends to think, not as a promise of approval.

Try this: Read the framing FDA uses for functional unblinding and expectancy. Those two terms will decide which trials survive review.

Orange County firefighter says a peer support hotline saved his life

One man, one call, still alive. This is the whole case for peer support at field level, told without a study behind it. The scope edge is plain, this is a suicide-adjacent story, so hold it gently. But it names the thing peer programs are built to do and sometimes actually do.

A firefighter in Orange County credits a peer support hotline with keeping him alive through a crisis. The report is a single first-person account rather than program data, and it points to the value of a responder being able to reach another responder who has been in the same dark. It is the human-scale version of the peer support argument.

Two New Zealand psychiatrists cleared to prescribe MDMA for PTSD

While the United States argues about frameworks, New Zealand has moved two clinicians into actual prescribing authority. It is worth watching because a small country going first becomes the early real-world evidence everyone else reads. This is policy news, not trial data, so the question it raises is delivery and safeguards, not efficacy.

New Zealand has approved two psychiatrists to prescribe MDMA for post-traumatic stress disorder under a controlled pathway. The move makes it one of the few places where the therapy exists outside a trial. What matters now is how prescribing, screening, and after-care get structured, since that is where the field tends to stumble.

Veterans have clear preferences across PTSD treatment options

Peer-reviewed and quiet, which is why it matters. The standard of care fails when the person will not walk through the door of the treatment on offer. This study takes seriously that a veteran's stated preference is not noise to be managed but data that predicts whether care lands at all.

Researchers in the Journal of Traumatic Stress examined how veterans rank and respond to different intervention modalities for PTSD. The work treats patient preference as a real variable in whether treatment is taken up and completed. The practical read is that matching the approach to the person may matter as much as the evidence base behind any single method.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

This is the closest thing today to primary research pointing the right way, an actual study rather than a stock filing. As with all of this space right now, the sample size and blinding are the questions to hold before the headline.

Wearable networks map how two nervous systems fall into sync

Strip the engineering language and this is co-regulation measured on the wire. Interpersonal autonomic physiology is the substrate under everything you name when you talk about felt safety between two people. The study is built for athletes and sensors, but the finding, that autonomic states track and align across bodies, is the polyvagal claim in hard numbers.

Frontiers researchers describe a sensor framework for tracking physiological synchrony, the moment-to-moment alignment of autonomic nervous system activity between people. The paper sits in the interpersonal autonomic physiology literature and treats co-regulation as something measurable across bodies. The application is athletic performance, but the underlying mechanism is the one that carries any work built on shared nervous-system safety.

Scars you can't see: a Pentagon 9/11 responder names the moral injury he carried

This is the wound the awareness posters cannot reach, carried quietly through nine more years of service. It sits in the moral injury lineage, not the resilience-highlight-reel one, and the difference is the whole point. A man does his duty in the wreckage and then lives with what the duty cost him, which is a different thing from PTSD and needs a different answer.

Former Air Force senior enlisted adviser Roger Woody recounts his role in the Pentagon recovery after the 2001 attack and says he carried moral injury and PTSD symptoms while serving nine more years. He frames the telling itself as part of the reckoning. The piece names moral injury as its own wound rather than folding it into a general trauma story.

Becoming a fighter: what beginners are really learning in the gym

A fitting place to end, because it is about formation, not fitness. To step into controlled contact with violence and be changed by it is old desert territory dressed in gym clothes. The study reads the beginner's transformation as a bodily and relational passage, which is the same thing the fathers meant by askesis: you become someone through what you submit yourself to.

A phenomenological study in Frontiers examines what beginners undergo when they take up martial arts and combat sports. The authors read the practice as a bodily and relational passage close to violence but ordered by rules and reciprocity, and they trace how it forms the practitioner rather than only training the body. It is a study of becoming, which is why it closes today's edition.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
