FDA Staff Lay Out a Psychedelic Drug Framework in the NEJM

This is the closest thing yet to the agency showing its hand on how psychedelic medicines might clear review. It matters to the integration lane you were trained for, because the rules of the road decide what reaches the people you sit with. Read it as agency staffers making a case, not as settled law. A framework in a commentary is a signal of intent, not a finished door.

Four FDA staffers, writing in the New England Journal of Medicine, describe what they call a new framework for evaluating psychedelic drugs. They open on the scale of the depression and PTSD crisis, then set out how the agency is thinking about the design problems these compounds pose, from functional unblinding to the role of the therapy wrapped around the dose. The piece reads as the regulator naming the hard questions out loud. It stops short of promising any particular path to approval.

Try this: If a client asks where MDMA or psilocybin stands with the FDA, point them to the actual questions the agency is wrestling with rather than the advocacy headlines.

Why Moral Injury Could Be the Signature Wound of the AI Battlefield

This sits in the structural camp, Litz and Shay's lineage, not the performance-psych one. The claim is that when a machine puts distance between a person and the act, the wound does not lessen. It moves. Worth holding next to what you see in responders who never fired a shot but carry the same freight from a call they could not change.

The piece argues that as autonomous and AI-mediated systems take over more of the decision loop in combat, the moral weight does not disappear. It relocates to the operators and analysts who authorize, watch, or live with the results. Drawing on the moral-injury research tradition, the writer treats the injury as a rupture in what a person believed was right, not a stress reaction to be managed away. The betrayal-and-distance angle is the load-bearing part.

MDMA-Enhanced Couples Therapy Shows Promise for PTSD and Relationship Distress

The interesting turn here is relational. PTSD does not only live in the body of the one who carries it. It lives in the space between two people. Pairing the compound with couples work, not solo dosing, lines up with what you already know from Getting Real: contact is where the repair happens. Small study, secondary reporting, so hold the promise loosely.

Researchers tested MDMA paired with a structured couples therapy for pairs where one partner carries PTSD. The report says both the symptom load and the relationship distress moved in the right direction. The design leans on the drug to open enough felt safety for the couple to do the relational work they otherwise stall on. As with most of this space, the sample is small and the coverage is secondhand.

Try this: Notice the frame shift for your own practice: some responder trauma is best worked in the presence of the spouse, not around them.

Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy May Help Veterans With Severe PTSD

This is the population you serve, in the severe end of the range. The study points the same direction as the rest of the psilocybin PTSD literature, which is encouraging and which is also the reason to stay skeptical: expectancy runs hot in trials where everyone knows what they took. Read the effect size before you read the headline.

A study reported here found that psilocybin-assisted therapy produced meaningful reductions in symptoms for veterans with severe PTSD, a group that often does not respond to standard treatment. The framing is that the compound plus the therapeutic container reached people the usual protocols leave behind. The coverage is a secondary account, so the trial's blinding, dropout, and durability are the things to check against the primary paper.

No. 07 · Coaching Practice · 2026-08-24

How Motivational Interviewing Helps With Common Mental Health Conditions

The article makes the case that motivational interviewing, an evidence-based, collaborative way of talking through ambivalence, helps with the anxiety and depression that walk into a general practice. It walks through the core moves: rolling with resistance rather than arguing it down, and drawing out the person's own change talk. The pitch is that these are learnable in the length of a normal appointment. It is a practitioner viewpoint, not new trial data.

You do not argue a man into changing. You get quiet enough that he can hear himself decide.

Familiar ground, worth a fresh look. MI is the clinical cousin of the non-directive stance you already work from: you do not push the person toward change, you help them hear their own reasons for it. The piece is aimed at GPs, but the mechanics port straight into a coaching hour.

Try this: Pick one MI move this week, reflective listening that ends in the client's own words for why they want the change, and use it deliberately in a session.

Restorative Music as a Workday Strategy for Clinician Wellbeing

Not psychedelic, not peer support, just a small honest look at whether a few minutes of the right sound can drop a caregiver's arousal mid-shift. This is nervous-system regulation by the plainest possible means. It belongs next to your polyvagal work because it is testing the same lever: can you shift state without leaving the floor.

This preliminary study looked at whether a brief restorative music intervention could ease stress for healthcare workers during the workday, a group in which more than half report burnout. Prior work has tied music-based interventions to lower stress and more relaxation, and this study tested a short, accessible version that fits inside a shift. The design is small and early. The value is in the direction: a low-cost, in-the-moment down-regulation tool, not a cure for the conditions that burn people out.

Try this: Try a two-minute restorative sound break as a between-client reset and notice what your own state does before you recommend it to anyone.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in Healthcare

The healthcare version of the wound your responders carry: told to do right by people while the system makes doing right impossible, then handed the guilt for the gap. The piece keeps the injury where it belongs, in the space between the person and the institution that failed them, and does not reduce it to a self-care problem.

The article names moral injury in clinicians as a distinct harm from burnout, one born of being forced to act against one's own sense of right by conditions outside one's control. It distinguishes the personal work of recovery from the structural conditions that keep producing the injury. The recovery it describes leans on honest naming, connection, and restoring a sense of agency. It is a practitioner-facing essay rather than a study.

Source: Psychology Today

Moving Through Moral Injury

We close on the word that ran through this whole issue. Moving through, not moving past. The injury does not get argued away or dosed away. It gets carried, named, and slowly reintegrated in the company of others. That is the same road the Fathers knew: you do not outrun the thing, you sit with it until it stops running you.

This reflective piece follows the work of moving through moral injury as a lived process rather than a diagnosis to be cleared. It treats the injury as a rupture in meaning and belonging, and locates healing in restoring both, patiently and in relationship. The tone is human-scale and unhurried. It offers presence and integration rather than technique.

Source: Santa Clara University
