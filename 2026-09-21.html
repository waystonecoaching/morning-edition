FDA Staff Lay Out a New Framework for Psychedelic Drugs in NEJM

When the regulator writes its own map in the New England Journal, the ground under everyone doing integration work moves a little. This is the agency describing how it intends to judge these drugs, not an advocate arguing they should pass. Read it as the frame the whole field will be measured against, including any lane that touches Being True to You work.

Four FDA staffers, including Davis and Farchione, published a commentary describing what the agency calls its new framework for evaluating psychedelic drugs. They open with the scale of the mental health crisis, centering depression, and then set out how the agency thinks about trial design, blinding, and safety for this class. It is a regulatory position piece, not new trial data. The signal is procedural: it tells sponsors what the bar looks like.

Try this: If you cite psychedelic progress to a client, cite the framework, not the hype. It is the sober version of where this stands.

Reuters: Federal Move to Ease Restrictions on MDMA for PTSD

Reuters reports a federal push to loosen the reins on MDMA as a PTSD treatment. Policy, not science, is the mover here, and policy moving ahead of a settled evidence base is exactly the tension the field is under. Watch what standard of care follows, because that is the part that reaches a first responder in a chair.

Reuters reports that the administration is preparing to ease restrictions on MDMA used to treat PTSD. The piece frames it as a policy shift rather than a new clinical result. It matters because access and scheduling changes shape what treatments a veteran or responder can actually reach, and how fast. Keep the credibility question open: easing rules does not close the questions researchers still have about the trials.

Orange County Firefighter Credits a Peer Support Hotline With Saving His Life

A hotline is only a phone number until the night a man decides to dial it instead of doing the other thing. This is the peer support model working the way it is supposed to, one call, one responder who picked up. Scope note: this sits on the suicidality edge, so hold it as testimony to a system, not a clinical claim.

An Orange County firefighter tells the story of a peer support hotline he says kept him alive during a crisis. The piece is first-person testimony rather than program data, and it credits the fact that the person on the other end was a peer who had been through it. It is a small, concrete picture of why responder-staffed lines exist. Treat it as one man's account, not an outcomes study.

Try this: Know which line you would tell a client to call, by name and number, before they need it.

Moral Injury as a Signature Wound of the AI Battlefield

Moral injury is what happens when a person does, or fails to stop, something that cuts against their own core. Task & Purpose asks what that wound looks like when a screen and an algorithm stand between the actor and the act. This is the structural-critique lineage, Litz and Shay territory: the injury is not a malfunction in the person, it is the cost of the situation they were put in.

The piece argues that autonomous and AI-mediated warfare may produce moral injury as its characteristic psychological wound, distinct from classic combat trauma. When responsibility is diffused across machines and distance, the operator can still carry the moral weight without the usual markers of danger or exertion. The article treats this as a coming problem for how militaries understand harm, not a solved one. It names the gap between guilt and the systems that create it.

Source: Task & Purpose

Veteran Preferences Across PTSD Intervention Modalities

A peer-reviewed look at what veterans actually want when offered different roads through PTSD treatment, not what clinicians assume they want. For anyone coaching responders, the preference gap is the whole game: the best modality is the one a person will walk into and stay in. This is the quiet, useful kind of research the louder headlines drown out.

This Journal of Traumatic Stress study examines how veterans rate and choose among different PTSD intervention modalities. It looks at preferences and attitudes rather than head-to-head efficacy, surfacing what draws people toward or away from a given approach. The practical value is in matching a person to a path they will complete. It is early-view research, so read it as signal about fit and engagement, not a ranking of treatments.

Try this: Ask a client which approaches they would refuse before you suggest one. Refusal tells you more than preference.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Laredo Agencies Confront Responder Mental Health as Suicides Rise

The piece reports that local agencies in Laredo are confronting responder mental health after the count of suicides in the community rose. It frames the response as agencies being pushed past awareness toward doing something concrete. The story is local reporting rather than research, but the pattern is the field-wide one: recognition outpacing intervention. It sits squarely on the suicidality edge and should be held with that weight.

PsyPost reports on research suggesting MDMA-assisted couples therapy may help both PTSD symptoms and the relationship strain that comes with them. The approach treats the couple as the unit, using the medicine to support connection and communication alongside symptom work. Results are described as early and promising rather than settled. The relational frame is the notable part, and it lines up with communication and connection work more than with a pill-and-symptom model.

Trauma rarely stays in one body. It leaks into the marriage, the partnership, the people who love the person carrying it. This study points at treating the pair, not just the patient, which is closer to how a responder actually lives. When Doing Good Hurts: Moral Injury in the Nonprofit Sector

Moral injury is not only a battlefield word. It shows up wherever people give themselves to a mission and the institution fails the people it claimed to serve. This piece carries it into helping work, which is a mirror for anyone in this trade. The betrayal-by-the-system thread is the one to follow.

The article extends moral injury beyond the military into nonprofit and mission-driven work, where people burn out not from doing too much but from being made complicit in falling short. It points at organizational and structural failure as the source of the wound, rather than individual weakness. The frame is that caring work can injure the caregiver when the institution betrays the mission. It is commentary, drawing on the moral injury literature rather than presenting new data.

Motivational Interviewing for Common Mental Health Conditions

Motivational interviewing is the closest thing the clinical world has to what a good coach already does: stop pushing, and let the person talk themselves toward change. This is a practitioner-facing piece on using it for everyday mental health work. Worth it as a refresher on the discipline of not steering.

The GPonline viewpoint argues motivational interviewing can help clinicians support patients with common mental health conditions like anxiety and low mood. It emphasizes the core stance: eliciting the person's own reasons for change rather than prescribing them. The piece is aimed at general practitioners but the technique carries directly into coaching. It reads as a practical case for a light, non-directive hand.

Try this: Pick one session this week and count how often you offer versus ask. Aim to ask more.
Source: GPonline

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in the Ones Who Heal

End on the ones who carry other people for a living and quietly come apart. The wound here is not weakness, it is the residue of caring inside a system that grinds. A fitting place to close: the healer needs tending too, and pretending otherwise is how good people are lost.

The Psychology Today piece addresses moral injury among healthcare workers, the harm that comes from being unable to provide the care one knows is right. It distinguishes this from ordinary burnout, locating the source in conditions and constraints rather than personal failing. It offers ways to recognize and tend the wound in oneself. As a close, it turns the field's attention back on the caregiver.

Source: Psychology Today
