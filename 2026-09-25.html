No. 01 · Psychedelic Therapy · 2026-09-18

Trump Signs Order Fast-Tracking Ibogaine and Psychedelic Drug Review for PTSD

This is the biggest federal psychedelic policy move of the year, and it lands squarely on the population you serve. Veterans are the test case, thirty of them, who traveled to Mexico for ibogaine treatment the US wouldn't authorize. That detail, people voting with their bodies ahead of the regulatory system, is worth sitting with longer than the headline. Read the safety data before the press release.

The order directs FDA to issue national priority review vouchers for three psychedelic compounds, cutting review timelines from months to weeks, and tells HHS to put at least fifty million dollars toward state-level psychedelic treatment programs. Ibogaine, derived from a West African shrub and still Schedule I, is the headline drug. The order leans on a small Stanford study of thirty veterans treated in Mexico who showed improvement in traumatic brain injury, PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms. That study had no placebo control and no US clinical infrastructure behind it. Ibogaine also carries documented cardiotoxicity, linked in the medical literature to more than thirty deaths; a Johns Hopkins researcher noted that risk profile is exactly why US research stalled for decades.

Try this: If a client asks about ibogaine, the honest answer right now is: promising signal, real cardiac risk, no US access pathway yet, and insurance won't touch it. Say that plainly before anything else.

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in Healthcare

Swap healthcare worker for first responder and this piece describes your caseload almost word for word. Moral injury from fast, high-stakes decisions under institutional constraint is the same wound whether it happens in an ICU or on a rig. Patrick's list of protective factors maps directly onto conversations you're already having.

Wendy Patrick, writing for Psychology Today, distinguishes moral injury from ordinary burnout: it comes from the felt inability to act in line with your own values, not simply from working too hard. In healthcare that shows up in emergency departments, ICUs, oncology, and end-of-life care, where decisions get made fast and institutional constraints don't always leave room for what a clinician believes is right. She points to COVID-era triage as the sharpest recent example. Her prescription is organizational as much as individual: stronger ethics support, better team communication, and structures that distribute the moral weight of hard calls instead of leaving it on one person.

Try this: Her list of protective factors, ethical reflection, shared decision-making, institutional support, works as a ready-made intake framework for a first client conversation on moral injury.

Why Moral Injury Could Be a Signature Wound of the AI Battlefield

The operational and structural-critique lineages of moral injury usually argue about leadership failure and betrayal. This piece adds a third axis: distance without protection. Drone and AI-assisted operators were supposed to be insulated by remove, and instead they describe killing that is up close and personal from a console, with less agency and less time to make a moral judgment than a rifleman on the ground.

Researcher Jesse Kirkpatrick of George Mason University argues that AI-enabled targeting does not reduce moral injury risk the way early theory predicted. Surveillance-heavy operators develop intimate familiarity with a target's daily life before a strike, and automated decision support can compress the window for moral deliberation. Retired Lt. Col. Rachel VanLandingham pushes back on any account that lets distance and automation dilute accountability. The Army has already convened a chaplain conference on AI's moral impacts, and the fiscal 2026 NDAA mandates a mental health study of drone personnel.

Royal College of Psychiatrists Calls for Improved Trauma Care, Asks Whether Psychedelic Therapy Can Help

This is the credibility-stress story of the week. The Royal College isn't cheerleading, it's naming the reason MDMA got rejected: unclear blinding, addiction risk, protocols that aren't standardized. If you talk to clients about MDMA therapy, this side of the ledger needs to be as memorized as the trial results.

The Royal College of Psychiatrists' position statement calls for trauma-informed, person-centred care and singles out MDMA as clinically promising because patients can stay verbal and communicative with their therapist during the session, unlike with psilocybin. It also flags psilocybin and medicinal cannabis as worth researching further. But it names the obstacles directly: the FDA declined to approve MDMA-assisted therapy in 2025 over insufficient blinding, addiction potential, and inconsistent protocols, and the UK's Schedule 1 and 2 restrictions still choke off research access. Australia's 2023 rescheduling is cited as the model the RCPsych wants the UK to move toward.

Pilot Study: Psilocybin-Assisted Therapy for Veterans With Severe PTSD

This is one of the better-run trials in the space right now, and it's worth reading past the headline number. Twelve veterans, drawn from an applicant pool north of thirty-six hundred, tells you two things: the demand is real, and this result does not yet generalize. Hold the number and the caveat in the same hand.

Published in Communications Medicine on July 30, this pilot combined synthetic psilocybin, fifteen and twenty-five milligram doses, with eight hours of prep therapy, two dosing sessions, and six to eight hours of integration work over eleven weeks. Twelve veterans completed it, drawn from an applicant pool of more than 3,600. Nine of twelve showed clinical response, PTSD symptom scores dropped an average of 27.5 points on clinician-rated measures, 75% were in remission at one month, and suicidal ideation scores held steady rather than worsening. No serious adverse events; mild headache was the most common side effect. The researchers state their own limitation plainly: pilot studies with no control group produce inflated effect sizes compared to standard trials. A larger randomized controlled trial with six-month follow-up is next.

Try this: Use the 27.5-point figure and the no-control-group caveat together if a client brings this study up. Both belong in the same sentence.
Source: Medical Dialogues

New Zealand Approves MDMA for PTSD: Two Prescribers, One Steep Price Tag

New Zealand becomes the second country after Australia (2023) to authorize pharmaceutical-grade MDMA for PTSD, following a yearlong review by the medicines regulator Medsafe. Two psychiatrists, one in Wellington and one in Auckland, are currently approved to prescribe it, always inside a controlled clinical setting paired with psychotherapy, never as a take-home dose. The trial data behind the approval is genuinely strong: an initial 2021 trial found 88% of severe PTSD patients improved, with 67% no longer meeting diagnostic criteria; a 2023 follow-up put that figure at 71.2%. The FDA rejected the same evidence base in 2024, citing concerns that prior MDMA exposure among trial participants biased the results. Reported treatment cost runs to roughly NZ$45,000, a figure that will decide who this actually reaches.

Two prescribers. One country. A price tag doing the work access policy hasn't caught up to yet.

New Zealand just did what the US hasn't: approved MDMA-assisted therapy in a real clinical pathway, not just an executive order. But two prescribers nationwide and a price tag steep enough to make headlines tells you this isn't a standard of care yet, it's a boutique one. Watch the access data over the next year more than the approval headline.

How Durham's HEART Program Is Reshaping 911 Response

This is your job, reported from another city. Durham's HEART program is unarmed clinicians and peer support specialists answering the calls that don't need a badge: welfare checks, intoxication, low-risk mental health crises. Nearly a hundred US cities now run something like it, up from six in 2020. Phoenix's own program is part of that same wave; this is comparison data you don't usually get handed.

Durham, North Carolina's Holistic Empathetic Assistance Response Team, launched in 2022, sends unarmed mental health clinicians and peer support specialists to answer low-risk 911 calls instead of police: trespassing, panhandling, public intoxication, welfare checks, and select mental health crises. The city also runs a co-response team pairing clinicians with officers, plus a clinician embedded directly in the call center. HEART has responded to more than 25,000 calls since launch, roughly 28 per 1,000 residents, among the highest rates nationally. A Duke University study found call volume rising, which researchers read as growing public trust rather than a problem. Research Triangle Institute data shows fewer arrests and rare need for police backup. Police in Durham have come to treat HEART as a fourth wing of 911 response alongside police, fire, and EMS.

Try this: Pull the Duke and RTI data points, arrest reduction and backup-request rate, for your next conversation about program legitimacy with department leadership.

How Trauma Drives Both Violence and Peace in Conflict Zones

This is moral injury research at a scale you don't usually see: longitudinal surveys across Iraq, South Sudan, and Syria instead of a single clinical population. The finding worth sitting with is that current living conditions predict mental health outcomes more strongly than the original trauma exposure. That's a direct challenge to any framework that treats the initiating event as the whole story.

King's College London's XCEPT research programme, with survey director Dr Fiona McEwen, tracks how trauma from violent conflict drives both continued violence and, in some cases, movement toward peace. The methodology combines longitudinal psychometric surveys, starting with 2,000 people in Iraq with nine-month follow-up, and qualitative work spanning clinical psychology, history, sociology, and political science. Two findings stand out: present-day conditions, access to resources and education, predict mental health problems more strongly than past conflict exposure does, and adverse childhood experiences plus low social capital correlate with recruitment into groups like ISIS, which researchers describe as deliberately eroding institutional trust to drive recruitment. Strong family and social support structures showed up as a real protective factor for treatment outcomes.

No. 10 · Contemplative · 2026-09-25

Sixty Years of Linguistic Change in the Poetry of a Zen Practitioner

Sixty years, nine collections, one long study of what happens to a person's language after decades of sitting. This isn't the wellness-industry version of contemplative practice. It's someone tracking what actually erodes: the pronoun I, certainty, sensory over-specification, in favor of something quieter and less asserted. It rhymes with your own hesychast reading more than most psychology papers will.

This case study analyzed 517 poems across nine Gary Snyder collections spanning 1959 to 2015, using computational text analysis measuring emotion vocabulary, self-reference, and sensory language, with poet Wendell Berry as a non-practicing comparison to isolate practice effects from ordinary aging. Three patterns held across every method the researchers used: negative-emotion language declined steadily while positive and neutral language held stable; first-person pronouns and certainty claims retreated together with somatic vocabulary; and sensory specificity thinned while the language drifted toward abstraction. The authors are careful to say these changes are consistent with sustained contemplative practice, not proof of it, and they frame the method as a way around the weeks-to-months ceiling that limits most meditation research: a poet's collected work is fifty years of unprompted, non-reactive data.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
