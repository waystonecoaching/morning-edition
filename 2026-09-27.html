Davis, Farchione and Colleagues Present FDA's Psychedelic Drug Framework in NEJM

Four FDA staffers laying out the agency's actual regulatory framework in NEJM, not a press release, is the closest thing to a primary source you'll get on where federal policy is headed. This is the ground everything downstream (Optimi's Phase 3, the Trump administration's moves, state programs) has to build on. Scope-of-practice note: this is drug regulation and clinical trial architecture, adjacent to but outside coaching. Read it for the terrain, not for anything to apply directly with a client.

In a commentary published in NEJM, four FDA officials (Davis, Farchione, and colleagues) describe what they call the agency's new framework for evaluating psychedelic drugs. The piece opens by laying out the scale of the U.S. mental health crisis, with particular attention to depression and PTSD, before turning to how FDA intends to structure review and approval pathways for this drug class going forward.

Try this: Worth a close read past the abstract if you're citing FDA posture to BTTY cohort-mates or clients asking whether MDMA therapy is 'approved yet.' The honest answer is still 'framework, not approval.'

Why respect and ethical leadership are critical to preventing PTSD and moral injury in service members

This sits in the structural-critique lineage, Litz, Shay, betrayal-and-leadership-failure work, not the operational/performance-psych camp. It argues the injury isn't just exposure, it's what leadership does or fails to do around exposure. That tension between 'build resilience in the individual' and 'fix the institution that betrayed them' is not a contradiction to smooth over. It's the actual fault line in the field, and it's the one you sit on with clients who are angrier at command than at the call that broke them.

The piece argues that ethical leadership and institutional respect are not soft add-ons to PTSD prevention but load-bearing factors, positioning moral injury as substantially a leadership and betrayal problem rather than purely an exposure problem. It draws the line from failures of respect and ethical conduct up the chain to the severity and persistence of trauma symptoms in service members.

Source: The Fulcrum

No. 05 · First Responder MH · 2026-09-04

Examining veteran preferences and attitudes across intervention modalities for posttraumatic stress disorder

Peer-reviewed, EarlyView, and about the thing that actually determines outcomes more than modality quality does: whether a veteran will show up and stay in a given treatment. First responders overlap heavily with veterans in this literature's population and its findings. If you're helping a client sort exposure therapy versus EMDR versus a psychedelic-assisted track, preference and attitude data is the missing piece most modality debates skip.

The study examines how veterans' own preferences and attitudes vary across different PTSD treatment modalities, looking at what shapes willingness to engage with one intervention type over another rather than only comparing clinical efficacy between them. The abstract situates this as filling a gap in research that has focused on outcome data while under-attending to patient choice and acceptability.

Try this: If you're fielding 'which treatment should I even try' questions from clients, this is a citable reminder that fit and buy-in are legitimate clinical variables, not just personal preference to be talked out of.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

No. 10 · First Responder MH · 2026-09-25

Sixty years of linguistic change in the poetry of a Zen practitioner: a longitudinal case study of Gary Snyder

Sixty years of a single practitioner's language, tracked for what long-term contemplative practice actually does to self-referential processing and perceptual engagement. This is an empirical version of a claim the Desert Fathers made without needing a study: sustained attention changes how a person speaks about themselves, not just how they feel. Worth sitting with, not skimming.

The study is a longitudinal case analysis of poet and Zen practitioner Gary Snyder's language across six decades, testing whether decades of meditation practice leave measurable traces in natural language use. It frames long-term contemplative practice as widely assumed to reshape emotion regulation, self-referential processing, and perceptual engagement, while noting that empirical research on these transformations has been thin, and positions this case study as a step toward closing that gap.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
