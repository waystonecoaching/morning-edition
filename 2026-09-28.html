Davis, Farchione and Colleagues Present FDA's Psychedelic Drug Framework in NEJM

This is the regulator naming the terms. Four FDA staffers used the New England Journal of Medicine, not a press release, to lay out how the agency intends to handle psychedelic drug applications going forward. Everything else in this issue, the MDMA trials, the psilocybin pilots, the state and federal moves, gets built inside whatever framework this becomes.

In a commentary published September 9 in NEJM, four FDA staffers including Davis and Farchione opened by naming the severity of the US mental health crisis, depression and PTSD specifically, before laying out FDA's approach to psychedelic drug development. The agency has already granted Breakthrough Therapy designation to several candidates, including Definium Therapeutics' LSD candidate DT120 for major depressive disorder, and Compass Pathways is currently under rolling review. The guidance behind this framework was refined after roughly 200 public comments and three additional years of data from drug development programs. The authors are explicit that psychedelic therapies remain investigational and that FDA's approach to folding these programs into existing regulatory structures is still evolving.

Try this: Read the actual NEJM commentary, not just coverage of it, before citing FDA's posture on psychedelics to a client or referral source. The framework is still being built in public.

Can MDMA Help Heal the Wounds of War? Inside an Israeli PTSD Trial

This trial is running on soldiers from an active conflict, not a population years removed from combat. The 80 percent figure will travel further than the caveats around it, so it is worth carrying the caveats along with it.

Clalit Health's HaEmek Medical Center is running an MDMA-assisted psychotherapy trial for soldiers with PTSD, led by psychiatry director Dr. Alon Reshef. The protocol runs 13 sessions: preparatory psychotherapy, three supervised MDMA sessions each with two trained therapists present, then follow-up integration work. HaEmek reports roughly 80 percent of soldiers who completed the protocol saw significant symptom reduction, most no longer meeting diagnostic criteria for PTSD. Reshef frames MDMA as a tool inside an intensive psychotherapeutic process, not the treatment itself, and the piece is careful to note the results are preliminary, that MDMA stays illegal outside authorized research, and that participants had to fail conventional treatment and pass strict screening before enrolling.

A Paramedic Rewrites How Student EMTs Handle Trauma

This is the exact infrastructure question Mark works inside every shift, built small at a campus EMS agency. One person, formally trained as a resiliency officer, decides that survivable trauma exposure needs a system, not luck.

Paul Smyth, manager of EMS for Syracuse University Ambulance and a paramedic of nearly three decades, was selected as one of 20 statewide fellows in New York's Mental Health Fellowship. He completed a three-day resiliency officer training built on a model the armed forces developed and the FBI later adopted, and now serves as SUA's resiliency point person: running weekly critical incident stress debriefing sessions, surveying students anonymously on their current mental health support, and routing students to the Barnes Center at The Arch for counseling. Smyth's framing is blunt: the average person hits three or four critical incidents in a lifetime, first responders can hit hundreds, and processing that load is not optional if the career is meant to last. The fellowship runs through June 2027; Smyth is one of only two collegiate EMS professionals to go through it since it launched two years ago.

Try this: Worth a look at the CISD model Smyth runs weekly, and whether the armed-forces resiliency officer training he completed has a civilian fire-service equivalent worth pointing coaching clients toward.

Why Moral Injury Could Be a Signature Wound of the AI Battlefield

This complicates a comfortable assumption, that distance and automation would insulate operators from moral injury. If anything the mechanism runs the other way, and it is a live question for how moral injury gets framed with any client whose work involves remote or AI-assisted decisions, not just infantry.

The piece argues AI integration in warfare is producing its own form of moral injury, not less of it. Researcher Jesse Kirkpatrick names it the paradox of distance: a drone operator can watch a target plant a bomb, buy milk, coach a kid's soccer team, then take part in killing him, intimate familiarity despite physical remoteness. A second mechanism is reduced agency, operators left holding responsibility for outcomes decided faster than they could meaningfully weigh in on, what Kirkpatrick calls moral injury from too little agency rather than too much. Army chaplains have started attending conferences on the moral and spiritual impact of AI-integrated missions, and the 25th Infantry Division has already tested AI in its sensor-to-shooter artillery chain, so this is not theoretical. Retired Lt. Col. Rachel VanLandingham is quoted insisting automation does not relieve operators or commanders of accountability. Congress has mandated a 2026 study on PTSD, depression, anxiety, burnout, and moral injury specifically among drone personnel.

Latent Profile Analysis of Sleep Quality and Its Association With Psychological Resilience Among Emergency Department Nurses

Not first responders in the fire-service sense, ED nurses in Shandong, China, and that gap is worth naming plainly rather than borrowing the data dishonestly. The mechanism still transfers: shift-disrupted sleep and trauma exposure compounding each other, resilience acting as the buffer between them.

Researchers surveyed 611 emergency department nurses across 12 tertiary hospitals in Shandong, using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. Latent profile analysis found four distinct sleep patterns: severely disturbed sleep (8 percent, worst across every dimension including medication reliance), sleep-onset difficulty with medication use (15 percent), mildly disturbed sleep (27 percent), and good sleep quality (50 percent). Higher resilience scores were significantly associated with lower odds of landing in any disturbed profile, with the strongest protective effect against the severe-disturbance group, a 53 percent reduction in odds per unit increase in resilience. The authors recommend different interventions by career stage: limiting night shifts and building resilience training for younger nurses, flexible scheduling and preventive psychological support for mid-career nurses, and non-pharmacological sleep approaches paired with shift redesign for nurses already relying on medication.

Try this: The career-stage-specific intervention model here, different supports for early versus mid-career shift workers, is worth borrowing directly for how coaching gets structured for younger versus veteran responders.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Army Veteran Finds New Hope With Psilocybin Therapy for Post-Traumatic Stress at Ohio State

This is the honest version of a psilocybin story, real remission numbers sitting next to the researchers' own admission that a pilot with no control group inflates effect size. That combination, hope and rigor held together instead of one selling the other, is what actually earns trust in this literature.

Ohio State's Center for Psychedelic Drug Research and Education ran an 11-week pilot with 12 veterans, 9 men and 3 women, carrying severe treatment-resistant PTSD: eight hours of preparatory psychotherapy, two psilocybin doses at 15mg then 25mg, and six to eight hours of integration work after. At one month, 75 percent of participants no longer met diagnostic criteria for PTSD, nine of twelve showed clinical response, and the group averaged a 27.5-point drop in clinician-rated PTSD symptoms. The only common side effect was mild headache, no serious adverse events, no increase in suicidal ideation. First author Stacey Armstrong and senior author Alan Davis, both with CPDRE, published the work July 30 in Communications Medicine, and they state plainly that pilot studies without a control group tend to overstate effect size. They are now seeking funding for a randomized controlled trial and want to follow this cohort out to six months.

Try this: Cite the Communications Medicine paper itself, not the press coverage, when a client asks what the psilocybin research on veteran PTSD actually shows.

Why Respect and Ethical Leadership Are Critical to Preventing PTSD and Moral Injury in Service Members

Dr. Barry B. Perlman, a retired psychiatrist and former president of the New York State Psychiatric Association, argues in this Fulcrum opinion piece that disrespectful or capricious leadership is itself a driver of PTSD and moral injury in service members, not a morale issue running alongside it. He points to the recent dismissals of General C.Q. Brown Jr., Admiral Linda Fagan, and Admiral Yvette Davids, and to reported comments from Trump and Hegseth about sailors' mental health aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln, as leadership conduct that sends a demoralizing signal, particularly to women and minority service members. He ties this to established clinical ground: rising PTSD rates among Desert Storm and post-9/11 veterans, and the specific harm of moral injury when personnel are made to act against their own values by the people meant to lead them.

Service members deserve thoughtful and moral civilian leadership.

This sits in the structural-critique lineage of moral injury work, not the operational or performance-psych lineage, and it reads better held apart rather than smoothed into the same bucket. Perlman's claim is that leadership conduct is itself a clinical variable, not a culture problem sitting beside the clinical one.

Pα+ Psychedelic Bulletin #231: Compass Shares 52-Week Follow-Up, Training Grant Recipients

Keep the scope straight here: this is treatment-resistant depression data, not PTSD, and the number itself is modest once it is sat with. It is a useful check against reading every psilocybin headline as PTSD news.

Compass Pathways released 52-week follow-up data from Part C of its Phase 3 COMP005 trial, which originally enrolled 258 patients with treatment-resistant depression on a 25mg dose of psilocybin candidate COMP360 against placebo. About 179 of the 258 moved into Part C, an open-label 26-week extension where anyone who had not remitted got another dose. Among those who received active drug from the start, the average reduction from baseline at week 52 was 13 points on the MADRS depression scale, with Compass describing notable additional benefit from retreatment. That is real movement, but it is a long way from a cure, and it is worth noticing that a meaningful share of patients needed a second dose to get there. The same bulletin covers five new training grant recipients from Compass.

Practical Motivational Interviewing Techniques for Busy GPs

This is Getting Real's direct-speech-as-contact instinct dressed in clinical language, and it is the most immediately usable thing in this issue. OARS is a structure that hands over cleanly, without turning a fifteen-minute check-in into a technique demonstration.

Drawing on RACGP guidance and the original Miller and Rollnick framework, the piece lays out OARS for time-constrained clinicians: open-ended questions instead of yes or no ones, affirmations that name a patient's actual effort, reflective listening that rephrases what someone said back to them to surface what is underneath it, and summaries that consolidate the conversation and name the gap between where someone is and where they say they want to be. Three supporting moves round it out: draw out change talk with open questions instead of arguing someone into change, meet resistance, sustain talk, by naming the barrier rather than pushing past it, and protect the person's sense of choice so they do not dig in defensively. Clinical psychologist Kylie McKenzie is cited on MI's wide use in Australian GP training, though the piece is honest that outcome-specific evidence for MI in primary care is thinner than its popularity would suggest.

Try this: Pressure-test whether the OARS sequence, open question then affirmation then reflection then summary, maps onto how intake calls already run, or whether it is a tighter structure than what is currently in use.

Healing Thyself: Managing Moral Injury in Healthcare

A quiet closer. Healthcare's version of moral injury is the same wound in a different uniform, the harm of being made to act against your own values by circumstance rather than by any one person's failure. Patrick's prescription, name it out loud instead of carrying it alone, is not new, but it is worth ending the issue on the plainest possible statement of it.

Wendy L. Patrick, a trial attorney and behavioral analyst, distinguishes moral injury from ordinary burnout: burnout is workload, moral injury is the specific wound of being unable to act consistently with your own ethical values. In healthcare that shows up around end-of-life decisions, rationed resources under institutional constraint, and the compressed, high-stakes calls made in emergency, ICU, pediatric, and oncology settings, sharpened further by crises like COVID that forced rapid decisions with too little to work with. Her recommended response has no shortcuts in it: build real ethical support structures, improve communication inside teams, create an actual culture where people talk through what they are carrying instead of sitting on it alone, and treat moral resilience as something to build deliberately rather than assume people either have or do not.

Source: Psychology Today
