**Link:** [https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1897894](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1897894)

**Why this matters to you:** Not first responders in the fire-service sense, ED nurses in Shandong, China, and that gap is worth naming plainly rather than borrowing the data dishonestly. The mechanism still transfers: shift-disrupted sleep and trauma exposure compounding each other, resilience acting as the buffer between them.

**The story:** Researchers surveyed 611 emergency department nurses across 12 tertiary hospitals in Shandong, using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. Latent profile analysis found four distinct sleep patterns: severely disturbed sleep (8 percent, worst across every dimension including medication reliance), sleep-onset difficulty with medication use (15 percent), mildly disturbed sleep (27 percent), and good sleep quality (50 percent). Higher resilience scores were significantly associated with lower odds of landing in any disturbed profile, with the strongest protective effect against the severe-disturbance group, a 53 percent reduction in odds per unit increase in resilience. The authors recommend different interventions by career stage: limiting night shifts and building resilience training for younger nurses, flexible scheduling and preventive psychological support for mid-career nurses, and non-pharmacological sleep approaches paired with shift redesign for nurses already relying on medication.

**Actionable:** The career-stage-specific intervention model here, different supports for early versus mid-career shift workers, is worth borrowing directly for how coaching gets structured for younger versus veteran responders.

**Why this matters to you:** A quiet closer. **The story:** Wendy L.
