A single dose of MDMA prevents trauma-fueled drinking in genetically prone rats

Responders drink after bad calls, and this is the closest thing to a mechanism story for that pattern. Keep it in the rat cage: the effect showed up only in the genetically vulnerable strain, and fear extinction did not improve. Substance use is on the scope edge, so nothing here is a coaching tool.

Researchers ran 53 alcohol-preferring Indiana rats and 64 outbred Wistar rats through fear conditioning, then gave a single MDMA injection or saline before an extinction session, followed by six weeks of voluntary alcohol access. MDMA blunted trauma-driven drinking in the vulnerable strain only, with no better fear extinction than saline. Wistar rats showed no trauma-driven drinking at all. Limits: one dose and timing, voluntary limited access, no blood alcohol, and no relational support of the kind human MDMA therapy relies on.

Try this: If a client links drinking to calls, the useful question is what the drink is doing, not what could replace it.

The Bench 988: A different approach to firefighter peer support

Peer support aimed at the retiree, the man who lost the firehouse and the people in it. That gap is real in the culture and almost nobody staffs it. It runs on walks and a bench, which is the opposite of a clinical program, and that is the point. Suicide is the background here; the bench is for contact before crisis, and it hands off when crisis arrives.

Two retired Orlando firefighters, Chris DeMaagd and Joseph McCluan, built The Bench 988 from a memorial to two firefighters lost to suicide. The model puts tennis shoes on the kitchen table: weekly walks, sunlight, and the camaraderie retirees miss, with links between retirees and active firefighters. It aims at people before the crisis rather than after. The 988 refers to the national crisis line.

Try this: Ask what a retiree-facing contact point would look like inside a department peer program you know.
Source: FireRescue1

Why moral injury could be a signature wound of the AI battlefield

Structural-critique camp, not the performance frame. The injury here comes from nominal responsibility without real control, which is a leadership and design failure, not a resilience deficit. That argument travels straight into dispatch, triage algorithms, and any responder role where a system decides and a person carries it. Spiritual and pastoral care are in the proposal, which fits the way moral injury actually presents.

George Mason ethicist Jesse Kirkpatrick warns operators of AI-supported weapons could suffer moral injury from too little agency, bearing responsibility without genuine control. He describes a paradox of distance: drone operators far from the battlefield who become intimately familiar with the people they watch. Retired Air Force Lt. Col. Rachel VanLandingham is also quoted. Proposals include AI ethics training for chaplains, more AI literacy, joining mental health professionals with pastoral care, and more research.

Try this: Ask a client where in their job they carry the outcome without the control. That is where the injury starts.
Source: Task & Purpose

Music as a tool for personal and social resilience: a three-level conceptual model

The author proposes three interacting levels: structural engagement with tension and release, personal meaning tied to memory and identity, and dialogical sharing that builds recognition and belonging. Attention at one level can open the next, and sharing feeds back into meaning. It names failure conditions such as ruminative engagement or dismissive reception. It reports no new empirical findings and awaits testing.

Music is not inherently beneficial; context and individual history decide.

A human-scale close. The model takes seriously that music is not automatically good for you: rumination and dismissive listeners can turn it against the person. That is true of most things offered to responders as self-care, and it keeps the claim honest. Theory only, so hold it loosely.

Try this: Ask a client what song they reach for after a hard shift, and who they have ever played it for.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology
