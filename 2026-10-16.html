The Psychedelic Practitioner Issue 7: Preparedness (Systems)

This is the practitioner-side view of what approval would actually mean, and it is more useful than the approval headlines. The operational load lands on whoever sits in the room: monitoring time, staffing, payer fights, labels that shape how care gets delivered. Psychedelic Alpha is a trade outlet, so read it as industry reporting, not trial evidence. Anything that touches dosing, screening or monitoring is clinical and outside coaching scope. The integration lane on either side of it is where your path runs.

Issue 7, dated 14 October, looks at systems readiness for psychedelic therapies with a U.S. lens. It reports that the FDA could approve a first psychedelic therapy within months, that Compass expects to file its psilocybin application this quarter with a possible launch in the first half of 2027, and that the expected REMS is modeled on Spravato with at least six hours of monitoring per session. Practitioner interviews put ethics approval in Australia at four to six months and Oregon screening at about 75 days before dosing, and name staffing and payer hurdles as the bottlenecks. An ethics column argues labels and billing codes will shape delivery.

Try this: Read the REMS and monitoring sections with one question in mind: who does integration work after a six-hour monitored session, and under what credential. That is the opening for your lane.

MAPS Attends Historic FDA Hearing on Psychedelics

MAPS is an advocacy organization and this is its own account of the hearing, so weigh it that way. The part worth your time is the agenda it names: systems readiness, therapist training and patient access. Those are the questions that decide whether first responders ever see this care. Note the credibility baggage: the MDMA application history is the reason regulators are asking about training and monitoring at all. This sits in clinical territory, not coaching scope.

The post covers the FDA's 14 September hearing on potential future therapeutic use of psychedelic drugs, which MAPS calls one of the most consequential regulatory moments for the field. MAPS says it attended and will submit written testimony to the public docket on systems readiness, therapist training and patient access. Per the Psychedelic Practitioner newsletter, the comment period closed 5 October. The piece is an organizational statement, not an analysis of what the agency signaled.

Try this: Find the docket testimony on therapist training and credentialing when it posts. Whatever training standard emerges will define who can legitimately do preparation and integration.

Impaired but now diagnosable: Combining analytic frameworks for incorporating symptom weighting in the classification of full-threshold and subthreshold posttraumatic stress disorder

Peer-reviewed and quietly important for your caseload. The sample is adults with PTSD and substance use disorders in treatment, which is the high-acuity edge, so this informs how you screen and when you refer, not what you treat. The finding is that people who miss the DSM symptom count can still carry full-threshold severity. Think of the responder who says he is fine because he does not tick enough boxes.

Using data from treatment-seeking adults with comorbid PTSD and substance use disorders, the authors weighted symptoms by how much each contributes to severity, using item response theory. They then looked at people who reach the severity threshold without meeting the standard DSM symptom count. Three groups emerged: those meeting full criteria both ways, and two underdiagnosed groups, one with no avoidance symptoms and one with only a single arousal symptom. Negative alterations in cognitions and mood did not differ across groups, which the authors suggest may mark high distress regardless of diagnostic status. The abstract gives no class sizes or effect statistics.

Try this: When a client is below the diagnostic line but describing guilt, shame or flattened mood, treat that as a referral signal and not as reassurance.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Veterans' positive parenting practices and satisfaction over time: Examining the impact of moral injury, posttraumatic stress, and internalizing symptoms

Moral injury as a family-system problem, not only an individual one. Veterans are not firefighters, but the pathway (moral injury, then withdrawal, then what the kids and spouse live with) will be familiar from shift culture. This sits in the structural-critique lineage: it measures moral injury separately from PTSD. Abstract text was not retrievable this morning, so the summary below stays at what the title and citation establish.

Published in the October 2026 issue of the Journal of Traumatic Stress, the study follows veterans over time and examines how moral injury, posttraumatic stress and internalizing symptoms relate to positive parenting practices and to parenting satisfaction. Its design separates moral injury from PTSD as a predictor. Effect sizes and sample details were not available in the feed or on a retrieval attempt, so check the paper before citing a number.

Try this: If you work with a responder who is withdrawing at home, ask what he thinks his kids are seeing. Let the answer lead.

Silence as relational adaptation: Strategic communicative practices of Chinese partners in Sino-Western intercultural marriages

Off your usual shelf and worth it. Silence read as active adaptation, not withdrawal, is a useful counterweight to the responder-at-home-going-quiet story, and it pairs with direct speech as contact: the question is what a given silence is doing. Caveat: qualitative grounded theory in intercultural marriages, small sample, and the paper's own reported counts do not fully agree. Use it as a lens, not as evidence about responders.

The authors interviewed Chinese partners in Sino-Western marriages and combined that with social media videos and texts, coding with a grounded theory method. They find silence is triggered by differences in values, conflict over everyday norms and limits on second-language ability, and that it serves to keep intimacy, protect boundaries and help mutual adaptation. Couples manage it individually, by clarifying their own silence, and jointly, by agreeing on shared ways to read it. Handled that way, silence shifts from a source of misunderstanding to a relational resource.

Try this: Next time a client goes quiet mid-session, name the silence and ask what it is doing for him before you fill it.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

13point average MADRS drop at week 52, company-reported, partly open-label

Pa+ Psychedelic Bulletin #231: Compass Shares 52 Week Follow-Up, Training Grant Recipients

Read this one critically. These are company-reported topline numbers in treatment-resistant depression, not PTSD, and the durability claim leans on an open-label phase where people who relapsed were offered a second dose. That design flatters the 52-week figure. About 70 percent entered the open-label part and the reasons for discontinuation were not disclosed. Useful as a read on where psilocybin approval is heading, not as evidence for responder care. The piece was paywalled, so this rests on the excerpt.

Compass Pathways shared 52-week topline data from Part C of COMP005, a Phase 3 placebo-controlled trial of 25 mg COMP360 psilocybin in 258 patients with treatment-resistant depression. Part A was a 6-week blinded phase, Part B a 20-week blinded extension with possible retreatment, and Part C a 26-week open-label phase offering a further dose to those who relapsed or did not remit. Compass reports notable additional benefit from the further dose and, for people dosed in Part A, an average 13-point MADRS reduction from baseline at week 52. About 179 of 258 entered Part C, with dropout roughly even across arms.

Try this: When a client or colleague quotes the 52-week result, ask whether it was blinded or open-label, and who got the second dose.
Source: Psychedelic Alpha
