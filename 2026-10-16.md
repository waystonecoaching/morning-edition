### 03. Impaired but now diagnosable: Combining analytic frameworks for incorporating symptom weighting in the classification of full-threshold and subthreshold posttraumatic stress disorder

**Source:** Journal of Traumatic Stress  \
**Category:** first_responder_mh  \
**Published:** 2026-10-06  \
**Link:** [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jts.70112?af=R](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jts.70112?af=R)

**Why this matters to you:** Peer-reviewed and quietly important for your caseload. The sample is adults with PTSD and substance use disorders in treatment, which is the high-acuity edge, so this informs how you screen and when you refer, not what you treat. The finding is that people who miss the DSM symptom count can still carry full-threshold severity. Think of the responder who says he is fine because he does not tick enough boxes.

**The story:** Using data from treatment-seeking adults with comorbid PTSD and substance use disorders, the authors weighted symptoms by how much each contributes to severity, using item response theory. They then looked at people who reach the severity threshold without meeting the standard DSM symptom count. Three groups emerged: those meeting full criteria both ways, and two underdiagnosed groups, one with no avoidance symptoms and one with only a single arousal symptom. Negative alterations in cognitions and mood did not differ across groups, which the authors suggest may mark high distress regardless of diagnostic status. The abstract gives no class sizes or effect statistics.

**Actionable:** When a client is below the diagnostic line but describing guilt, shame or flattened mood, treat that as a referral signal and not as reassurance.

### 05. Silence as relational adaptation: Strategic communicative practices of Chinese partners in Sino-Western intercultural marriages

**Source:** Frontiers in Psychology  \
**Category:** coaching  \
**Published:** 2026-10-16  \
**Link:** [https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1957138](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1957138)

**Why this matters to you:** Off your usual shelf and worth it. Silence read as active adaptation, not withdrawal, is a useful counterweight to the responder-at-home-going-quiet story, and it pairs with direct speech as contact: the question is what a given silence is doing. Caveat: qualitative grounded theory in intercultural marriages, small sample, and the paper's own reported counts do not fully agree. Use it as a lens, not as evidence about responders.

**The story:** The authors interviewed Chinese partners in Sino-Western marriages and combined that with social media videos and texts, coding with a grounded theory method. They find silence is triggered by differences in values, conflict over everyday norms and limits on second-language ability, and that it serves to keep intimacy, protect boundaries and help mutual adaptation. Couples manage it individually, by clarifying their own silence, and jointly, by agreeing on shared ways to read it. Handled that way, silence shifts from a source of misunderstanding to a relational resource.

**Actionable:** Next time a client goes quiet mid-session, name the silence and ask what it is doing for him before you fill it.
