Examining the multivariate genetic architectures of posttraumatic stress disorder and cardiovascular disease phenotypes

PTSD and heart disease traveling together is not news on the floor. Cardiac events kill more firefighters on duty than fire does, and the men carrying the heaviest trauma load are often the same men carrying the worst cardiac risk. This paper asks whether some of that overlap is written in the genome rather than only in behavior and wear. If it holds, PTSD moves out of the mental health column and into the mortality column, which changes how you talk to a man who thinks his nightmares are a character problem.

The paper examines shared genetic architecture across posttraumatic stress disorder and cardiovascular disease phenotypes using multivariate methods. The feed carries title and citation only, with no abstract, so the effect sizes and the specific phenotypes are not in hand. Treat the direction as a hypothesis under test rather than a result to quote. Pull the full text before repeating anything about magnitude.

Try this: When a client treats his trauma symptoms as separate from his physical health, stop splitting them. Ask what his last physical said.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

AT the Forefront: Psychedelics Are Coming. Is PTSD Care Ready?

This is advocacy press, not literature, and it reads like it. The question underneath is the right one, and it reaches integration practitioners before it reaches anyone else. Approval does not build a care pathway. When a drug clears, the referral network, the prep work, and the integration hours decide whether anyone gets better, and almost none of that is funded or staffed.

Wounded Warrior Project argues the veteran PTSD care system is not prepared for psychedelic medicines arriving through regulatory channels. Only the headline and wire stub came through the feed, so the specific gaps the piece names are not in hand. The frame is institutional readiness rather than trial data. Read it as a position from an organization with a stake in the answer, and go to the source before citing it.

Try this: Write down what you would need in place to take an integration referral next month. Name the gaps, not the hopes.

Intimate Partner Violence and Paramedicine: An Updated Scoping Review of Perspectives and Practices

Medics see intimate partner violence before anyone else does and get the least training for it. The review maps what crews believe about these calls against what they do on scene, which is usually two different things. This sits on a hard scope edge: recognition is in the job, intervention mostly is not, and the gap between them is where a crew carries injury home. For coaching, the material is not the call. It is what a man does with having seen it and done nothing he was allowed to do.

An updated scoping review of paramedic perspectives and practices around intimate partner violence. The feed gives citation data only, with no abstract, so the number of studies included and the synthesized themes are not in hand. Scoping reviews map territory rather than settle a question, so expect training gaps and reporting pathways to be the finding. Get the full text before quoting any figure.

Try this: If a client keeps returning to a call he could not change, work the moral injury, not the incident.

Current Emergency Medical Services Systems Approaches to Refusal of Assessment, Treatment, or Transport: Examination of Statewide Protocols

Refusal is the thinnest ice in behavioral response. A man who declines transport may be exercising judgment, or may be in a state where judgment is not available to him, and the protocol a crew stands on changes at the state line. This paper reads the statewide protocols against each other. For a behavioral crisis role, this is the legal floor under every call where someone says no.

The study examines how statewide EMS systems handle refusal of assessment, treatment, or transport. The feed carries citation data without an abstract, so the number of states reviewed and the variation found are not in hand. The value is comparative: refusal language is where capacity, consent, and liability meet, and it is written differently in every jurisdiction. Read Arizona's language alongside it.

Try this: Read Phoenix Fire's own refusal and capacity language this week. Find the sentence a crew leans on when a person in crisis declines care.
Source: Prehospital Emergency Care

Veterans’ positive parenting practices and satisfaction over time: Examining the impact of moral injury, posttraumatic stress, and internalizing symptoms

Moral injury does not stay in the man. It shows up at his kitchen table, in how he handles a kid who talks back, in whether he can take any satisfaction in being a father. This paper tracks that over time and keeps moral injury separate from PTSD symptoms rather than blending them. The separation matters. Shame and self-condemnation move parenting differently than hyperarousal does.

A longitudinal examination of how moral injury, posttraumatic stress, and internalizing symptoms relate to veterans' positive parenting practices and parenting satisfaction. The feed carries the citation without an abstract, so direction and magnitude are not in hand. The design treats moral injury as distinct from PTSD rather than a subtype of it. That places it in the structural-critique lineage, Litz and Shay rather than performance psychology.

Try this: Ask a first responder client what his trauma costs his family, not only what it costs him. The answer usually arrives faster.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Compass Says NDA Will Be Filed Within Two Weeks, Declines to Comment on Usona

Two psilocybin applications are now racing the same window. Compass says its filing comes inside two weeks, and Usona filed days earlier. Both are for treatment-resistant depression, not PTSD, so this does not move responder trauma care directly. It does set the regulatory template a PTSD indication would later have to walk through, and it tells you how fast the ground is moving.

Compass Pathways reported Q3 2026 results stating its COMP360 psilocybin application for treatment-resistant depression is nearing completion and will be filed within two weeks. The company declined to comment on Usona, which filed its own psilocybin application days before. The indication is depression, not PTSD. Filing is not approval, and it says nothing yet about label, risk management requirements, or who gets to deliver the therapy hours.

Try this: Keep the depression and PTSD timelines separate when clients ask. Conflating them is how false hope gets built.

Okla. law requires overdose notifications to law enforcement

Oklahoma has enacted a law requiring overdose notifications to law enforcement. Only the headline and wire stub came through the feed, so the reporting threshold, the data retained, and any immunity provisions are not in hand. The mechanism worth watching is whether mandatory reporting suppresses bystander calls. Get the statute text before drawing a conclusion.

A policy written to save a life can raise the price of asking for one.

A notification requirement sounds administrative until you sit in the truck. When EMS has to report an overdose to law enforcement, the person on the floor learns it, and next time they or their friends think twice about calling at all. This is the substance use scope edge in plain form: a policy written to help can raise the price of asking for help. Watch whether Arizona follows.

Try this: If you coach anyone in recovery, know what gets reported in your state and who sees it.

‘Deep, profound anguish’: moral injury behind teachers’ decision to quit

Moral injury was named in combat and has since turned up wherever people are held responsible for outcomes they are not given the means to produce. Teachers leaving over it is the same structure firefighters describe: the job asked me to do right and the system made it impossible. The population changes and the injury does not. Useful as evidence that this is organizational, not a weakness in the man.

The piece reports teachers leaving the profession over moral injury rather than workload alone, describing anguish tied to being unable to act in students' interests. Education trade press, headline and stub only in the feed, so the sourcing and any underlying study are not in hand. Treat it as field signal rather than data. The cross-occupational pattern is the content.

Try this: When a client calls it burnout, ask whether he was stopped from doing right. Different injury, different work.

Living under a “seismic time bomb”: hyperarousal and insomnia severity in the context of anticipated earthquake threat

No polyvagal paper came in today, so this carries the autonomic slot, and it earns it. The study looks at people whose nervous systems hold sympathetic arousal for a disaster that has not happened yet. That is standby physiology, and it is what a man on shift lives in for twenty four hours at a stretch. Anticipation alone, with no incident, degrades sleep. Say that plain to a client who cannot work out why he is wrecked after a quiet tour.

The study examined trauma-related symptom dimensions, emotional distress, and insomnia severity among adults living in Istanbul under anticipated major earthquake threat, in a non-clinical sample. Hyperarousal is the dimension of interest, tested against insomnia severity before any disaster occurs. The abstract came through in part and the effect sizes did not. This is correlational, and it is not a responder sample, so carry the mechanism across and leave the numbers behind.

Try this: Ask a client what his sleep does the night before a shift, not just the night after a bad one.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Body image disturbance and post-traumatic growth among patients with an intestinal stoma: a sequential mixed-methods study

Odd place to end, and it belongs here. These are people whose bodies were changed permanently to keep them alive, who then have to work out who they are afterward. That is the same room a man sits in after a career-ending injury or a medical retirement. Growth and disturbance show up in the same person at the same time, which is the part most frameworks get wrong by making them a sequence.

A sequential mixed-methods study of body image disturbance and post-traumatic growth in patients living with an intestinal stoma, examining self-identity, psychological wellbeing, and adaptation to daily life. The partial abstract describes the design and the population, and the findings did not come through the feed. Mixed-methods work of this kind tends to surface distress and growth inside the same account. Read it for the structure of that coexistence rather than for a result.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology
