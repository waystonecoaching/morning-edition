Usona Files Psilocybin for Depression NDA as Race With Compass Tightens

A nonprofit just put the second psychedelic application in front of FDA. The indication is major depression, not PTSD, so it does not change responder care today. It does show the review pathway getting walked in real time, and the timelines in the piece lean on anonymous sourcing and analyst judgment. Read it as trade reporting, not as a decision.

Usona Institute has completed its new drug application for psilocybin in major depressive disorder, per Psychedelic Alpha's reporting. That makes it the second psychedelic application under FDA review, after a refiled MDMA application for PTSD. Usona holds a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher, so a decision could land late 2026 on optimistic assumptions or early 2027. Compass Pathways had guided to its own filing within the quarter and had not filed when this ran. The article reports no efficacy or safety data, and Usona declined to comment.

Try this: When clients ask where psychedelic therapy stands, separate three things: filed, reviewed, approved. Only the first has happened here, and only for depression.
Source: Psychedelic Alpha

2026 Third Quarter: Legislative Wrap Up

This is advocacy-organization reporting, so read the framing as MAPS's. The state-level counts are still useful because policy is moving faster than the evidence base, and that gap is the credibility risk in this field. Nothing in it addresses first responders specifically.

MAPS reports at least 23 states have introduced psychedelic legislation in 2026 and at least ten are backing ibogaine clinical research. Five states have enacted trigger laws meant to streamline psilocybin access once FDA approves. Louisiana's SB 43 created a Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy Initiative covering ibogaine, psilocybin, and MDMA, effective August 1, 2026. California's AB 113 directs $4.7 million toward research that could help veterans with treatment-resistant conditions. The page does not mention first responders.

Try this: If your Arizona clients ask about legal access, check your own state's bills directly instead of relying on a national tally.
Source: MAPS

No. 03 · First Responder MH · 2026-11-04

Navigating First Responder Mental Health: The Cabinet, Empathy, and Healing

Sponsored podcast episode, so the sponsor shapes the frame, and it is a therapist's account, not research. The cabinet metaphor for compartmentalizing is familiar shift-culture language, and the episode ties it to nervous system regulation and addiction. It sits near your scope edge: it touches substance use and treatment recommendations such as EMDR that belong to licensed clinicians. Good for how responders describe their own experience, thin as evidence.

Host Scott Hewlett talks with licensed marriage and family therapist Destiny Morris about the hidden toll of emergency work. Morris describes responders keeping trauma in mental cabinets so they can keep working, and says unprocessed material builds into burnout, lost empathy, and addiction. The conversation covers nervous system regulation, trauma held in the body, and EMDR as one route for struggling responders. The closing message is that no one is too far gone to heal. The episode is sponsored by Dreamseat.

Try this: Borrow the cabinet image with a client only if it is theirs first. Ask what is in their cabinet before you hand them the metaphor.
Source: Firefighter Nation

2.1Mof 6.4 million veterans in VA care have PTSD, depression, or treatment-resistant depression

Ten VA Sites, REMS and Ibogaine's Uphill Battle: Feds Weigh In on Psychedelic Access

Federal officials talking about readiness, not evidence. The VA is planning ten initial sites and says it will deploy as soon as treatments are commercially available, and it already admits demand will outrun supply. The need number is large and the capacity number is small. Ask who delivers the preparation and integration hours, because that is where the access gap will land.

At the Psychedelic Healthcare Forum, officials from the FDA, VA, and ARPA-H spoke on a panel moderated by POLITICO's Erin Schumaker. The VA has chosen ten initial sites, though Andrew Gradison declined to name them. Gradison said about 2.1 million of the 6.4 million veterans who regularly use VA care have PTSD, major depressive disorder, or treatment-resistant depression. He acknowledged treating only four digits' worth of patients in year one would be a challenge. A headline in the piece notes ARPA-H backs ibogaine while cautioning against hype.

Try this: Watch for how the VA staffs preparation and integration. That staffing decision shapes where non-clinical integration support could fit.
Source: Psychedelic Alpha

No. 08 · Contemplative · 2026-11-05

Kundalini phenomenology as expansion of perceptual capacity: a hypothesis and theory article integrating contemplative traditions and neuroscience

A hypothesis paper with no new data, and the author says so. It is interesting for one reason: its claim that outcomes turn on whether perceptual expansion outruns a person's integration capacity. That echoes the integration-as-path frame, though from a Hindu tradition, not the Philokalia, and it should not be flattened into hesychasm. Its diagnostic heuristics for telling contemplative states from psychosis are explicitly unvalidated. That is clinical territory and a scope edge for you.

Swapan Samanta proposes that kundalini experiences can be read, without metaphysical commitment, as a state-dependent expansion of perceptual capacity. The paper draws on global workspace theory, predictive processing, and network integration models, and argues that convergent accounts across seven contemplative traditions point to a common structure. It offers five falsifiable predictions and hypothesizes that safety versus crisis depends on how fast expansion outpaces integration capacity. The author states there is no direct study linking neural integration measures to kundalini, and the clinical heuristics need prospective validation.

Try this: Hold the integration-capacity idea loosely as a question to put to clients in distress after intense practice, and refer anything that looks like psychosis.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology
