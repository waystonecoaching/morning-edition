### 01. Mental health outcomes and intervention strategies for first responders exposed to disasters and occupational trauma: a scoping review

**Source:** Frontiers in Psychology  \
**Category:** first_responder_mh  \
**Published:** 2026-11-12  \
**Link:** [https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1998415](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1998415)

**Why this matters to you:** This is the map of where your work sits. The authors lay interventions on one line from preparedness through early support to therapy, and a coach working with responders lives in the first two thirds of that line. Useful language for drawing scope-of-practice boundaries out loud, with clients and with chiefs. Thin evidence base, so read it as a frame and hold the claims loosely.

**The story:** A scoping review of 14 studies published 2017 to 2024 on first responders and public safety personnel exposed to disaster deployments or cumulative occupational trauma. Nine of the fourteen are cross-sectional; there is one RCT. Exposure intensity, social isolation, maladaptive coping, lack of preparatory training and lower education tracked with worse outcomes, while social support, optimism, perceived preparedness and organisational factors looked protective. The authors sort interventions into preparedness and resilience building, early supportive approaches including peer and digital formats, and therapy, which carries the strongest evidence for established symptoms. They name the gaps plainly: few controlled trials, poor intervention reporting, outcomes measured every which way.

**Actionable:** Sketch your own one-page continuum: what Waystone does at preparedness, what it does at early support, and the hand-off point where a client goes to a clinician. Use it on your next intake.

### 04. Psychometric properties of the Chinese version of the Posttraumatic Cognitions Inventory (PTCI) in male first responders

**Source:** Journal of Traumatic Stress  \
**Category:** first_responder_mh  \
**Published:** 2026-10-06  \
**Link:** [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jts.70079?af=R](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jts.70079?af=R)

**Why this matters to you:** A measurement paper, dry on its face. Underneath is the story layer, in Campbell's terms: the beliefs about self, world and blame that trauma leaves behind. The finding that nine items do the work of thirty-three matters because those nine are short enough to hear in an ordinary conversation. Large sample, all men, all Chinese responders, so carry it across with care.

**The story:** Researchers tested the Chinese 33-item PTCI and its nine-item short form in 1,448 male first responders, alongside the LEC-5 and PCL-5. The original three-factor and an alternative four-factor structure fit poorly. The PTCI-9 fit well, held up across those with and without interpersonal trauma exposure, and correlated with PTSD symptoms as strongly as the full version. Suggested cutoffs are 53 for the full PTCI and 16 for the PTCI-9.

**Actionable:** Pull the English PTCI-9 items and read them as listening cues, the sort of sentences a client says out loud when the story has hardened. Leave the scoring to clinicians.


### 05. From text to touch: choosing how artificial intelligence communicates empathy in healthcare

**Source:** Frontiers in Psychology  \
**Category:** communication  \
**Published:** 2026-11-12  \
**Link:** [https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.2010347](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.2010347)

**Why this matters to you:** Read this as a mirror. The authors line up text, voice, visual cues and touch by how embodied each is, and concede that text alone strips out most of what carries empathy. That is the polyvagal argument in engineering language: safety travels by face, voice and presence. It is a sharp case for why your clients need a person in the room, and a fair warning about what an app can and cannot hold.

**The story:** A perspective piece arguing that most AI-empathy research fixes on text, which drops the nonverbal channels people rely on. The authors place modalities on a spectrum from text, through voice and visual expression, to touch, running roughly from most scalable to most embodied. They say more embodiment does not automatically feel more empathic, that patient preferences vary, and that touch remains the hardest channel to build and study. They note critics who call text-based AI responses empty empathy talk, and leave open whether machines can have empathy or only imitate it.
