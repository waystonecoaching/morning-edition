U.S. Government Taps Mark Cuban's Company to Develop Ibogaine for PTSD; Commits to Fund $20M Real-World Evidence Network

This is the federal government putting its hand on the most dangerous drug in the psychedelic lineup, and doing it for veterans first. The money is groundwork, not treatment, so nobody in your world gets ibogaine from this for years. Read it with the cardiac risk in plain view: ibogaine stays Schedule I because it can stop a heart. When guys at the station start asking about Mexico clinics, this is the thread they will be pulling.

At an HHS press conference on November 12, RFK Jr. announced two psychedelic initiatives tied to the April executive order. ARPA-H commits up to $4.9 million over five years, with the VA, to build the manufacturing, dosing and study-design groundwork for ibogaine in veterans with PTSD. Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company is backed to develop the regulatory and manufacturing process, and ARPA-H bought treatment data from Beond, a clinic operator that has run roughly 3,000 patients through ibogaine in Mexico. Separately, NIMH made concrete a $20 million call for a real-world psychedelics evidence network. No patient trial is funded yet, and the announcement said nothing about safety.

Try this: Have a plain answer ready for the responder who asks about an ibogaine retreat: what it is, the cardiac risk, and that no U.S. trial is enrolling. Know where you send him medically before he books a flight.

Betrayal Explains More of Veterans' Alcohol Problems Than PTSD Does, VA-Backed Study Finds

Structural-critique camp, and it puts numbers behind what you hear at the kitchen table: the drinking follows the betrayal, not just the call. Substance use sits on your scope edge, so this is a frame for coaching the meaning underneath, with clinical care alongside, not a reason to treat the drinking yourself. Cross-sectional data on combat veterans, not firefighters, so carry it over with care.

Researchers from the VA, UCSF and Yale analyzed 1,321 combat-exposed veterans from a nationally representative VA National Center for PTSD sample, published in Psychological Medicine. About one in four reported betrayal by leaders, peers or institutions. Betrayal was the strongest predictor of past-year alcohol use disorder among the moral injury exposures, and the authors write it explained more variance than depression or PTSD. Witnessing others' transgressions tracked with drug use disorder instead. The associations held after adjusting for combat severity, lifetime trauma, depression and PTSD, though the single time point means the order of cause cannot be shown.

Try this: When a client's drinking comes up, ask who let him down before you ask what he saw.

No. 03 · First Responder MH · 2026-11-16

First Responders Are at Risk of Concussions. Ohio Researchers Say Policy Shifts Could Help

Brain injury is the piece most responder wellness talk skips, and it muddies everything downstream: sleep, temper, mood, drinking. A client who took a head hit on a fall or a fight and never got checked can look like a PTSD story that won't resolve. Peer support and supervisors are named in the fix, which is your lane. Diagnosis is not.

Ohio State researchers led by Jaclyn Caccese convened more than 50 police, fire and EMS professionals at the first international conference on TBI in first responders and reached consensus recommendations. A statewide Ohio survey found about three in four work-related concussions among law enforcement and corrections officers go untreated, a worse rate than collegiate athletes. Recommendations include brief automatic screening after crashes, assaults and falls, temporary removal from emergency driving after a TBI, attention to the mental health effects of injury, and trained supervisors and peer teams who can spot it. NIOSH gave the team $1.9 million to adapt athlete concussion testing for police. Stigma, thin medical access and overnight shifts that wreck sleep are named as the barriers.

Try this: Add one intake question: any head injury on or off the job, and did anyone check it?

Theoretical Model Frames Moral Injury as Rupture of Sustaining Emotional Attachments

Bill Nash wrote the Marine Corps combat stress doctrine, and here he gives Shay's old claim a mechanism: moral injury as a torn attachment, not a fear response. That sits close to your own frame. Felt safety and relationship come first, and the wound is relational before it is cognitive. Pure theory, untested, so use it as language rather than evidence.

William Nash's moral engine model, published in Frontiers in Psychiatry and covered by Bioengineer, defines moral injury as a wound to the self when a betrayal of moral trust ruptures defining and sustaining attachments. Attachments run to people, ideals, community, one's own body and internalized figures, and sudden rupture can fragment the self and produce dissociation. Drawing on Kohut's self psychology, the model criticizes the DSM's fear-centered view of PTSD for sidelining shame and anger. Its treatment implications are tentative: offer a steady valuing relationship, clear away obstacles like dissociation and substance use, and repair or replace what was broken, including amends where possible.

Cognitive-Behavioral Conjoint Therapy for PTSD: A Systematic Review of Outcomes, Mechanisms, and Clinical Applications

Communication and connection work with a trial base behind it. CBCT treats PTSD through the couple, with the partner in the room, which is where a lot of your responders' trouble shows up first. It is a clinical protocol, so the point for you is the frame, not the manual: the marriage as a place of recovery rather than collateral damage. The abstract would not load for this edition, so check the numbers in the piece before you quote them.

A systematic review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress gathers the evidence on cognitive-behavioral conjoint therapy, the couple-based PTSD treatment built by Monson and Fredman. The review covers outcomes for both the person with PTSD and the partner, the mechanisms proposed for change, and where the protocol has been applied clinically. CBCT works on communication, conflict and the avoidance patterns that trauma plants in a household, alongside the trauma itself. Study counts and effect sizes were not retrievable for this edition.

Retired NYPD Officers Turn to Peer Support as Trauma, Suicide Concerns Persist

Retirement is the cliff nobody trains for, and this is your career-transition work in numbers. The department stops counting when the badge comes off, so the peer group is the only one keeping the tally. Suicidality and alcohol both run through the piece, which puts it on your scope edge: peer and coach hold the line, clinical care stays in the loop.

POPPA, the peer-run nonprofit independent of the NYPD, started its retiree program in 2006 with 26 volunteers and now has more than 100 across New York and other states, available around the clock. Retiree calls rose from 100 in 2024 to 209 in 2025, with 183 so far this year. Retired officers POPPA knows of who died by suicide went from 3 in 2024 to 4 in 2025 to 6 in 2026, and the NYPD does not track retiree suicides at all. Retirees describe lingering 9/11 trauma and illness fears, fixed pensions losing ground, family strain and drinking to cope. Director Tom Hovagim reads the rising calls as stigma easing, but says it is not gone.

Try this: Ask Phoenix Fire whether anyone tracks retiree deaths. If nobody does, that is worth naming to someone with the authority to fix it.

Corruption in Healthcare Linked to Stress, Anxiety and Moral Injury, Review Finds

A mixed-methods study in PLOS Global Public Health pairs a scoping review of 25 sources with four key informant interviews on the mental health cost of healthcare corruption. Workers caught in informal payments, patronage or systemic corruption reported stress, insomnia, low mood, eroded professional identity and burnout. Moral injury showed up across several studies, especially among nurses whose institutions blocked ethical action. Patients denied care or squeezed for payments reported anxiety, hopelessness and depressive symptoms, with women, rural and poor patients hit hardest. The evidence is thin and mostly cross-sectional, and the authors call for treating moral distress as an occupational hazard.

The authors call for treating moral distress and moral injury as occupational risks, not private failings.

Structural-critique camp again, from the hospital side. When the institution asks you to do wrong to get the job done, the injury falls on the worker, not just the patient. Most of the data comes from low- and middle-income health systems, so the bridge to an American fire department is the pattern, not the particulars.

Source: Bioengineer.org (on PLOS Global Public Health)
