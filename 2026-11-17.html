Compass Files Psilocybin Application with FDA, U.S. Government Taps Mark Cuban to Develop Ibogaine, Psychedelics After the Midterms

This is the first classic psychedelic to reach a full FDA application. The indication is treatment-resistant depression, not PTSD, so nothing changes for a firefighter's care this year. What it does is build the delivery machine: certified sites, trained monitors, a billing code. Every later PTSD indication, MDMA included, will ride that machine, and the integration work you do sits right at its edge. Read the numbers when they post, because the field has been burned before by trials that looked better in press than in the data.

Compass Pathways told investors on November 9 that it had completed its New Drug Application for COMP360, its synthetic psilocybin, for treatment-resistant depression. The filing rests on two Phase 3 trials, COMP005 and COMP006, with more than 1,000 participants combined. The FDA has given the program a National Priority Voucher with a one to two month review target, and Compass is aiming for launch in the first half of 2027. Psychedelic Alpha's podcast covers the filing alongside the federal ibogaine money and what the midterms mean for psychedelic policy. ICER has also announced a cost review of the drug, which will shape whether insurers pay for it.

Try this: When the FDA advisory materials post, pull the COMP006 durability data and the therapist-support requirements. That is the clearest picture yet of what supervised psychedelic care will look like in practice.

Therapists' hints of upcoming affect silence in psychotherapy and their responses to it: an applied conversation analysis study

Every coach has felt it. A man says the hard thing, his voice speeds up, then nothing. Three seconds feels like a minute, and the urge is to fill it. This study looks at that exact moment on tape. It is small, so it gives you no rules. What it gives you is a map of what came right before the silence, which is where your attention belongs if you want to stay with him instead of rescuing him.

Researchers recorded 20 hours of online individual psychotherapy across seven therapist and client pairs and found 23 moments of what they call affect silence: a pause of three seconds or longer after a cluster of emotional expressions. They sorted the silences into anger, distress and uncertainty, with uncertainty the most common. Clients signaled the silence ahead of time with sped-up talk, pauses and sentences left unfinished. Therapists mostly answered with affirmation, sometimes with reformulation or a minimal continuer, and with direct questions when the silence was uncertainty. The authors say plainly that the sample is small and the findings are groundwork, not guidance.

Try this: In your next three sessions, notice the cue before a silence: rushed words, a dropped sentence. Name it to yourself and wait one breath longer than is comfortable before you speak.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Effects of a cognitive-behavioral therapy-based program on substance use, well-being, and psychological symptoms

Substance use is part of your coaching lane, so the evidence on structured group work is worth knowing, and this study is weak evidence. These are university students, not responders. Groups were sorted by schedule, not by chance. Ninety people in all. It earns a place only as a reference point for what a short seven-week group can and cannot claim. This sits on a scope edge: a coach running anything that looks like a substance use treatment group is in clinical water.

Ninety university students were placed in a seven-session CBT-based group, an inactive control or an active placebo group, assigned by which sessions they could attend rather than at random. Each CBT session ran about 90 minutes, once a week. The researchers measured tobacco and nicotine use, heavy drinking and other substance use frequency, along with well-being and psychological symptoms, at baseline, at the end and twelve weeks later. The design means any gains cannot be cleanly credited to the program.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology

Why I became an EMT at (almost) age 50

Most of your men are walking the other direction, out of the job and into something else. This one walked in at fifty. Hold the two side by side. Whatever pulled him in is the same thing your clients are afraid they will lose when they hang up the gear, and it was never the uniform.

Mike Tanghe sold property and casualty insurance for more than 25 years. During the pandemic he heard his son in an online gym class, enrolled in an EMT course at the local community college, and took a part-time interfacility transport job while keeping his day work. People asked why a man his age would take an entry-level ambulance job. His answer came on a transfer with a non-verbal patient who had cerebral palsy and schizophrenia and grew agitated in the back. Tanghe held his hand, and the man stayed calm for the rest of the twenty-minute ride.
