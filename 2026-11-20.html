A Living Meta-Analysis of MDMA-Assisted Therapy for PTSD, With the Data Left Open

The field has been running on press releases and one sponsor's trials. A living review with open data is the other way to do it: pool every trial, publish the spreadsheet, update as new work comes in, and let anyone check the arithmetic. That matters more than whatever today's pooled effect size says, because the credibility problem in MDMA was never just the numbers. It was who held them. I could not get past the paywall to read the effect estimates myself, so take this as the method story until you read the paper.

Published October 11 in Neuropsychopharmacology, the paper sets up a living systematic review and meta-analysis of MDMA-assisted therapy trials for PTSD, paired with an open data resource. A living review is built to be updated as new trials report, rather than frozen at one date. The open data piece means the trial-level inputs are available for others to reanalyze. Earlier reviews in this space have split on how much functional unblinding and therapist allegiance inflate the effects; this format makes those questions checkable instead of arguable.

Try this: Bookmark the open data resource. Next time a client or chief cites an MDMA number, check it against the pooled set before you repeat it.
Source: Neuropsychopharmacology (Nature)

Betrayal Explains More of Veterans' Alcohol Problems Than PTSD Does, VA-Backed Study Finds

This sits in the structural camp of moral injury work, the Shay line: the wound comes from those above you, not from what you did. If betrayal carries more of the drinking than PTSD symptoms do, then the standard move of treating the trauma and hoping the bottle follows is aimed at the wrong target. Substance use is a scope edge. You can name the betrayal and hold it; the drinking itself may need a clinician beside you. It also rhymes with Sowden, Hart and Bonanno's prospective Army work this year, where betrayal, not transgression, predicted who struggled after separation.

Military.com reports a VA-supported study finding that perceived betrayal by leaders or institutions accounts for more of the variance in veterans' alcohol problems than PTSD symptoms do. I read the headline and the surrounding literature, not the full article, so treat sample and effect size as unconfirmed. The finding fits a growing body of work separating betrayal from self-directed moral injury and showing the two behave differently. A May 2026 ISTSS summary of a prospective Army study found only betrayal predicted worse posttraumatic stress and reintegration after leaving service, with modest effects.

Try this: On intake with a drinking responder, ask one plain question about who let him down, before you ask about the worst call.
Source: Military.com

PTSD Among Paramedics: A Systematic Review of Prevalence, Risk, and What Protects

Your old trade, pooled. The prevalence number will get quoted in decks; the protective factors are what you can use. Reviews like this tend to find that organizational stressors, sleep, and support at work carry as much weight as the calls themselves, which puts a lot of the leverage outside the clinic and inside the crew. Read the protective list against what a coach can touch and what only a department can change.

A systematic review in the October issue of the Journal of Traumatic Stress (Vol. 39, Issue 5, pp. 745-760) gathers studies on PTSD prevalence among paramedics, along with risk and protective factors. Wiley's page would not load for me, so I have the scope from the citation, not the pooled figures. Prior meta-analyses have put paramedic PTSD well above the general working population. The value here is a single current source for both the risk side and the protective side.

Try this: Pull the protective factors table and sort it into two columns: things a peer or coach can strengthen, and things only command can fix.
Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

UCLA Tests MDMA Combined With Trauma-Focused Therapy for Young Adults With PTSD

Two shifts worth noticing. The age band moves younger, and the therapy piece is named trauma-focused therapy rather than the bespoke MAPS protocol, which is where critics said the field should go after the 2024 FDA rejection. That is a study announcement, not a result. Young adults with PTSD are a high-acuity group; anything that touches them is clinical work, and the coach's place is after, not during.

UCLA Health announced a study testing MDMA paired with an established trauma-focused psychotherapy for young adults with PTSD. The release describes a trial underway, not findings. Pairing MDMA with an evidence-based therapy, instead of a protocol built around the drug, is a design choice aimed at the main criticism of earlier trials. Details on sample, comparator and blinding were not in what I could see.

Source: UCLA Health

What Helps After a Mass Casualty Event: A Network Meta-Analysis for Survivors and Responders

A network meta-analysis lets you rank interventions that were never tested head to head. For mass casualty work that matters, because the field still argues about what to do in the first weeks, and debriefing habits die hard. Responders are pooled here with survivors, so watch whether the paper splits them out. The answer for a medic three weeks after a shooting may not match the answer for a bystander.

Published early view in October in the Journal of Traumatic Stress, the paper is a systematic review and network meta-analysis of psychosocial interventions for adults exposed to mass casualty events, covering both survivors and responders. The network design compares interventions indirectly across trials to estimate which perform best. I could not open the Wiley page, so the ranking itself is unread. Read it for whether early, structured interventions beat watchful waiting, and whether responders were analyzed separately.

Source: Journal of Traumatic Stress

Retired NYPD Officers Turn to Peer Support as Suicides Among Retirees Climb

Retirement is the cliff nobody trains for. The job ends, the crew scatters, and the department stops counting. The NYPD does not track retiree suicides, so the only number is the one a peer group gathers by word of mouth. That is your career transition lane in plain sight. Suicidality edge: this is a peer program working beside clinicians, and the piece should be read that way.

amNewYork profiles POPPA, the Police Organization Providing Peer Assistance, and its retiree program. It began in 2006 with 26 retired volunteers and now has more than 100. Calls to the retiree line rose from 100 in 2024 to 209 in 2025, with 183 logged in 2026 with about two months left. POPPA knows of six retired officers who died by suicide this year, against four in 2025 and three in 2024, and its director says the true count could be higher because no one tracks it.

Try this: Ask Phoenix Fire wellness whether anyone tracks retiree deaths. If not, that silence is the finding.
Source: amNewYork

Cognitive-Behavioral Conjoint Therapy for PTSD: Outcomes, Mechanisms, and Clinical Use

A systematic review in the Journal of Traumatic Stress, early view November 15, gathers the evidence on cognitive-behavioral conjoint therapy for PTSD, a couple-based treatment that brings the partner into the work. It covers outcomes for both the person with PTSD and the relationship, proposed mechanisms of change, and clinical applications. I have the scope from the listing, not the pooled results. A related JTS paper this month looked at veterans with suicidal ideation in a couple-based treatment.

The marriage is often where the trauma lives after shift.

The marriage is often where a responder's trauma lives after shift. CBCT treats PTSD inside the couple, not around it, and the mechanism question is the one to watch: does it work by cutting avoidance, or by restoring the plain talk that Getting Real trains? This is clinical work. Your lane is the connection skills on either side of it, and knowing when to refer a couple in.

The Hidden Mental Health Burden of Corruption in Healthcare

Structural lineage again. When the system itself is crooked, the worker who stays honest pays in stress, anxiety and moral injury. Swap corruption for the quieter institutional failures responders know, budget games, staffing lies, the protocol that protects the agency and not the patient, and the mechanism is the same. The coverage comes through an aggregator; the source is a PLOS Global Public Health scoping review.

Bioengineer.org covers a scoping review with key informant interviews, published in PLOS Global Public Health, on corruption in healthcare and its mental health effects on workers. The review links working inside corrupt systems to stress, anxiety and moral injury among health staff. It is a scoping review, so it maps the literature rather than pooling effects. Most of the source studies come from settings where corruption is overt, which limits how far it carries to US fire service, but the moral injury path it describes travels.

Source: Bioengineer.org / PLOS Global Public Health

Archbishop Broglio Warns U.S. Soldiers Are Increasingly at Risk of Moral Injury

A shepherd naming what the clinicians name in other words. Broglio has spent this year saying troops may refuse morally questionable orders, and here he puts the cost of obeying them in the language of the soul. The fathers would call it a wound of conscience, and they would not hand it to a protocol. Read it as a pastor talking, not a study, and sit with it.

In an America Magazine interview published November 2, Archbishop Timothy Broglio of the Archdiocese for the Military Services warns that American service members face rising risk of moral injury. The interview sits against his public statements this year on the Iran war and on troops' freedom to decline morally questionable orders. He speaks as a pastor responsible for Catholic chaplains and the troops they serve. The interview frames moral injury as a spiritual and pastoral matter, not only a clinical one.

Source: America Magazine
