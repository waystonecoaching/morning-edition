220 Patients or 20,000? Clinics Split on Psychedelics' First Year

The people building the clinics cannot agree on whether year one means two hundred patients or twenty thousand. That spread is the honest state of the field. The one point they all agreed on matters most to an integration practitioner: the two-facilitator trial model will not survive real practice, which means the support around the dosing day gets thinner, and someone has to carry what the clinic drops. That is scope-of-practice ground. Integration coaching is not therapy and not the clinical team, and the gap they describe is exactly where that line gets tested.

A recap of the Clinics and Care session at the Psychedelic Healthcare Forum in New York on October 15, moderated by Joy Sun Cooper. Asked how many patients would be treated in the first 12 months after a first FDA approval, Journey Clinical's Myriam Barthes said 20,000, Osmind's William Sauve about 5,000, Sunstone's Kimberly Roddy 'in the thousands', and Community Health Center's Daniel Bryant 220. Estimates for time until a patient can book ranged from two to three months to about three years. Bryant noted federally qualified health center visit rates are fixed by statute at about $200 per half hour. Every panelist said the two-facilitator model from the trials would not hold up in the real world.

Try this: Write down, in one paragraph, what you would and would not do for a client coming off a clinic dosing day with one facilitator and no follow-up. Have the line drawn before someone asks you to stand on it.

Similarly Situated? Anonymous Citizen Petition Takes Aim at Usona's Psilocybin Application

Somebody paid a D.C. law firm to go after a competitor's psilocybin application and kept their name off it. Read that plain: the fight over psychedelic approvals is now commercial as much as scientific. The critiques named, durability and blinding, are real weaknesses in this literature. Who is making them, and why, is the other half of the story.

On November 17 the firm Kleinfeld, Kaplan and Becker filed a Citizen Petition with FDA for an unnamed client. It asks FDA to apply its safety and effectiveness standards consistently across all pending psychedelic drug applications, three in total, but spends most of its weight on Usona Institute's psilocybin application. FDA posted it to a public docket on November 19. Psychedelic Alpha reads the 'similarly situated' language as a pointed comparison with Compass Pathways' program. The full argument sits behind a paywall; the page summary says it attacks Usona's data on durability, blinding and safety.

From Approvals to Appointments: Who Pays for Psychedelic Therapy, and How?

Approval is not access. Between the FDA letter and a responder in the chair sit DEA rescheduling, payors, Medicaid, employers and a dosing day nobody has priced yet. For first responder clients the employer and union route is the one to watch. That is where a fire department's benefits plan either opens a door or keeps it shut.

A recap of the launch and reimbursement session at the same October 15 forum, moderated by Jazz Glastra of BrainFutures, who called the path from approval to access a collective action problem. Compass Pathways' chief commercial officer Lori Englebert said the company expects two to four months from approval to first dose, with DEA rescheduling the main unknown; rescheduling conventionally takes about ninety days. Compass has since completed its psilocybin application for treatment-resistant depression. The later sections, on Medicaid, the employer route, commercial payors and patient support, are paywalled.
