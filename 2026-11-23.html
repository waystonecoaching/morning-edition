Betrayal Explains More of Veterans' Alcohol Problems Than PTSD Does

This is the structural lineage of moral injury, Shay's ground: the wound is what was done to you by the people above you. If betrayal drives the drinking harder than PTSD does, then a PTSD-only frame misses the man in front of you. The sample is veterans, not firefighters, but the shift-culture parallel is plain enough. Substance use is a scope edge. Name it, refer where it is clinical, and still ask the betrayal question out loud.

A Psychological Medicine paper from VA, UCSF, and Yale researchers drew on 1,321 combat-exposed veterans in the National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study. About a quarter (24.5%) reported betrayal, 23.1% witnessing others' transgressions, and 11.2% their own. Betrayal was the strongest of the three predictors of past-year alcohol use disorder, carrying 38.7% of the explained variation, and held after adjusting for PTSD, depression, combat severity, and lifetime trauma. Witnessing tracked with drug use disorder. It is a single cross-sectional snapshot with modest odds ratios, so it cannot say which came first.

Try this: Add one plain betrayal question to intake: who above you broke faith, and what did you do with it?

Ketamine trial targets PTSD in emergency workers

An Australian-first trial aimed squarely at emergency workers whose PTSD did not lift with standard treatment. The design pairs ketamine with exposure therapy, so the drug is an adjunct to the work rather than the cure. That is the right shape for the question. Ketamine is clinical ground, not coaching ground. The relevance to Mark is the referral map and the integration conversation afterward.

The Black Dog Institute announced the trial at a Canberra roundtable on October 27. It will test whether ketamine given alongside exposure therapy improves recovery in emergency service workers with persistent PTSD and depression who have not responded to existing treatment. It runs through the institute's National Emergency Worker Support Service with the UNSW Traumatic Stress Clinic, and recruitment is expected to start in 2027. No sample size, arms, or primary outcome have been published yet.

Source: Medical Republic

U.S. Government Taps Mark Cuban's Company to Develop Ibogaine for PTSD; Commits $20M to Real-World Evidence Network

Federal money is moving toward ibogaine, but read the fine print before the headlines. The ARPA-H dollars fund groundwork, manufacturing, and clinic records, not patient trials. Ibogaine carries real cardiac risk, and the clinical data being bought are observational records from a Mexican clinic operator. Veterans will hear about this first. Expect first responders to ask.

At a November 12 HHS press conference, the government said ARPA-H will back Mark Cuban's Cost Plus Drug Company to develop ibogaine for PTSD in collaboration with the VA, committing up to $4.9 million over five years. Beond, a clinic operator that has treated more than 3,000 patients in Mexico, supplies clinical data. HHS also announced a $20 million NIMH funding opportunity for a real-world psychedelics evidence network, with awards expected summer 2027. HHS acknowledged possible life-threatening heart rhythm problems and called for screening, cardiac monitoring, and independent safety oversight. No trials are authorized by this money.

Source: Psychedelic Alpha

Interview: Archbishop Broglio warns U.S. soldiers are increasingly at risk of ‘moral injury’

A Catholic archbishop who shepherds soldiers says plainly that some orders can wound the soul of the man who carries them out. The Fathers knew this. Abba Moses would not judge his brother, but he carried the sand. The question for responders is the same as for soldiers: what happens to a conscience the institution has no room for?

Kevin Clarke interviewed Archbishop Timothy Broglio of the Archdiocese for the Military Services on October 16. The setting is a proposal to livestream the military execution of Nidal Hasan, which the White House later dropped; the execution is still set for December 3 at Fort Hood. Broglio says taking part in an execution risks moral injury, raises the same worry for drone operators and remote combat, and says Catholic service members should not take part. He notes the United States does not recognize selective conscientious objection.

Source: America Magazine
