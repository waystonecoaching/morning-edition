Five Labs, One Protocol: Psilocybin's Lasting Effects Did Not Replicate in Mice

This is the field checking its own math, and the math came back thin. The animal work under the psilocybin story was supposed to show lasting change. Run the same way in five labs, it mostly did not. That does not settle anything for humans, but it is one more reason to read psychedelic headlines with your hand on your wallet. The field is under credibility stress and this is part of why.

Five labs at UC Berkeley, UC San Francisco and Stanford ran identical psilocybin protocols in mice, published in Nature Neuroscience and covered here by ZME Science. Pooled, the most reproducible effects were a spike in anxiety-like behavior right after dosing and reduced fear expression in the acute window. Lasting reductions in depression- or anxiety-like behavior did not replicate. The researchers put the inconsistency down to a small underlying effect, and ruled out handler sex, mouse supplier and housing time. Co-first author Odilia Lu notes the limits of translating mouse behavior models to human depression.

Try this: When a client quotes a psilocybin result, ask one question first: was it people or mice, and has anyone run it twice?
Source: ZME Science

Veterans Groups Press Congress as VA Says It Is Preparing for Approved Psychedelics

The VA usually sets the track first responder care runs on a few years later. If the VA builds a psychedelic delivery system, fire service EAPs and workers' comp will be asked why they have not. Read the testimony for what it is: advocates citing small studies at a hearing. A 30-person study with a near 90 percent claim is a reason to look, not a standard of care.

At a House VA Health Subcommittee hearing on November 18, the VFW, the Special Forces Foundation and Disabled American Veterans urged lawmakers to speed psychedelic research and clear access barriers. VA officials said the agency is preparing for FDA-approved psychedelic therapies, several of which may move toward approval in 2027 following the April 2026 executive order. Rep. Julia Brownley pressed the VA to have a trained workforce and safety protocols in place first. One veteran described ibogaine treatment abroad and said his symptoms came back after 18 months. DAV's Naomi Mathis cited a 2024 Stanford study of 30 veterans with TBI; the paper reports the claim as testimony and does not verify it.

Try this: Note the veteran whose symptoms returned at 18 months. That is the integration question in one line: what holds the change after the medicine wears off.

'Under Fire': An Idaho Fire Captain, Ketamine, and Who Takes Care of the Caretakers

A fire captain going through ketamine-assisted therapy on camera, and the film is out where crews will see it. That is first responder psychedelic care leaving the journals and entering the station kitchen. The director's own father died by suicide, so this sits on the suicidality edge and on the clinical edge both. It is a documentary, not data. Treat it as the conversation starter it says it is.

TheWrap interviews filmmaker Brandon Kapelow about 'Under Fire,' an 88-minute documentary that premiered to a sold-out crowd at Doc NYC. It follows Idaho fire Capt. Rob Christensen through experimental ketamine-assisted therapy for PTSD. The film grew out of Kapelow's 2024 New York Times op-doc 'An Act of Service,' and Kapelow says his father died by suicide when he was 12. Riley Keough is among the executive producers. Virtual screening tickets run through November 29, and Kapelow says the film is meant to open conversation rather than push one answer.

Try this: Catch a virtual screening before November 29. Watch for what the captain's crew and family do around the treatment, not just the session itself.

Burnout Is an Operational Issue, Not a Personal Wellness Problem

This is the structural lineage talking, in the field's own trade press. Medics are saying the yoga app is not the fix; staffing is. That is the tension between the wellness frame and the leadership-failure frame, and it lands right on a solo coach's pitch to a department. If you sell resilience to a crew that is short three bodies a shift, you are selling the wrong thing. Know which camp you are standing in when you walk into a station.

Drawing on respondent comments from EMS1's 'What Paramedics Want in 2026' survey, Rachel Engel argues burnout is driven by staffing shortages, workload, poor scheduling, low pay, weak leadership communication and too little recovery time. Respondents asked for more staff, predictable schedules (some named 24/72), better pay and benefits, and leadership they can trust. They still want counseling and peer support that actually works, but not as a stand-in for fixing operations. The piece gives no respondent count or sampling method, so read it as field voice, not measurement.

Try this: Before your next department conversation, write one sentence that says what coaching can and cannot fix when the problem is staffing.
Source: EMS1

Bessel van der Kolk on Complex PTSD, Attachment, and the Limits of Pharmacotherapy

Van der Kolk puts attachment under trauma and says the drugs fall short for complex PTSD. That is close to how you already hold it: the nervous system shaped in relationship, healed in relationship. His aside on the MDMA phase 3 trial matters for the critical read. If most participants carried childhood abuse and neglect, the trial may say more about developmental trauma than about a medic's single bad call. It is an interview, so these are his views, not findings.

In a Psychiatric Times 'Brain Trust' interview with Joseph F. Goldberg, MD, van der Kolk argues much of what gets labeled trauma is an attachment disorder, and that medication falls short for complex PTSD. He notes there are no randomized drug trials for complex PTSD and only two FDA-approved PTSD medications. He cites his group's study of 20,000 children supporting a developmental trauma disorder diagnosis that DSM-5 did not adopt, and EMDR remission in most adults with adult-onset trauma. He also points out that 88 percent of participants in the phase 3 MDMA trial had histories of chronic abuse and neglect.

Try this: With your next client, ask about the first room before the worst call: who was there for you when you were small, and who is there now?
Source: Psychiatric Times

Eleven Walk Off a South Dakota County Ambulance Service

This is what moral injury looks like when it stops being a word: people leaving the truck because the people above them would not fix the station. No bathroom, no climate control, a commission that could not agree. It is rural EMS, not Phoenix, but the betrayal shape is the same one you hear on the floor. The count comes from one former employee and the county says service continues, so hold the number loosely.

EMS1 and Dakota News Now report that employees of the Day County Ambulance Service resigned effective immediately on November 17 after the county commission failed to reach agreement. Former employee Joshua Edwards put the number at 11, with five more expected. Former staff cited facilities without climate control or bathrooms, and a since-deleted service post blamed communication breakdowns among commissioners, the ambulance director and crew. The county says the service remains in full operation. Separately, Rapid City Fire says its rural ambulance service ends December 31 without new funding, and a state EMS task force is under way.

Source: EMS1

The EMS Pulse: Medics on De-escalating Difficult Family Members

EMS1 collected reader answers to one question: your best de-escalation tip for difficult family members on a patient call. Common threads were listening and empathy, firm and simple scripts, clearing the room or leaving when the scene turns unsafe, and redirecting attention. Editor Rachel Engel notes the responses converged on acknowledging fear, giving plain explanations and protecting scene safety. The number of responses is not given; this is field advice, not research.

Acknowledge their feelings and reassure. If it's unsafe, leave.

This is your day job in plain language from the people doing it. Acknowledge the fear, say it plainly, and leave if it is unsafe. That is Getting Real at the curb: name what is happening, make contact, keep the body safe. The thread never settled where de-escalation ends and disengagement begins. That line is worth drawing for yourself before the next call.

Try this: Pick one line you will say first to a frightened family member, and practice it out loud until it comes out in a ventral voice under load.

No. 09 · Contemplative · 2026-11-25

Somatic Settling and Non-Coercive Regulation: A Theory for Comparing Contemplative Practice

Two philosophers try to compare contemplative traditions without boiling them down to one technique. Their four dimensions, somatic settling, interoceptive meaning, non-coercive regulation and a relational orientation, map well onto what the Fathers call watchfulness and what Dana calls coming home to the body. It is pure theory with no human data. Read it as a lens, not a result, and notice what it cannot hold: prayer addressed to Someone.

In a Hypothesis and Theory article in Frontiers in Psychology, Ziang Wang and Linghong Kong of Zhejiang University propose four process dimensions for comparing contemplative practices: somatic settling, interoceptive-semantic organization, non-coercive affect regulation and normative-relational orientation. They sketch an author-designed meditation prototype informed by the Chinese idea of zhengqi, framed as 'experienced vital integrity,' and lay out testable propositions and a staged research program. The authors are plain that no original human data are reported and that the prototype is not a recovered historical tradition. No efficacy or safety claims are made.

Try this: Sit with the Prayer for twenty minutes and notice which of the four dimensions shows up first. Then notice what none of them name.
Source: Frontiers in Psychology
