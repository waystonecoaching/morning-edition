FDA, VA and ARPA-H on Psychedelic Medicine

Three federal desks on one stage: the regulator, the agency that would deliver care to veterans, and the agency funding the high-risk research. This is panel talk, recorded at an industry event, so treat it as statements of intent, not policy. It matters for one reason: the VA is the most likely first door a former responder with a service history walks through for MDMA or psilocybin, and where the VA lands will shape what the fire service follows. Listen for what they say about therapist training and monitoring, and for what they leave out on durability and adverse events.

Psychedelic Alpha posted a video of a panel bringing together representatives of the FDA, the Department of Veterans Affairs, and ARPA-H on psychedelic medicine. It is one of a batch of recorded sessions the outlet published on November 26. The feed carried no transcript or written summary, so the specifics of what each agency committed to are not confirmed here. Watch it before citing it.

Try this: Watch the VA segment and note any stated timeline or eligibility language. That is what a client with veteran status will ask you about first.

HHS's Matt Zorn on Psychedelic Safety and Speed

The title names the tension the whole field is living in. Federal people are pushing to move fast, and the evidence base has taken real hits: an MDMA application turned down in 2024, a psilocybin durability finding that did not replicate in mice this week, serious adverse events in a psilocin trial. Speed and safety are not on the same side of the ledger right now. Hear the man from HHS out, and keep your own count.

A recorded conversation with Matt Zorn of the Department of Health and Human Services on balancing safety against speed in bringing psychedelic therapies to patients. It was posted by Psychedelic Alpha alongside its other event videos. No transcript came through the feed, so the specific positions he takes are not summarized here.

Foundation Cites Suicide in Death of Brown County 911 Dispatcher

Dispatchers take the call nobody else hears the end of, and they still get counted as clerical in half the country. Brown County's center has been in the regional news for more than a year over a staffing shortage, forced overtime, resignations, and a study commission on morale. A foundation naming suicide as the cause is the part the record usually leaves out. Scope edge: this is suicidality, and it is a real person with a family. Read it as field signal on telecommunicator load, not as a case to work.

Green Bay station WLUK reports that a foundation has cited suicide in the death of a Brown County, Wisconsin, 911 dispatcher. The feed carried only the headline. The county's dispatch center has had documented staffing and working-conditions problems, including forced overtime and a commission that issued improvement recommendations.

Try this: If your peer or coaching work touches dispatch, ask your local center who covers telecommunicators under its peer support and EAP. Often the honest answer is nobody.
Source: WLUK (Fox 11)

Wales Puts £500,000 Toward Protecting Firefighters and Building Resilience

This sits in the operational lineage: government money, framed as protection and resilience, routed through the fire and rescue services. That frame does good work and also has a blind spot. Resilience funding tends to buy training and screening for the individual, and leaves the betrayal side of moral injury, the staffing and leadership decisions, untouched. Worth knowing what the money actually buys before calling it a wellness win.

Strict Buprenorphine Dispenser Monitoring Fails to Improve Opioid Treatment Retention

Tighter surveillance did not keep people in treatment. That lines up with what peer work already knows: control is not the same as safety, and a nervous system under watch does not settle. Source caution: Bioengineer.org rewrites journal papers, so find the original trial before you lean on it. Scope edge: opioid use disorder treatment is clinical territory. A coach's lane is the relationship around it, not the dosing.

A write-up of a trial that found strict monitoring through a buprenorphine dispensing device did not improve retention in opioid use disorder treatment. The feed carried only the headline, so the trial design, sample size, and comparison arm are not confirmed here.

Try this: Pull the source paper before using this with a client or a department. A headline about monitoring will get quoted on both sides of a policy fight.
